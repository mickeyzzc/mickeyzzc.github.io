MiBeeNvr v0.6.0: Timelapse and Transcoding
June 5, 2026 · 12 min read

Once recording is up and running, the disk cries first: one 1080p camera writes tens of GB a day, and 30-day retention eats most of a 1TB drive. The community reported the same thing over and over, and timelapse plus transcoding came up as the favored fixes.

Less disk

  • Timelapse: cameras whose scenes sit still most of the day need roughly 5% of the space for the same duration
  • Frames are skipped automatically while the picture stays static — idle time isn’t wasted
  • The extraction interval is configurable, and multiple cameras stagger themselves instead of spiking together

Recordings can be transcoded

  • H.264 ↔ H.265 in both directions, with historical recordings backfillable
  • Hardware encoding is auto-detected: use it when present (the Banana Pi M5 saturates H.265 hardware encoding), disable cleanly when not — the Raspberry Pi 3B is unaffected
  • Transcoding starts from the web UI, with status and progress visible

Steadier playback, easier diagnosis

  • A frozen picture is detected automatically
  • A class of H.265 playback corruption is fixed
  • Prometheus metrics and remote logging are wired in — problems get diagnosed from data instead of guesses

Keep the disk alive first.

Taking Over onvif-go: Real-Camera Fixes, Zero Dependencies, and Service Facades
August 27, 2026 · 9 min read

MiBeeNvr talks to cameras over ONVIF, and this library is what sits underneath. ONVIF is the de facto standard for IP video surveillance equipment: device discovery, media profiles, PTZ control, event subscriptions — all one set of SOAP-over-HTTP service conventions. Any brand that claims ONVIF compliance can, in theory, be integrated through the same calls.

onvif-go is that stack implemented in Go. We did not write it from scratch: its upstream was 0x524a/onvif-go — the previous author reshaped it into its modern form of a client plus a virtual camera server (the git history still traces back to earlier Go ONVIF attempts in the community). But upstream stalled entering 2026: the last merged PR landed in mid-January, the v1.0-through-v1.1.3 release cadence from late 2025 never resumed, and three Improve Test Coverage issues filed in November 2025 sit unclaimed to this day. Meanwhile we hit two problems in our own camera pool that could only be fixed inside the library.

Four Protocol Libraries, Extracted: ONVIF and GB28181 in Go and Rust
August 29, 2026 · 8 min read

MiBee’s camera access runs on two protocols. ONVIF is the de facto standard internationally — Hikvision, Axis, Dahua, and Bosch all speak it. GB/T 28181 is China’s national surveillance standard, unavoidable in government and state-owned projects. The device side (Raspberry Pi camera firmware, one Go and one Rust implementation) and the NVR side (MiBeeNvr, Go) both speak these two protocols, and the stacks have always lived inside the projects.

Two problems with that. First, this code is pickled in interop details — digest-auth qop handling, SIP Via-branch uniqueness, MANSCDP element-vs-attribute dual forms, TCP framing header byte counts — each one was ground out against a real platform, and a new project would have to grind them all again. Second, a library survey two weeks ago showed the open-source landscape for these protocols is unevenly populated; several positions have no one standing in them.
