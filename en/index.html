Can Vision Models Read Images and Watch Video? A Capability Survey: What's Solved and What's an Industry-Wide Boundary

October 23, 2026 10 min read
Suppose you’re handed a requirement: ten thousand hours of surveillance footage, and it must answer two kinds of questions. Kind one: “Who is this person,” “where did this car go,” “when did he enter the server room.” Kind two: “What was he doing in there,” “at what moment did he start,” “was he turning those screws fast or slow.” Both kinds are called “visual understanding,” but their fates are completely different. Kind one has been beaten to saturation on public datasets — engineering-wise it’s deliverable today. Kind two is the entire industry’s capability boundary in 2026 — the strongest closed models fail the pass line, and not in a “wait six months” way. This post walks through the state of both, where the boundary sits, what the correct architecture looks like, what the money math is, and where the open-source licensing traps are. Third-Party Free P2P Nodes: Public STUN, Free TURN Quotas, and Community Relays

October 19, 2026 15 min read
When it comes time to provision NAT traversal for a P2P application, “just use someone else’s free nodes” is the most commonly suggested route. In practice, most third-party P2P networks cannot be connected to at all: node discovery, authentication, traversal coordination, and the encrypted handshake are each governed by the network designer’s own private protocol. Without implementing that protocol stack, your first packet never goes out. Only three categories of nodes are open to any application and free of charge: public STUN, free TURN quotas, and community relay networks.

Building Dual-Spectrum Thermal Cameras on Embedded: From Core Module to NVR

October 13, 2026 14 min read
The companion post surveyed the technology, the NVR ecosystem, and the market. This one builds: say you have a thermal core on your desk — a Raytron 640×512 module, or a FLIR Lepton — and the goal is a dual-spectrum camera a stock NVR can manage. The hurdles, in order: the imaging pipeline, the radiometry chain, dual-channel registration, multi-stream encoding, platform protocols, analytics, and acceptance testing. The Big Picture: What’s Inside a Dual-Spectrum Camera mermaid flowchart TD A[Thermal core] --> C[SoC pipeline] B[Visible sensor] --> C C --> D[Dual encode<br/>H.265] C --> E[Temp events] D --> F[NVR/platform] E --> F style A fill:#f44336 style B fill:#2196F3 style C fill:#9C27B0 style D fill:#FF9800 style E fill:#FF9800 style F fill:#4CAF50 The thermal core and the visible sensor each image into the same IPC SoC (HiSilicon Hi3516DV500, SigmaStar, Rockchip — dual MIPI input is the hard requirement). The SoC runs thermal image processing, registration/fusion, dual-stream encoding, radiometry, and event reporting, then hands RTSP/ONVIF or GB28181 streams to the NVR. Worth knowing when picking the SoC: platforms like the Rockchip RV1126B ship native thermal support — beyond dual MIPI-CSI, the ISP includes IRFPA (infrared focal-plane array) processing: pixel-offset calibration (OCC), automatic NUC, de-striping, and dynamic-range compression all live in the hardware pipeline, effectively pushing part of the step-two image chain into silicon, with a 3 TOPS NPU alongside for person/vehicle classification. Three budgets to watch the whole way: memory (per-pixel temperature matrices and registration tables are not small), compute (640×512@25fps means 8M pixels/second through the pipeline), and bandwidth (two to three streams).

Dual-Spectrum Thermal Cameras: Principles, NVR Integration, and the Market

October 12, 2026 19 min read
Two in the morning, a fence line on a business campus. A visible-light camera with IR illuminators shows you the ten meters its light reaches — beyond that, black. A chemical tank farm is worse: the camera is supposed to watch, around the clock, not “is anyone there” but “which tank is running hot” — point a visible camera at a steel tank and it tells you nothing. Thermal imaging fixes exactly these two scenes: it needs no light at all, receiving infrared radiation emitted by the objects themselves, so image brightness maps directly to temperature. But thermal has its own blind spot — no texture, no readable faces, no license plates. So the industry bolts two lenses into one housing: thermal finds things, visible light identifies them. That’s a dual-spectrum (bi-spectrum) camera.
