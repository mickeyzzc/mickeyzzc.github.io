Omni-Modal AI Interaction Survey: Vision, Audio, Dialogue, and Task Execution

October 1, 2026 October 2, 2026 AI Tools Multimodal, Voice Dialogue, Full-Duplex, Agent, Survey AI Engineering and Survey Series 3806 words 18 min read
🔊

A 2024 GPT-4o demo — talking while listening, interruptible mid-sentence — turned omni-modal interaction from a paper keyword into a product direction. Two years on, the industry has split into two implementation routes: one stuffs listening, seeing, and speaking into a single end-to-end model (Omni models); the other chains VAD, ASR, LLM, and TTS into a pipeline. This post walks through both routes’ technical approaches, maturity, and representative open-source projects. Facts verified as of October 2026.

What “Omni-Modal Interaction” Actually Breaks Down Into

A human-like interaction needs at least five capability layers:

  • Hear: speech to semantics — ASR, or audio tokens inside the model
  • See: images and video joining the conversation — VLM frame sampling, or continuous video streams
  • Speak: speech generation — TTS, or the model emitting audio tokens directly
  • Take turns: full-duplex turn management — when to start talking, how to handle interruptions
  • Act: turning dialogue into action — function calling, GUI operation, robot control

Latency is the hard constraint across all five layers. Human turn-taking gaps average around 200ms (Stivers et al., PNAS 2009, measured across 10 languages); ITU-T G.114 classifies one-way interactive voice delay under 150ms as premium and up to 400ms as acceptable. For voice agent products, the common 2026 industry bar is p50 < 800ms and p95 < 1.5s — still roughly a 4x gap from human. The two implementation routes disagree, at their core, about how to spend those 800ms and where to put emotion and barge-in.

Route One: Single End-to-End Model (Omni)

The Architecture Pattern: Audio Becomes Tokens, the LLM Speaks Directly

The crux of the end-to-end approach is audio representation. Waveforms can’t feed an LLM directly, so neural codecs compress audio into discrete tokens, typically at 12.5–25Hz (12.5–25 token frames per second), with semantic content and acoustic detail either separated or layered:

CodecRateNotes
EnCodec (Meta)75HzEarly RVQ representative, carried on by the MusicGen line
Mimi (Kyutai)12.5Hz, ~1.1kbpsAcoustic stream distilled from WavLM semantic features; duplex-friendly
CosyVoice2 tokenizer25HzSemantic + acoustic tokens, flow-matching decoder
Qwen speech tokenizer12.5Hz multi-codebookCodebook 0 carries semantics; ~9 acoustic codebooks filled per frame by an MTP module

On top of the codec, there are five main ways open-source projects turned “text generation” into “speech generation”:

  1. Thinker-Talker (Qwen2.5/3-Omni): the LLM backbone (Thinker) emits text while a streaming speech module (Talker) predicts audio tokens in parallel, then a Code2Wav convolutional network turns tokens into waveforms; both decode heads share the Thinker’s hidden states
  2. LLM emits speech tokens + flow-matching decoder (GLM-4-Voice, Kimi-Audio): speech tokens trained like language tokens, decoded by the CosyVoice-line flow-matching model
  3. Interleaved text and audio tokens (Mini-Omni, Chroma): both token types mixed into one sequence at a fixed ratio — the simplest training recipe, and naturally “thinks while speaking”
  4. Parallel dual streams (Moshi): an RQ-Transformer models the user stream and the assistant stream simultaneously, with the text stream artificially delayed a few steps as an “inner monologue” — the only mature native full-duplex recipe in open source
  5. Interaction layer decoupled from thinking layer (DuplexOmni, and Freeze-Omni’s state-classifier variant): turn-taking policy separated from content reasoning

Audio and vision each pass through their own encoder into the LLM backbone (Thinker); the Thinker emits text while feeding hidden states to the Talker; the Talker predicts discrete speech tokens frame by frame; Code2Wav restores them to a waveform. The whole chain is streaming — first-packet latency doesn’t wait for the sentence to finish. The encoder box is really two: an audio encoder (~650M in Qwen3-Omni) and a vision encoder (~540M), time-aligned by TMRoPE.

Points worth calling out:

  • Qwen3-Omni remains the open-source benchmark: 211ms first packet (tech-report figure; MoE keeps active parameters small), video input, native tool calling, 119 text languages, Apache-2.0 with no commercial strings. But Qwen’s next-gen omni line (3.5-Omni, 3.8-Omni-Realtime) went API-only in 2026 — the open frontier is now pushed by OpenBMB, StepFun, BAAI, and Kyutai.
  • Full-duplex comes in two camps: native parallel streams (Moshi, FLM-Audio, MiniCPM-o 4.5’s Omni-Flow) versus system-level assembly (Kyutai’s Unmute wraps any text LLM in a voice layer; Xiaohongshu’s FireRedChat builds duplex from pVAD + end-of-turn detection + cascade pipeline). Mainstream models like Qwen3-Omni and Step-Audio 2 are still half-duplex with demo-level interruption.
  • The weakest link is interruption quality: telling a real interruption from a backchannel (“yeah”, “right”) — closed APIs lead clearly: OpenAI GPT-Live-1 (API since 2026-09, true full-duplex + SIP telephony), ByteDance SeedRealtime (2026-08, audio+video duplex shipped in the Doubao app), Alibaba’s Qwen-Audio Realtime line with semantic interruption. Open duplex latency also trails (MiniCPM-o 4.5’s honest figure: 0.5–0.9s).
  • Serving caught up in 2026: vLLM-Omni and SGLang-Omni decompose omni inference into an asynchronous multi-stage graph (Thinker → Talker → Code2Wav), which is what makes a Qwen3-Omni-class model deployable at concurrency. Native full-duplex serving for open models is still an RFC in vLLM-Omni, not a shipped feature.

The Training Side: Getting Reasoning into the Speech Loop

Step-Audio 2 introduced CoT reinforcement learning into a speech model (the open 2 mini shares the recipe), Step-Audio-R1.1 targets audio reasoning, and MiMo-Audio’s RL post-training pushed MMAU to 64.5. Further out, the “think while speaking” paper cluster from late 2025 (Shanks, Mind-Paced Speaking, Thinking While Speaking) converges on one idea: the speaking model keeps latency low while a reasoning model asynchronously supplies the intelligence. OpenAI’s GPT-Live-1 official architecture — “one model talks, another thinks” — shows the closed frontier and the open papers converging on the same design.

Each stage is an independently replaceable service: VAD detects someone speaking, turn detection decides whether they’ve finished, ASR produces text, the LLM understands and decides (including tool calls), TTS speaks. Video frames travel a side channel into a VLM (in frameworks like LiveKit and Pipecat), with the understanding results merged into the LLM’s context — they never touch the voice path.

Turn detection saw the biggest single-stage change between 2025 and 2026: from “VAD silence timers” to audio-native end-of-turn models — LiveKit’s Turn Detector v1 listens to raw audio (no longer waiting on transcripts), Pipecat’s Smart-Turn runs an 8M-parameter classifier on CPU, and Deepgram simply built endpointing into the ASR model. How well you distinguish “finished” from “just pausing for breath” directly determines how natural the conversation feels.

On latency budget: a fully streaming pipeline runs roughly VAD/endpoints 50–200ms, ASR finalize 50–150ms, LLM first token 300–800ms (the biggest chunk), TTS first packet 100–200ms, network 50–100ms. Pipecat’s official benchmark puts the whole pipeline around 700ms; but independent measurement of real phone calls (openbenchmarks on the Telnyx platform) found a median time-to-first-audio-byte of 1,296ms — the gap between summed component latencies and real experience comes mostly from multi-vendor network hops. For the p50 < 800ms bar, the bottleneck is almost always LLM first-token time and the network, not the ASR/TTS components themselves.

Function calling and MCP are standard equipment on voice pipelines now: the OpenAI Realtime API accepts remote MCP servers mid-session; in LiveKit, function tools and MCP servers mix in the same tool list; Pipecat registers functions with register_function. Both frameworks have first-class video channels — LiveKit can sample video frames from a call into the chat context or feed a VLM directly; Pipecat moves images through the pipeline as Frames. For an avatar, LiveKit’s avatar plugins (Anam, Tavus, Simli) attach after the TTS output.

Cascade or End-to-End?

DimensionCascade pipelineEnd-to-end S2S
Latency~500–800ms fully streamed, engineering-tunable200–400ms starting point, theoretical edge
Emotion and paralinguisticsLost at the text bottleneck; patched with TTS emotion tagsPreserved natively (breath, laughter, dialect, pace)
InterruptionAssembled from VAD/EoT, logic hand-writtenNative (open-source quality varies)
Controllability and debuggingText at every stage, models swappable freelyBlack box; swapping the base LLM means retraining
Tool callingMost mature at the text layerWorks, but one notch less reliable
Compliance and deploymentComponents can be hosted and audited separatelyOne monolithic model, hard to partition

Cascade remains the production mainstream in 2026, especially for telephony — the 8kHz phone network mismatches the 16–48kHz training distribution of most S2S models, and it shows the moment a call hits the PSTN. End-to-end wins on naturalness and interruption, which cascade can’t match; hence the hybrid: an S2S model manages conversation rhythm while an async reasoning LLM handles tools and complex tasks. GPT-Live-1’s “one talks, one thinks” is the closed-source version; LiveKit’s docs call the pattern half-cascade.

How a Conversation Starts: Entry Points Beyond the Wake Word

Everything above assumes the conversation is already running; there is a prior question of how it starts. The wake word is the default answer for always-on devices, but not the only entry point — and its role is shrinking from “say it before every command” to “say it once per session”. By trigger type:

  • Button / tap: the de-facto standard on phones — ChatGPT Voice and Gemini Live open a persistent session with a tap; headset long-press and steering-wheel buttons work the same. Zero false triggers, zero standby power; the cost is having to reach the device first
  • Wake once, wake-word-free within the session: the wake word only opens the session; full-duplex plus turn detection keeps the conversation going (Xiaomi’s Super XiaoAi continuous dialogue, Alexa Follow-Up, Gemini Live) until it times out
  • Always-listening without a wake word + addressee judgment: the microphone streams continuously with no keyword, so the hard question becomes “is this addressed to me?” Scenario-based solutions lock the addressee via context (AI outbound calls, drive-thru ordering, meeting notes — the call itself is the wake); multi-person environments disambiguate with mic zones (in-car multi-zone) and voiceprints
  • Visual wake: respond only when someone looks at the device and speaks — next subsection
  • Presence sensing: mmWave radar (the LD2410 class), PIR, or BLE proximity as pre-wake — when someone approaches, raise mic gain, light up, spin up the pipeline; leave the “did they speak” decision to VAD
  • Gestures / actions: raise-to-talk (Apple Watch), radar gestures, AirPods Conversation Awareness (media volume drops automatically when you start speaking)
  • AI-initiated: alarms, reminders, sound events (baby crying), proactive agents that speak first — the initiator flips to the device, and the hard problem becomes interruption control

Visual Wake: Look at It and Start Talking

Visual wake has one fully shipped product form: Look and Talk on Google’s Nest Hub Max, announced at I/O 2022. An on-device camera runs face detection, gaze estimation, and head-orientation checks (effective range about 1.5 m); a response requires the signals to coincide — someone speaking, a face enrolled in Face Match, and gaze directed at the screen, with video never leaving the device. Google also pitched an accessibility rationale: for deaf and hard-of-hearing users, an interaction that doesn’t require shouting a keyword is friendlier. In robotics the same problem is called attention estimation — deciding “you’re looking at me, you’re talking to me” — which is what powers desktop robots that greet you when you look at them; on the research side, 2025 brought gaze-triggered activation (sustain eye contact with an avatar to initiate silently). On the open-model side, MiniCPM-o 4.5’s Omni-Flow aligns video and audio streams on one timeline — exactly the model foundation for “always watching, always listening” interaction; for a minimal DIY prototype, MediaPipe face/iris landmarks plus Silero VAD will do.

Chinese smart-display products that ship publicly mostly stop at wake-word-free voice; gaze wake remains concentrated in research and demos for screen-equipped devices and robots. The reasons are plain: gaze accuracy under low light, profile views, and glasses, plus the privacy explanation cost of an always-on camera, are an order of magnitude harder than an always-on microphone.

Why the Wake Word Isn’t Dead

The engineering math is clear: always-on keyword spotting runs on a dedicated DSP for a few milliwatts, audio doesn’t leave the device before the keyword hits (privacy), and a two-syllable name settles “who is this for” for free. Every wake-free route pays on one of those three. Side-by-side of the entry points:

The resulting pattern: wake words for battery-powered, privacy-sensitive, noisy multi-person settings; buttons on phones and headsets; multimodal attention for screen-equipped and embodied devices; and for telephony the channel itself is the wake — nothing to add.

Wiring in Vision: Single-Frame QA to Continuous Video

Two ways to give a real-time voice agent eyes in 2026:

Frame sampling into a VLM — the default. Open-source picks concentrate on the Qwen3-VL family (from 2025-09, 2B–235B, Apache-2.0, 256K context extendable to 1M, hour-long videos indexed by second-level timestamps, built-in GUI and 3D grounding), InternVL3.5 (up to 241B MoE), GLM-4.5V (106B MoE, MIT), and MiniCPM-V 4.5 (8B, on-device, 96x video token compression). The framework samples frames, the VLM interprets, results join the dialogue context — cost and latency stay manageable.

Streaming video — always-on watching. StreamingVLM (MIT Han Lab + NVIDIA: attention sinks + a 16-second visual-window KV cache, 8fps over unbounded streams on one H100), Apple’s StreamBridge (proactive streaming assistant), and MiniCPM-o 4.5’s Omni-Flow, which aligns video and audio streams on one timeline. “Continuous observation + interjection anytime” went from papers to usable during 2026.

GUI agents form a complete “see → act” side branch, and the shortest path from vision to execution:

  • Open models: UI-TARS-1.5-7B (OSWorld 42.5%), OpenCUA-72B (45.0%), Alibaba GUI-Owl/Mobile-Agent-v3 (AndroidWorld 73.3%), Simular Agent S3 (self-reported 72.6%, past OSWorld’s 72.36% human baseline)
  • Closed: Claude computer use, Gemini 3’s built-in computer use, and the OpenAI Agents API computer-use tool, generally from 61% up
  • After single-app static tasks neared saturation, OSWorld 2.0 (2026-06, long-horizon multi-app workflows) knocked everyone’s scores back down — long horizons remain the branch’s weak spot
  • The runtime ecosystem is mature: browser-use (MIT, ~78k stars), UFO³, Mobile-Agent

For a voice agent, a GUI agent is its “hands”: speak the instruction, the agent executes on screen, with no per-app integration work.

The Execution Layer: Function Calling, MCP, and Embodied VLA

Execution in the digital world is standardized: function calling through the LLM, external systems through MCP, on-screen actions through GUI agents. Execution in the physical world is a separate model stack — VLA (vision-language-action):

  • openpi (Physical Intelligence’s π0/π0.5, Apache-2.0): knowledge-insulation fine-tuning touches ~1.4% of weights; a new task adapts in 10–15 hours on one A100
  • NVIDIA GR00T N1.6 (3B, 2025-12): dual-system architecture, cross-embodiment, deployable on Jetson Thor
  • OpenVLA-OFT (MIT): parallel decoding + action chunking, 26x faster action generation
  • LeRobot / SmolVLA (Hugging Face, 450M): community-scale robotics stack, playable on an SO-101 desktop arm
  • Chinese open source: AgiBot GO-1 (OpenGO-1), Unitree WLA-1.0 (evolved from Qwen2.5-VL)
  • Closed reference points: Figure Helix (7B VLM System 2 at 7–9Hz, 80M System 1 at 200Hz, plus a whole-body System 0 added in 2026), Gemini Robotics 1.5/2.0 (ER models as the brain orchestrating VLAs)

Helix’s S1/S2, GR00T’s System 1/2, Gemini’s ER + VLA — they all converge on the same architecture: a slow brain (VLM carrying internet-scale knowledge, language, planning) plus fast hands (diffusion or flow-matching action policies), bridged by abstract task descriptions. The implication for dialogue systems is direct: hooking a robot to a voice dialogue model doesn’t mean training a bigger end-to-end model — the dialogue layer serves as part of System 2 and the VLA executes. It’s the same division of labor as S2S-plus-async-reasoning-LLM, replayed one layer down.

Choosing Between the Two Routes

Conclusions by scenario:

  • Building a product (support calls, outbound calling, voice assistants): cascade. Start from LiveKit or Pipecat; pick your ASR/LLM/TTS. The self-hosted Chinese stack is complete in 2026 — Qwen3-ASR or FireRedASR in, any LLM in the middle, CosyVoice 3 out. For naturalness, go straight to managed S2S: GPT-Live-1, Gemini Live, Doubao SeedRealtime, and Qwen-Omni-Realtime all support barge-in, some with video input
  • Research or self-hosted full-duplex: Moshi (native dual-stream), MiniCPM-o 4.5 (video + audio duplex, runs on-device), FLM-Audio; for system-level, FireRedChat (open-sourced by Xiaohongshu, pluggable pVAD + end-of-turn + cascade/semi-cascade)
  • Adding eyes: start with Qwen3-VL frame sampling; StreamingVLM for live streams; MiniCPM-o 4.5 for an integrated on-device option
  • Adding hands: UI-TARS-1.5 or GUI-Owl plus browser-use for screens; openpi or GR00T N1.6 for robots

One unavoidable trap: open weights are not open for commercial use. OpenAudio S1-mini and Spark-TTS weights are CC-BY-NC-SA (no commercial use), TEN VAD’s license carries non-compete terms, and GLM-4-Voice’s weights ship under Zhipu’s custom license — check the weights license individually, not just the repo’s LICENSE file.

Benchmarks and Known Weak Spots

Benchmarks come in three families:

  • Static multimodal understanding: MMAU-Pro (audio — Qwen3-Omni-30B leads open models at 62.9), OmniBench, WorldSense, Video-MME — QA-style benchmarks nearing saturation at the top, with diminishing discrimination
  • Realtime interaction behavior: Full-Duplex-Bench iterated from v1 (2025-03) to v3 (2026-04), measuring pauses, backchannels, turn-taking, and speech overlap — v3 even tests tool calling amid disfluent speech; Sierra’s τ-voice evaluates voice agents on real support tasks. GPT-Live-1 launched claiming +30 points on Full-Duplex-Bench over its predecessor
  • Task execution: OSWorld-Verified (desktop GUI), AndroidWorld (mobile GUI — UI-TARS-2 and Mobile-Agent-v3 both at 73.3), LIBERO and AgiBot World (embodied)

Still hard as of October 2026:

  1. Interruption quality: no agreed benchmark separates interruptions from backchannels, and open full-duplex latency remains noticeably behind closed APIs
  2. Audio hallucination: in duplex mode, models hearing their own output as input — “self-listening loops” are an active 2026 research topic
  3. Inconsistent latency claims: first packet 211ms, response 195ms, 80ms — different definitions; check what’s measured before comparing
  4. The quality ceiling of discrete tokens: Ming-flash-omni 2.0 retreating to a continuous speech tokenizer shows the discrete-token quality tax is still real
  5. Long-horizon tasks: every system drops sharply on OSWorld 2.0 — long chains of vision plus action are the current weak spot

Neither route replaces the other: cascade keeps production default status on controllability, end-to-end models hold the “sounds human” line on naturalness and interruption, and the genuinely new shape in 2026 is the hybrid — a speaking model and a thinking model splitting the work. The shortest open-source path to catch up: start voice dialogue from Qwen3-Omni or Moshi, start pipelines from LiveKit or Pipecat, look at vLLM-Omni for omni serving, and track Full-Duplex-Bench for realtime interaction evaluation.
