Moving the Voice Pipeline On-Device: The 2026 Edge Speech Stack

September 7, 2026 September 6, 2026 AI Tools ASR, TTS, On-Device AI, Wake Word, Voice Assistant, Edge Computing AI Engineering Series 1932 words 10 min read
🔊

A cloud voice assistant’s response latency breaks into four segments: audio upload, cloud recognition, model inference, and synthesis on the way back. Each one looks short on its own; stacked together they are the “over a second” users feel. Two hidden costs ride along: the privacy bill of streaming every conversation to the cloud, and the availability bill of going completely dark when the network does.

In 2026, the conditions for moving the entire pipeline on-device finally lined up. On the streaming-ASR piece, Moonshine v2 turned “speak, wait, then read” into “read while speaking.” The intent layer gained dedicated models like Needle 2 at a few dozen MB. And on-device TTS crossed the quality bar. This post splits the pipeline into five stages, surveys the usable open-source options and their hardware floors for each, then settles the latency bill with real deployment cases. The TTS technology history and the 31-engine selection guide already exist on this blog (promo-video series, parts five and six), so this post only covers the on-device angle.

Two engineering facts get overlooked. First, the biggest win of an on-device wake word is not power saving — it is that the audio stream never has to leave the device, settling privacy and bandwidth in one move. Second, VAD is not optional: without it, the ASR gets fed silence and coughs, and you pay in both compute and latency.

Ears: wake word and VAD

A wake word is a small acoustic classifier running 24/7, power-sensitive — the only stage of the pipeline that truly belongs on an MCU.

The inflection came in 2024: Kevin Ahrendt’s microWakeWord runs quantized INT8 TFLite wake word models directly on the ESP32-S3. Home Assistant’s Voice PE and the ESP32-S3-BOX-3 use it, ESPHome ships a ready-made micro_wake_word component, and microwakeword.com trains custom wake words for free — you can make your device respond to whatever phrase you like, no vendor permission needed. openWakeWord is more flexible with better customizability, but it runs server-side in Home Assistant — a fit when the device is too weak for custom models but a LAN server exists. Espressif’s ESP-SR is the other line: WakeNet for wake words plus the AFE audio front end (echo cancellation, noise suppression, VAD), the deepest fit with ESP32-S3 — the same chip that ran WiFi sensing in the previous post also owns the front end of the voice pipeline.

For VAD there is little suspense: Silero VAD is the default answer — a 2MB-class ONNX model, MIT licensed, real-time on CPU, from Raspberry Pis to browsers. Its output is not just “someone is speaking” but speech-segment boundaries: the ASR consumes cleanly cut segments, not a continuous stream.

Hearing clearly: three routes for ASR

Pick an ASR by asking two questions: do you need streaming (text while speaking), and what language. The 2026 open-source landscape splits cleanly along those two questions.

The offline transcription standard: whisper.cpp. C/C++ inference for OpenAI’s Whisper with GGUF quantization, covering workstations down to phones and SBCs, 99 languages, and the thickest ecosystem (VAD filtering, diarization, bindings — all off the shelf). The weakness is architectural: it is not streaming — built for “record a segment, transcribe a segment” — so first-token latency suffers in live conversation.

Streaming low latency: Moonshine v2. Useful Sensors’ new paper this year (arXiv:2602.12241) restructures the model into four stages: audio preprocessing, a streaming encoder, an adapter, and a decoder. The key change is in the encoder — sliding-window self-attention with no positional embeddings, giving bounded memory and compute, naturally suited to unbounded live streams. The lineup runs from tiny up to streaming-medium (245M); English models are MIT, other languages use a community license. The v1 speed advantage over Whisper (roughly 5x on the same hardware) carries into v2; the price is language coverage, which stays English-first.

The accuracy ceiling: Canary-Qwen-2.5B. NVIDIA NeMo’s hybrid architecture uses Qwen2.5-0.5B as its decoder and tops the Open ASR leaderboard at 5.63% mean WER with an RTFx of 418. But it is a 2.5B English-only, non-streaming model whose deployment floor is workstation class — using it in an edge voice assistant is overkill; it belongs under transcription services. Watch the license: the HF model card says CC-BY-4.0 while the leaderboard paper says CC-BY-NC — check it yourself before commercial use.

The Chinese line: SenseVoice-Small. Alibaba FunAudioLLM’s non-autoregressive model — by its official numbers it beats same-class Whisper on Mandarin and Cantonese, and bundles language ID (zh/yue/en/ja/ko), emotion tags (happy/angry/neutral), and audio event detection (music, applause, laughter). For Chinese voice interfaces, those bundled capabilities are often more useful than WER itself. The surrounding FunASR ecosystem ships complete VAD, punctuation, and streaming pipelines. NVIDIA’s Parakeet series deserves a mention as the evergreen of the fast English line — it shows up in the supported lists of edge engines like Cactus too.

Understanding: the intent layer

ASR outputs text; deciding “what to do” happens in the intent layer. Two schools exist here — the Needle 2 deep dive covered the details, so here is just the division of labor:

  • General on-device LLMs (the Qwen3-0.6B class): can chat and handle open input, at the cost of starting at hundreds of MB of memory;
  • Dedicated intent models (Needle 2, 45M): text in, JSON out, grammar-guaranteed well-formedness, MCU-class hardware — at the cost of no conversation ability.

Real projects often run both plus confidence gating for a hybrid: local handling for high-confidence requests, cloud escalation otherwise — Needle 2’s built-in confidence head exists for exactly this. Worth calling out separately: the Cactus engine runs whisper/parakeet for ASR and Needle 2 for intent in the same runtime, so the whole “hear clearly + understand” chain shares one deployment — a rare convenience on-device.

Speaking: the on-device TTS increment

The TTS paradigm history (VODER to Flow Matching) and the 31-engine comparison already live in parts five and six of the promo-video series; here are just the three names that matter on-device:

ModelSizeNotes
Kokoro-82M~160MB (fp32) / less quantizedOpen-source quality benchmark, Apache-2.0, multilingual
Kitten TTS14M–80M (nano <25MB)ONNX, CPU-only, runs in browsers and wearables, Apache-2.0
Piper20–60MB per voiceThe SBC classic, fast, MIT

The positioning gap between the tiers matters more than the parameter gap: Kokoro is the quality-first tier and still the community favorite for open-source on-device TTS in 2026; Kitten TTS fills the ultra-light tier — v0.8’s 14M nano brings TTS into browser and wearable budgets; Piper is the old friend on single-board computers, stable and fast. The on-device metrics to watch are not MOS scores but first-audio latency and memory footprint. Voice-cloning models (the XTTS / F5-TTS tier) remain too heavy for edge use and carry high misuse risk — leave them out of voice-assistant scenarios for now.

Assembling: cases and the latency bill

from kokoro_onnx import Kokoro

Those two snippets are the minimal voice loop; the rest of your budget goes to the intent layer.

Case one: fully offline on an RK3588 SBC. The whisper.cpp + Piper combination runs a completely offline voice stack on ARM64 single-board computers — recognition and synthesis never leave the LAN. Notably, the RK3588’s 6-TOPS NPU does not participate in this pipeline; plain CPU suffices, which says the bottleneck for SBC-class voice is never raw compute — it is memory bandwidth and engineering integration.

Case two: whisper.cpp on Android. A developer embedded whisper.cpp in an Android app, transcribing locally and then applying a structured layer that turns speech into executable actions — the same idea as localizing the intent layer from the Needle 2 post, with the intent logic in plain code instead. The phone is the most realistic landing spot for on-device voice: mic, speaker, and compute all present; the only fight is the memory budget.

Case three: the 14-month fully local assistant. A community builder spent 14 months on a Qwen + whisper.cpp + Kokoro assistant, fully local. The current state: about 5 seconds from finishing a sentence to hearing the first syllable. Read that number honestly: slower than the cloud, but every block is open-source and auditable and it works offline. The biggest share of those 5 seconds is LLM inference — precisely what Moonshine v2 (front end) and Needle 2 (intent layer) are built to shave.

Case four: ESP32-S3 as front end only. Home Assistant Voice PE’s architecture keeps microWakeWord awake on the device while recognition and understanding run on a LAN server — MCU-class hardware honestly takes only the wake stage and does not pretend to run ASR. This “device wakes, edge server understands” compromise is the mainstream for ESP32-class hardware in 2026, and the ESPHome ecosystem makes it turnkey.

The boundaries, written plainly:

  • Few streaming options for Chinese on-device. SenseVoice is non-streaming (the ecosystem’s pipelines stream around it, but the model itself does not), and Moonshine v2 is English-first — real-time Chinese conversation on-device is the most visible gap in this landscape.
  • Full-duplex conversation is not ready. Models like Moshi and Sesame CSM prove the direction, but they are far from edge deployment; 2026 edge assistants remain half-duplex — you finish, then I answer.
  • MCUs stop at wake and fixed commands. An ESP32-S3 handles wake words and fixed command phrases (ESP-SR MultiNet), not open-vocabulary ASR — a “fully local assistant on an MCU” remains premature.

To close: of the five stages, wake word, VAD, and TTS are settled on-device; ASR still falls short on streaming and Chinese; and the intent layer has just been cracked open by dedicated models like Needle 2. When selecting, keep track of which stage is not good enough yet — that matters more than remembering which model is best, because the not-good-enough stage decides which part of your product stays in the cloud.
