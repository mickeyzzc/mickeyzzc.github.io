YOLO Version Differences Explained: Mainline, Academic Line and Ecosystem Families

September 2, 2026 September 6, 2026 AI Tools YOLO, Computer Vision, Deep Learning, Object Detection AI Engineering Series 3281 words 16 min read
🔊

The timeline table in the getting-started post tells you which version shipped when. It does not answer the real selection question: what exactly changed in each version, and what does the change mean for training and deployment. The trap I kept hitting while researching this: most comparison articles squeeze three unrelated development lines into one timeline, and you walk away remembering nothing but version numbers. This post splits them apart — the Ultralytics mainline, the academic numbered line, the ecosystem families — and covers the end-to-end rival line (RT-DETR family), the open-vocabulary branches, and licenses along the way.

Version Numbers Are Not One Line

The “vN” numbering fragmented long ago. After 2024, “YOLOv13” and “YOLO26” are parallel work by two different teams in two different codebases, with no code inheritance between them:

  • Ultralytics mainline: YOLOv5 → YOLOv8 → YOLO11 → YOLO26. After v8, Ultralytics dropped the “vN” naming and jumped to 11 and 26, leaving 12 through 25 unused (12 got taken by the academic line).
  • Academic numbered line: YOLOv6 (Meituan), YOLOv7 and YOLOv9 (Academia Sinica, Taiwan), YOLOv10 and YOLOv13 (Tsinghua-affiliated teams), YOLOv12 (University at Buffalo + UCAS). One paper per version, no code inheritance from Ultralytics.
  • Ecosystem families: YOLOX (Megvii), PP-YOLOE (Baidu), YOLO-NAS (Deci), DAMO-YOLO (Alibaba DAMO Academy). Each has its own framework and never participated in the numbering sequence.

One boundary needs spelling out: YOLOv9, v10 and v12 have been absorbed into the Ultralytics pip package — yolov9c.pt, yolov10n.pt and yolo12n.pt load directly (v6 has configs too; v7 and v13 do not). But absorption means hosted weights and a unified API only; the architecture research is still done by the original teams, and most of their upstream repos are dormant.

Two “existence” questions up front: the YOLOv14 arXiv paper, posted in August 2026, was withdrawn by its own authors weeks later — it does not count; YOLOv15 does not exist. Ultralytics’ YOLO27 appears only in the roadmap teaser for YOLO Vision 2026 on September 13, 2026 — no weights, no specs as of this writing.

Ultralytics Mainline: YOLOv8, YOLO11, YOLO26

This is the industry default. The direction of each generation is clear: v8 established the framework, YOLO11 cut parameters, YOLO26 cut post-processing.

YOLOv8: Anchor-Free and a Unified Framework (2023.01)

YOLOv8 has no paper — it shipped as code on January 10, 2023 — but it is the starting point of most YOLO engineering today. Key changes from v5:

  • C2f replaces C3: borrowing the ELAN idea from YOLOv7, the first conv outputs 2x channels split into two branches, and every Bottleneck output joins the concatenation — a richer gradient flow than C3’s two-branch concat.
  • The head becomes anchor-free + decoupled: classification and regression each get their own conv branch; positive/negative assignment no longer depends on anchor priors and IoU thresholds but uses TaskAlignedAssigner, which picks top-k positives by a weighted combination of classification score and alignment IoU.
  • Regression no longer predicts 4 coordinates directly; each side predicts 16 bins integrated through DFL (Distribution Focal Loss) — a design that foreshadows YOLO26.
  • Five task types share one API; train, val and export all use the same commands.

One benchmarking caveat people miss: the v8 docs measure GPU latency on A100, while YOLO11 onward use T4 — comparing latency numbers across versions directly will mislead you.

YOLO11: C3k2 and Position-Sensitive Attention (2024.09)

YOLO11 was announced at YOLO Vision 2024 on September 10, 2024, with code landing September 30 in ultralytics v8.3.0. The backbone and neck topology looks close to v8; the changes concentrate in three places:

  • C3k2 replaces C2f: shallow stages use compact blocks at 0.25 expansion, deeper stages swap the inner Bottleneck for the small-kernel C3k block. The idea is a deeper, narrower network for the same parameter budget.
  • C2PSA sits after SPPF: a CSP block wrapping PSABlocks (position-sensitive multi-head spatial attention plus FFN), giving the backbone tail a stretch of global receptive field.
  • Scaling changes: the depth coefficient goes from v8’s 0.33 to 0.50, and max_channels for m/l/x is capped at 512.

The result is the official 22% parameter reduction (YOLO11m 20.1M vs YOLOv8m 25.9M) plus an mAP gain (51.5 vs 50.2), and CPU ONNX inference dropping from v8n’s 80.4ms to 56.1ms. One widespread claim to correct: “YOLO11 redesigned the segmentation head” comes from a third-party paper — diff the Segment head configs of v8 and v11 and they are identical; Ultralytics never said this.

YOLO26: End-to-End Dual Head, No DFL (2026.01)

YOLO26’s release stretched across nearly four months: previewed at YOLO Vision 2025 (London) on September 25, 2025, with final weights delayed to January 14, 2026 in ultralytics v8.4.0. The backbone carries over the YOLO11 topology; the changes concentrate in the head and training strategy:

  • End-to-end dual head, NMS-free inference by default. The one-to-one head outputs up to 300 detections directly; a one-to-many head provides dense supervision during training. Setting end2end=False brings back the old pipeline — per the docs, the one-to-many head is typically slightly more accurate, and the gap between the two numbers in the docs table is 0.6 to 0.8 mAP.
  • DFL removed. The regression head’s reg_max drops from 16 to 1 and the DFL module degenerates to nn.Identity() — a lighter head, with regression range no longer bounded by bins.
  • MuSGD optimizer: a hybrid of Muon and SGD — some parameters get Muon-style updates, others plain SGD. The training blog credits the idea to Muon’s success in LLM training, naming Moonshot’s Kimi K2.
  • ProgLoss (progressive loss balancing): the loss weights of the two heads shift dynamically over training — early training favors the one-to-many head (stable, high recall), later training favors the one-to-one head (matching inference).
  • STAL (small-target-aware label assignment): on 640 input, objects smaller than about 8 pixels often received no anchor assignments at all; STAL enforces a minimum of four assignments for them.
  • New task types: semantic segmentation (-sem) and monocular depth (-depth) become official tasks for the first time; pose estimation adopts the RLE loss with up to 7.2 AP claimed.

CPU inference is YOLO26’s headline: officially 43% faster than YOLO11n (their own Intel Xeon 2.00GHz benchmark), while the docs tables give 38.9ms vs 56.1ms — about 31%. Direction agrees; the exact number depends on test conditions, so cite the source conditions.

The arrows indicate release order only — most of these teams share no code lineage: v9 continues v7’s gradient-path research, v10 and v12 are independent works from Tsinghua and Buffalo, and v13 is built on the Ultralytics framework without being merged into the pip package. Each version has a paper with a clear motivation, and each has its own deployment catches.

YOLOv9: PGI and GELAN (2024.02, ECCV 2024)

Chien-Yao Wang’s team at Academia Sinica, Taiwan — the YOLOv7 group. Two contributions:

  • PGI (Programmable Gradient Information): as gradients travel through deep networks, information bottlenecks distort the supervision signal by the time it reaches shallow layers. PGI attaches a reversible auxiliary branch during training to deliver full input information to the loss; at inference the branch is removed entirely, at zero cost.
  • GELAN (Generalized Efficient Layer Aggregation Network): using only conventional conv operators, gradient-path planning achieves parameter utilization that beats depthwise-based designs.

YOLOv9-E reaches 55.6% mAP at 57.3M params — one of 2024’s accuracy benchmarks. Mind the license: v9 inherits the YOLOv5 codebase and is GPL-3.0 — the older copyleft that infects derived code, where AGPL additionally covers network-service scenarios. Closed-source commercial use requires a commercial license either way. The repo went dormant after August 2024; practical maintenance moved to Ultralytics-hosted weights.

YOLOv10: Consistent Dual Label Assignments (2024.05, NeurIPS 2024)

The Tsinghua team — the same people who later wrote YOLOE. The core is one idea: train with two heads, a one-to-many head for dense supervision and a one-to-one head producing exactly one prediction per ground truth; inference runs only the one-to-one head, so NMS is simply not needed. A consistent matching metric ties the two heads together so the one-to-one head’s quality holds up.

This is the intellectual source of YOLO26’s end-to-end dual head. v10 also did an efficiency-driven structural pass: decoupled spatial-channel downsampling, rank-guided compact blocks, large-kernel depthwise convs in accuracy-oriented stages, and partial self-attention only in later stages.

Officially, YOLOv10-S is 1.8x faster than RT-DETR-R18 at similar AP, and YOLOv10-B cuts YOLOv9-C’s latency by 46% and params by 25% at equal accuracy. The repo went dormant after March 2025 and the idea was absorbed into YOLO26 — on the “no NMS” feature alone, v10 beats v26 by 20 months.

YOLOv12: Squeezing Attention into a Real-Time Detector (2025.02, NeurIPS 2025)

University at Buffalo + UCAS; the title is the thesis: an attention-centric backbone can still run real-time. Two mechanisms:

  • Area Attention: split the feature map into 4 areas and run self-attention per area, bringing attention cost from quadratic back to near-linear without exotic operators.
  • R-ELAN: block-level residual with scaling, fixing the training instability of large attention models.

YOLOv12-N hits 40.6% mAP at 1.64ms (T4), 1.2 points over YOLO11-N. Two hard deployment constraints: FlashAttention only supports Turing and later GPUs (T4, RTX 30/40 series, A100 — Volta cards like V100 cannot run it), and Ultralytics’ retrained weights measure 2 to 4 points below the paper at m/l/x (paper 55.2/57.2/58.1, Ultralytics table 52.5/53.7/55.2) — cite the right source.

YOLOv13: Hypergraph High-Order Correlations (2025.06)

A Tsinghua-affiliated team; the code lives at iMoonLab — the iSunCoral URL widely cited in third-party articles is a 404 now, so watch your sources. Previous YOLOs model pairwise correlations whether through conv or attention; YOLOv13 uses a hypergraph that treats multi-scale feature pixels as vertices and learnable hyperedges as correlations, capturing high-order correlations in one shot:

  • HyperACE (hypergraph-based adaptive correlation enhancement): hypergraph computation with linear-complexity message passing.
  • fullPAD (full-pipeline aggregation-and-distribution): the enhanced correlation features are re-injected through three tunnels — backbone-to-neck, within-neck, neck-to-head — improving gradient flow.
  • DS blocks: depthwise-separable convs replace large-kernel convs to cut parameters.

YOLOv13-N reaches 41.6% mAP at 2.5M params, 2.1 points over YOLO11-N — the academic line’s most recent word as of September 2026, with no credible new numbering after it. The repo is AGPL-3.0, built on the Ultralytics framework, not in the pip package, with a community far smaller than the mainline.

YOLOX’s historical weight exceeds its present one: the anchor-free decoupled head plus dynamic label assignment (SimOTA) combination became mainstream through it, and every major detector now uses that playbook. The upstream repo’s last release was April 2022, but its export chains to ONNX, TensorRT, ncnn and OpenVINO are the most complete in the YOLO world — still a solid inference workhorse, just don’t expect upstream bug fixes.

PP-YOLOE+ represents the deployment-friendly route: it deliberately avoids quantization-hostile operators like Deformable Conv and Matrix NMS, pairing the x model’s 54.9 test-dev mAP with 95 FPS TensorRT FP16. It is deeply bound to the PaddlePaddle ecosystem; outside it, there is little reason to pick it.

YOLO-NAS is a cautionary tale. Deci’s AutoNAC-searched architecture with quantization-aware design lost only 0.5 points in INT8 conversion — a solid selling point. In May 2024 NVIDIA acquired Deci and dissolved the entity; pretrained weight URLs have been 404ing one by one, the weights were non-commercial from the start, and the commercial licensing channel is gone. The technical ideas (NAS plus quantization awareness) live on in RF-DETR’s NAS and D-FINE’s distillation; the product itself should not be chosen anymore.

This is RT-DETR’s official iteration line (lyuwenyu/RT-DETR, Apache-2.0, still updated as of August 2026). Baidu released RT-DETR in April 2023, and the CVPR 2024 paper title pulls no punches: “DETRs Beat YOLOs on Real-time Object Detection”. R50 hit 53.1 mAP at 108 FPS (T4) — the first end-to-end detector to beat YOLOs on speed. It is not a YOLO variant; mechanically it is a different animal:

  • Efficient hybrid encoder: AIFI runs self-attention only on the highest-level features (semantic interaction), CCFF does cross-scale fusion with CNNs, avoiding full-scale attention cost.
  • Uncertainty-minimal query selection: when initializing 300 queries, it explicitly suppresses classification-localization inconsistency so the decoder gets more reliable queries.
  • Inference is NMS-free by nature — a year before YOLOv10.

Beyond the official line, two independent successors matter: D-FINE (ICLR 2025 Spotlight) reformulates box regression as progressive refinement of probability distributions across decoder layers (FDR), then distills the final layer’s distribution knowledge into shallower layers via global optimal localization self-distillation (GO-LSD) — 55.8 mAP at X scale, 59.3 with Objects365 pretraining; RF-DETR (Roboflow, March 2025) pairs an NAS-searched LW-DETR with a DINOv2 pretrained backbone, claiming at launch the first real-time model over 60 mAP on COCO (60.5 @ 728px), now with a segmentation line.

The 2026 accuracy-latency picture is roughly this: the highest absolute closed-set accuracy belongs to YOLO26x (57.5), but that is Ultralytics grading its own homework; at the same T4 latency tier, RF-DETR-L’s 56.5 mAP @ 6.8ms (Roboflow’s own benchmark) beats YOLO26l’s 55.0 @ 6.2ms. NMS-free is no longer the DETR line’s exclusive advantage (v10 and v26 both have it); what remains is the accuracy-latency ratio at mid-small scales, and the option of an architecture other than YOLO. The YOLO line’s strength was never a single metric — it is 17 export formats and the corner-case ecosystem of ncnn/RKNN/OpenVINO.

Open-Vocabulary Branches: YOLO-World and YOLOE

A traditional YOLO is closed-set: COCO’s 80 classes with the head’s output dimension baked in — adding a class means retraining. The open-vocabulary branches turn that into editing a prompt:

  • YOLO-World (Tencent AI Lab, CVPR 2024): RepVL-PAN connects CLIP’s text encoder into YOLO’s path-aggregation network, aligning both embedding spaces with region-text contrastive learning. The accompanying “prompt-then-detect” paradigm is the speed key — the vocabulary is encoded into offline embeddings ahead of time, so detection never runs the text encoder; that is how LVIS 35.4 AP at 52 FPS (V100) is achieved. Pick the v2 checkpoints: re-parameterizable and exportable to ONNX/TensorRT, which v1 cannot do.
  • YOLOE (Tsinghua team, in the pip package since ultralytics v8.3.99, March 2025): three prompt modes — text, visual (a few example boxes), and prompt-free (a built-in 4,585-class vocabulary, “see everything”). Three mechanisms over YOLO-World: RepRTA refines CLIP text embeddings with an auxiliary network that folds away at export, zero inference cost; SAVPE is a visual-prompt encoder with decoupled semantic and activation branches; LRPC lets the prompt-free path skip the language model. Per the paper, YOLOE-v8s beats YOLO-Worldv2-S by 3.5 AP on LVIS at a third of the training cost and 1.4x the inference speed.

Engineering-wise the integration cost is now trivial: swapping YOLOWorld for YOLOE is a one-line change, and the first text-prompt use downloads a mobileclip2 TorchScript encoder (about 254MB for YOLOE-26). For scenarios where classes change often — inspection targets expanding, temporary targets added — prompt-swapping without retraining wins by a wide margin.

Two lessons. First, same name “YOLO”, different licenses: v6/v7/v9 are GPL-3.0 while v10/v12/v13 are AGPL-3.0 — the former infects derived code, the latter additionally covers network services. Second, “the repo is Apache-2.0” does not mean “the weights are commercial-friendly”: YOLO-NAS is exactly that combination, and RF-DETR’s XL tier carries its own license. The standard escape hatches for AGPL in closed-source products are buying a license or isolating inference into a separate process/service (whether that constitutes a derivative work is a question for your lawyers).

Selection Advice

Distilling the differences above:

  • General detection, ecosystem first: YOLO11 or YOLOv8. Thickest docs, tutorials and third-party export chains, most troubleshooting material.
  • Edge devices, CPU inference: YOLO26n/s. NMS-free end-to-end output plus a lighter regression head — the only generation seriously optimized for CPU.
  • Maximum accuracy: closed-set, look at YOLO26x (57.5, official self-benchmark); mid-scale low latency, look at RF-DETR-L or D-FINE; for small targets, YOLO26’s STAL is a targeted design.
  • Changing classes, zero-shot: YOLOE. Text-prompt class switching without retraining, prompt-free mode sweeping a 4,585-class vocabulary.
  • PaddlePaddle ecosystem: PP-YOLOE+.
  • Research reproduction: v9’s PGI/GELAN, v12’s Area Attention and v13’s hypergraph are each borrowable mechanisms; code exists, maintenance varies.

License first. For closed-source commercial use, AGPL (the whole Ultralytics family) means buying a license or isolating the service; Apache-2.0 (RT-DETR, D-FINE, YOLOX, PP-YOLOE) is the genuinely burden-free choice.
