Who Speaks, Who They Mean, Whether to Answer: Response Decision in Voice Dialogue

October 2, 2026 October 2, 2026 AI Tools Voice Dialogue, Speaker Diarization, Voiceprint, Full-Duplex, Survey AI Engineering and Survey Series 3599 words 17 min read
🔊

The previous survey had a section on “how a conversation starts” comparing wake words with other entry points. This post goes one layer deeper: what does the wake word actually do for the system, and what has to replace it when you remove it?

The answer is three judgments. When you call “Hey XiaoAi” at a speaker, the system instantly settles three things: whoever says the name is probably an enrolled user (who is speaking); the name is aimed at the device (who they’re talking to); a name obviously deserves a reply (whether to answer). Human conversation never starts every sentence with a name, because the human brain runs these three judgments continuously in the background — recognizing voices, working out whether a remark is aimed at you, deciding when to chime in and when to just listen.

Making a voice assistant treat the wake word as optional — and answer only some of what it hears — means unpacking those three bundled judgments and running each one per utterance. This post covers the technical implementation, current state, and shipped products for each judgment. It assumes no prior voice-development background and explains every term at first use; facts verified as of October 2026.

A Living-Room Scenario

Picture the most common setup: a TV drama is playing, a family member is chatting nearby, and you look at the smart speaker and say “turn off the lights.”

Before and after that one utterance, the system has to get three things right in a row:

  1. Who is speaking: the microphone picks up a mix of TV audio, chatter, and your voice — the system must first decide that the voice worth processing is yours. Otherwise one line of TV dialogue could switch the lights off
  2. Who they’re talking to: your family member says “turn on the AC” — to each other, not to the speaker. The device should pretend it heard nothing
  3. Whether to answer: even mid-conversation with you, you take a phone call and talk for five minutes — the system should wait quietly, not try to cut in

Get any of the three wrong and the product turns into a chatterbox (answers everything) or plays dead (ignores you when called). The wake word is stable because one explicit signal skips all three judgments — at the cost of shouting a name first. Wake-free designs unpack those judgments into the system one by one:

Judgment One: Who Is Speaking — Diarization and Voiceprint

The Core Concept: Speaker Embedding

Every voice differs in timbre, resonance, and speaking habits — distinguishable like a fingerprint. Technically this is a speaker embedding: a neural network compresses an utterance into a fixed-length vector — for example Alibaba’s open-source CAM++ model (~7M parameters) compresses audio of any length into a 192-dimensional vector — utterances from the same person land close together in that space, different people far apart. Compare two vectors with cosine similarity; above the threshold, same person. Where to set the threshold is an engineering trade-off: too high and strangers get mistaken for the owner; too low and the owner isn’t recognized when they change their tone.

“Enrollment” is how the vector gets into the database: consumer products have you read a few sentences to the device, store your embedding, and compare every subsequent utterance against it. HomePod stores up to 6 users; Alexa Voice ID, Google Voice Match, and the voiceprint enrollment in Xiaomi’s and Baidu’s assistants all follow the same pattern. Notably, none of them publishes voiceprint accuracy figures — the only public numbers are in academic papers.

Three Generations of Speaker Diarization

Voiceprints answer “is this someone I know.” When three people talk over each other in one recording, you also need to split their utterances apart — that is speaker diarization. Three generations:

Generation one: modular pipelines. A VAD (voice activity detection — deciding which spans of audio contain speech versus silence/noise) cuts out speech segments, each segment gets a speaker embedding, then clustering — segments with nearby vectors are grouped as one person. pyannote is the open-source benchmark for this generation (code MIT): on the AMI meeting corpus it reaches 18.8% DER (diarization error rate — the share of speech time attributed wrongly, including missed segments, wrong speakers, and lost overlaps), which you can read as roughly a fifth of speech time being attributed incorrectly. Fine for offline transcription (meeting notes, subtitles).

Generation two: end-to-end neural networks. EEND (2019) reframes diarization as multi-label classification per frame — “which speakers are vocalizing right now.” The big win is overlapping speech: two people talking at once can both be labeled, which clustering pipelines struggle with.

Generation three: streaming diarization. Real-time dialogue cannot wait for the recording to end — the system must answer while the user is still finishing, so diarization must produce results as audio arrives. Anchors:

  • NVIDIA Streaming Sortformer (2025-08, 117M parameters, up to 4 speakers, open weights): latency down to 0.32s, error rate about 10–11.5% on the CALLHOME phone corpus; its successor Nemotron-3-Diarization (2026-09) extends to 8 speakers, also open
  • Diart (MIT): pyannote segmentation + embeddings + incremental clustering; the usual foundation for building your own streaming diarization
  • pyannote: open-source version remains the offline transcription pick; the commercial pyannoteAI Live-1 offers a streaming diarization API under 300ms
  • Soniox (managed API, v5 from 2026-06): token-level attribution — every transcript token carries a speaker label, enrollment-free, up to 15 speakers per session

Responding Only to the Owner: Personalized VAD

A generic VAD answers “is anyone speaking”; a personalized VAD answers “is it my owner speaking”: the enrolled embedding is fed into the VAD as a condition, and the model judges per frame the probability that this voice belongs to the target user. Google proposed this in 2019 (Personal VAD 2.0 in 2022, optimized for on-device streaming), precisely to suppress “strangers wake the device” errors. Xiaohongshu open-sourced a reproducible implementation in FireRedChat in September 2025 (pVAD, an ECAPA-embedding gate), designed for full-duplex barge-in. One step further is target speaker extraction (Google’s VoiceFilter line): filter the owner’s voice out of a two-person mixture — the TV dialogue becomes literally inaudible to the system.

The Car Cabin: Space as the First Separator

The in-cabin problem gets a different solution: instead of asking “who,” ask “from where.” A microphone array (4–8 microphones) computes the sound source’s direction from the tiny time differences of the same sound reaching each microphone, then beamforming synthesizes an enhanced signal toward that direction while suppressing others — conceptually like cupping a hand behind your ear toward the speaker. Driver, front passenger, and both rear seats become four zones, each with independent wake and recognition. Li Auto has used 4 microphones for four zones since the Li ONE (zone locking added by OTA in 2021); XPeng’s 2025 G9/G6 ship four-zone recognition with source localization across the lineup; NIO’s Banyan 3.0 went cabin-wide wake-free. These are vendor claims without third-party metrics, but the design conclusion is clear: geometric isolation beats purely acoustic separation.

Where the Ceiling Is

Know the acoustic ceiling: 10–20% DER on benchmarks, worse in meetings full of interruptions, overlaps, and laughter. Voiceprints can be spoofed by replayed recordings and speech synthesis (consumer products mitigate with random challenge phrases as liveness checks; open-source solutions have no anti-spoofing — build your own). Different devices, a cold, and noisy backgrounds all degrade recognition. In practice, “who is speaking” is never one model — it’s voiceprint, zone, and gaze stacked together.

Judgment Two: Who They’re Talking To — Addressee Detection

Is this utterance meant for me? Harder than it looks. Academia calls it addressee detection, with three signal families:

  • Lexical cues: “Xiao Wang, turn off the lights” versus “turn off the lights” — the addressee and semantic direction differ completely
  • Acoustic cues: where the sound comes from (zone attribution, beamformed direction estimates) — you don’t face the speaker when chatting with family
  • Visual cues: who the speaker is looking at — when you ask a device “what time is it,” your gaze is probably on it

Two numbers show the difficulty. Microsoft’s ICASSP 2015 paper fused all three families plus dialogue state for addressee detection in triadic dialogue and concluded that acoustic + lexical + dialogue state is basically sufficient — gaze and head pose add little. On the AMI meeting corpus, human annotators agree on “who is this utterance addressed to” at only about 0.45 — two people listening to the same utterance often give different answers, because many turns in everyday dialogue simply have no explicit addressee. In January 2025, Kyoto University released the first LLM benchmark for multimodal multiparty addressee recognition; only about 20% of the corpus’s turns have an explicit addressee, and GPT-4o scored barely above chance.

Since neither machines nor humans judge this reliably in open dialogue, shipped products simplify the problem with engineering. Three patterns:

Pattern One: Closed-Set Intent Gating (the Mainstream Wake-Free Form)

The idea: don’t answer the open question “who is this for”; answer the closed question “is this one of my known commands.” Always-on streaming ASR produces text continuously, a local intent classifier checks for known intents, matches execute, everything else is ignored — the term is open-set rejection: the classifier must reserve an “none of the above” exit. Without it, the classifier force-fits chatter into the nearest command, and wake-free operation becomes a false-trigger machine.

Examples at three scales:

  • Google Quick Phrases (launched January 2022): say “stop” or “snooze” when the alarm rings without saying “Hey Google” — these phrases live in an on-device closed phrase set with very high matching precision. Still live four years on
  • ESP-SR MultiNet (Espressif, ESP32): offline recognition of up to 200 commands at <500ms — wake-free operation for smart lamps and toys
  • Chinese EV cabins (the largest deployment): XPeng’s full-scenario voice, Li Auto’s cabin-wide wake-free operation, Huawei’s multi-intent sentences, NIO’s cabin-wide wake-free mode. The mechanism is zone localization to lock the speaker + streaming ASR + local intent classification with rejection. The driver says “turn on the AC” — zone locked, intent matched, executed. “It’s a bit hot today” — no intent match, ignored, even if the driver is the only one speaking. Vendors don’t publish rejection thresholds, but the conservative “ignore all open-vocabulary speech” strategy is exactly why false triggers stay manageable

Pattern Two: Sensor Gating

Replace the linguistic judgment with the certainty of another sensor. Look and Talk (Nest Hub Max) requires “looking at the screen + an enrolled face + speaking” to hold simultaneously — turning addressee detection into a visual precondition. Covered in the previous post; not repeated here.

Pattern Three: End-to-End Models Judge Directly

2026 brought research that hands the judgment to the omni model itself: in-vehicle fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-Omni-7B to decide which utterances in multiparty car dialogue are directed at the device (published in Neurocomputing 2026). This layer is moving from hand-crafted feature classifiers to models that learn it — still at the paper stage.

Judgment Three: Whether to Answer — Response Decision

Both judgments passed — now the behavioral one: it’s addressed to me, but should I answer, and how? The full option set is three-way: silence, backchannel, full response. When humans listen to other humans, most of what they emit are backchannels — “mm,” “right,” “really?” — signaling attention without taking the floor; a full response comes only when asked or tasked; unrelated chatter gets quiet listening. Machines mostly lack this layering: they either answer everything or ignore everything.

End-to-End Models: Learning the Decision into the Model

Full-duplex models implement the decision with different mechanisms, from fully implicit to fully explicit:

  • Moshi: no explicit rules at all. A dual-stream transformer models the user’s audio stream and its own simultaneously; having seen vast amounts of real dialogue in training, when to take the floor, yield, or backchannel is all learned. The most natural — and you can’t explain why it didn’t answer on any given turn
  • Freeze-Omni: the other extreme, fully explicit. A three-state classifier on the LLM’s hidden state, evaluated at the end of each audio chunk: state 0 — the user is still speaking, keep listening; state 1 — the user is interrupting, abandon the current generation and restart; state 2 — the user finished without interrupting, the current generation continues. Interpretable and low-latency, but coarse — it cannot express subtle behavior like backchanneling
  • VITA-1.5: a middle ground — a parallel interrupt-detection model monitors user audio next to the generation model, governing just the “should I stop” decision
  • DuplexOmni (2026-06): two asynchronous layers — an interaction layer (end-to-end full-duplex, real-time turn handling) and a thinking layer (deep reasoning); the latter thinks slowly, the former catches the floor meanwhile
  • GPT-Live-1 (OpenAI, API since 2026-09): the most completely productized full-duplex, officially “listens and speaks simultaneously, handles pauses, interruptions, and backchannels.” Public numbers: 80.1% interactivity on Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5, turn-taking latency 0.798s (predecessor: 1.41s), 87% tool calling under disfluent speech, $0.05/min

The Pipeline Side: How Builders Assemble It

If you build on orchestration frameworks like LiveKit or Pipecat, first separate two concepts: EoT (end-of-turn) detection answers “did the user finish” — not “should I take this.” The former has off-the-shelf parts: LiveKit’s Turn Detector listens to audio directly, Pipecat’s Smart-Turn runs an 8M-parameter model in 12ms on CPU, and LiveKit’s adaptive interruption handling analyzes the first few hundred milliseconds of a barge-in to tell a real interruption from a cough. The latter has no general product component — you assemble it. The common construction is a two-stage gate plus a state machine:

On each audio chunk:
  1. Voiceprint gate: not an enrolled user → stay silent (skip even full ASR)
  2. Outside a session: no known intent match → ignore (closed-set gate)
  3. Inside a session:
     a. Did the user finish? (EoT)
     b. How to respond? (LLM structured output: silence / backchannel / answer)
  4. Long silence or a closing phrase → exit the session, return to step 2

Two engineering notes: hysteresis — once engaged in a conversation, stay engaged until it ends; do not re-judge participation per utterance, or the experience stutters. And learning from interruptions — a user interrupting the AI’s output, or long stretches of no reply, are the most direct negative feedback; 2025-2026 research turns these into RL training signals (“Aligning Spoken Dialogue Models from User Interactions”, TurnGuide) — the right direction, but nothing has shipped as a product.

Group Chats and Meetings: The Multiparty Version

A group-chat bot deciding whether to reply to a message, a meeting AI deciding whether to interject — same decision, more people. The state of play is more conservative: IBM’s IUI 2025 taxonomy of AI participation control in group conversations finds that current products (Teams Copilot Facilitator, meeting bots) still base participation timing on @-mentions and whitelist rules — no product lets the model decide autonomously when to speak; low profile beats misfired interjections.

Walking the living-room scenario through the chain: a line of TV dialogue — the voiceprint gate finds no enrolled user, silence, everything downstream skipped; a family member tells another “turn on the AC” — voiceprint matches (they enrolled too), but the intent doesn’t match or the zone is wrong, ignored; you look at the speaker and say “turn off the lights” — voiceprint matches, intent matches, the dialogue engine executes; you follow with “and wake me at seven tomorrow” — already in-session, EoT confirms completion, the LLM’s three-way decision returns “answer,” the alarm is set.

Every gate eats latency and adds false rejections: misjudge “who is speaking” and you miss responses; misjudge “whether to answer” and you get a chatterbox or a dead device. The common 2026 product trade-off is openness for precision — car cabins use fixed zones plus closed-set intents, giving up free-form chat for controlled false triggers; end-to-end full-duplex merges the three middle gates into the model, trading engineering for training data, at the cost of behavior that is hard to verify and control.

Common Questions

Why not just feed everything to a large model and let it judge?

Money, latency, and false triggers. Always-on streaming ASR plus a resident LLM is not affordable for a multi-person household; LLMs answer everything, and would take TV dialogue and family chatter as commands, with false-trigger rates far beyond user tolerance; and continuously sending home audio to cloud inference is a privacy cost most users won’t accept. Hence the universal design: cheap gates first, expensive models later.

Can voiceprints be spoofed with a recording or synthetic speech?

Yes. Replay attacks (playing a recording of the owner) and voice-cloning are real threats to voiceprint recognition. Consumer products mitigate with liveness detection — randomly asking the user to read a given phrase. Open-source solutions (including pVAD) contain no anti-spoofing; a self-built system must add it.

What about two people speaking at once?

Overlapping speech is the hardest part of diarization (where DER is worst) and exactly what full-duplex models train for. Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5 has a dedicated side-conversation test; target speaker extraction can filter just the owner’s voice; car cabins isolate physically by zone. Open multi-party conversation remains a research frontier.

I want to build this myself — where do I start?

Three tiers: for full-duplex dialogue on a phone or server — start from LiveKit Agents (with Turn Detector and interruption handling) + Silero VAD + any LLM + CosyVoice 3. For “respond only to my owner” — 3D-Speaker’s CAM++ for enrolled embeddings with a cosine threshold, or FireRedChat’s pVAD directly. For edge devices — ESP-SR (WakeNet wake words + MultiNet commands), noting it has no official voiceprint module yet. To experience end-to-end full-duplex models, Moshi and MiniCPM-o 4.5 both run locally.

State of Play: What Works and What Doesn’t

A capability-tiered answer to “does this exist today”:

  • Wake-free within a session, with interruption and backchanneling: mature. GPT-Live-1, Gemini Live, Nova 2 Sonic (GA 2025-12, with turn-taking controllability), and Chinese cabin full-duplex are all product-grade
  • Respond only to enrolled users (voiceprint gating): mature. In use across consumer products; open-source-wise, pVAD plus CAM++/ECAPA assembles it yourself
  • Wake-free on a closed command set: mature. Quick Phrases has run for four years, EV cabins deploy at scale, MultiNet reaches down to ESP32
  • Automatically judging addressee and response-worthiness in open dialogue: frontier, unsolved. Agreed benchmarks only exist since 2025, GPT-4o is near chance on addressee recognition, and a 2026-09 analysis paper’s title is the conclusion — full-duplex models “take the floor when asked, not when needed” (Full-Duplex Speech Models Take the Floor When Asked, Not When Needed). Content-driven self-selection (correcting a mistake, supplying a missing word, warning when nobody asked) remains out of reach for every model

So the accurate statement of “wake-word-optional like a human” is: achieved in closed domains and within sessions; still a wide gap in open multi-party dialogue — after all, even human inter-annotator agreement on “who was that addressed to” is only 0.45. That is not one model’s weakness; the problem itself is ambiguous. The pragmatic engineering route remains: voiceprint gating + intent rejection + an explicit state machine, assembled into a conservative but controllable system, leaving the learned response policy for the next version. For evaluation, watch Full-Duplex-Bench (v1 through v3 cover pauses, backchannels, overlaps, and tool calling under disfluency); for addressee recognition, Kyoto University’s 2025 multimodal benchmark.
