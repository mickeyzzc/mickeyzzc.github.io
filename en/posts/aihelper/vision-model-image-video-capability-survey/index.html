Can Vision Models Read Images and Watch Video? A Capability Survey: What's Solved and What's an Industry-Wide Boundary

October 23, 2026 October 24, 2026 AI Tools Vision Models, Video-LLM, Multimodal, Tracking, ReID, Survey AI Engineering and Survey Series 2027 words 10 min read
🔊

Suppose you’re handed a requirement: ten thousand hours of surveillance footage, and it must answer two kinds of questions. Kind one: “Who is this person,” “where did this car go,” “when did he enter the server room.” Kind two: “What was he doing in there,” “at what moment did he start,” “was he turning those screws fast or slow.”

Both kinds are called “visual understanding,” but their fates are completely different. Kind one has been beaten to saturation on public datasets — engineering-wise it’s deliverable today. Kind two is the entire industry’s capability boundary in 2026 — the strongest closed models fail the pass line, and not in a “wait six months” way. This post walks through the state of both, where the boundary sits, what the correct architecture looks like, what the money math is, and where the open-source licensing traps are. Data verified as of October 2026; every benchmark score carries its caveats, because the water content here is higher than in most fields.

Splitting “Understanding” into Six Layers

“Can vision models understand video” is unanswerable until you decompose it. The industry layers tasks like this:

LayerTaskInputOutputMaturity
L1 image perceptionclassification, detection, segmentation, OCRsingle frameboxes, masks, texthigh, industrial grade
L2 identity & attributesface matching, person re-identification, attributesframe or cropidentity, similarityhigh (saturated on public sets)
L3 cross-frame associationmulti-object tracking, cross-camera trackingframestrack IDshigh (occlusion/clothing-change hurt)
L4 action & eventsaction classification, temporal groundingclipaction + time intervalmedium
L5 scene structureshot boundaries, highlightswhole videosegments, summarymedium-high
L6 semantic understandingvideo QA, text-to-video searchlong video + instructionlanguage, timecodesmedium-low; long-horizon weak

Terms, explained in place: person re-identification (ReID) means recognizing “this is the same person” across cameras — not knowing who they are, but threading their track through a hundred cameras; multi-object tracking (MOT) gives each person a stable ID across frames that survives occlusion; temporal grounding answers “this action starts at second N and ends at second M.”

The critical split is between L2/L3 and L4/L6: the former answer “who is this, where did they go” via per-frame feature matching; the latter answer “what are they doing, when did it start” via temporal modeling. Two completely different tech stacks, not interchangeable — that distinction runs through this whole post.

Images: Recognition Is Basically Solved

Good news first. Static-image recognition (L1/L2) is mature industry:

TaskPublic benchmarkPublic scoresCaveats
Face 1:1 verificationLFW99.5%–99.85%Saturated — can’t separate good models from great ones
Face 1:N searchIJB-C~87% TAR @ FAR 1e-5Protocol-sensitive; fine-tuned vs zero-shot differ hugely
Person ReIDMarket1501mAP 94–96Must state whether re-ranking was used (a post-process worth ~2 points)
Person ReID (harder)MSMT17mAP 77–87Closer to real cross-camera conditions; more informative

mAP is the standard retrieval metric — roughly “how high the right answers rank.” Read the Market1501 number carefully: “mAP 0.96, therefore solved” conflates two things — public-set saturation is not your scenario being solved. The risk has moved off accuracy into three places: data compliance (faces are sensitive biometrics with legal obligations around collection and storage), liveness anti-spoofing (photos and replayed video fool pure algorithms), and cross-camera domain shift (your angles, lighting, and sharpness differ from training data; field accuracy drops).

Engineering conclusion: identity tasks take mature solutions (InsightFace-class open models or commercial SDKs) and spend the budget on anti-spoofing and compliance — not on chasing accuracy.

Video: Three Benchmarks, One Answer

Now the bad news — and the most important part of this post. “Understanding continuous action and timing” (L4/L6) is the industry-wide boundary. The evidence isn’t one model’s weakness; three independent academic benchmarks point the same way:

BenchmarkTestsHumansBest modelsNote
TimeBlindtemporal blind spots: when did it start, how fast, in what order98.2%Gemini 3 Pro 48.2%, GPT-5 46.3%Fine-grained timing: none pass 50%
MotionBlindpure motion understanding (self-recorded video, leak-proof)—Only Gemini 3.1 Pro passes overall, speed judgment still fails; open models at the 6.25% random floor60 instances only — direction credible, don’t over-extrapolate
Video-MME-v2comprehensive video QA under strict protocol90.7%Best closed 49.4%, best open 39.1The old protocol overrated everyone by ~17 points

Different designs (semantic composition, physical motion, group-level consistency), one conclusion: models don’t watch motion — they read frame content plus language priors. Plainly: the model samples a few frames, sees “a man on the floor next to a cup,” and guesses “he slipped” from common sense; it never tracked the slipping process. Shuffle the frames — or remove the video and keep only a text description — and many models’ answers barely move.

Direct consequences for selection:

  • “When did it start, how long, how fast” — use dedicated temporal-grounding models (ActionFormer class); don’t ask a Video-LLM
  • “Count the people / boxes moved” — current models are near random on running counts (instance-counting accuracy measured at 7–18%)
  • Speed and direction judgments (driving behavior, motion tempo) — no usable general model today
  • “What happened” — semantic summaries: Video-LLMs clearly beat rule engines; this is their home turf

One more score trap: the “Video-MME 87%+” figures quoted in launches come from leaderboard pages that label themselves “display only, vendor-reported.” Acceptance testing always runs on your own data — those numbers aren’t even a reference baseline.

The Correct Architecture: Two Pipelines, Each Doing Its Own Job

Since no single stack covers all six layers, production systems run two layers. Watch:

The top layer is a discriminative pipeline: detection (YOLO, RT-DETR — boxes around every person and vehicle) → tracking (ByteTrack, BoT-SORT — boxes chained into trajectories) → ReID (same person across cameras) → action/temporal grounding (small dedicated models). It’s precise, real-time (runs on edge hardware), and explainable (you can point at the frames and boxes behind every conclusion). Its weakness: predefined classes only — never trained on “no hard hat,” never reports one.

The bottom layer is a Video-LLM (video multimodal large model: Gemini, Qwen3-VL, Kimi): frames are sampled, compressed, and fed to a large model for natural-language QA. Zero-shot (describes behaviors it never saw trained), cross-segment reasoning, “find me the clip where someone falls.” Its weaknesses are the mirror image: unreliable speed/count, imprecise timecodes, token-billed.

Each layer is evaluated on its own, then merged: the timeline comes from the top layer (second-accurate), the description from the bottom (natural language), and the report combines them. Do it the other way — Video-LLM for timecodes, discriminative for descriptions — and you get the worst of both.

Long Video: The Problem Becomes “How to Watch Frugally”

How does a Video-LLM “watch” a one-hour clip? By sampling: default one frame per second, each frame converted to tokens (the model’s billing unit of information). With Gemini’s official numbers: low-res ≈100 tokens/second, default ≈300 tokens/second — one hour at default ≈1.08M tokens, already pressing the 1M context ceiling. That’s the long-video bottleneck: not “can it watch,” but whether the token budget survives.

The three main 2026 savings routes:

RouteExampleMechanismEffect (with caveat)
Agentic on-demand samplingGemini agentic mode (Flash models only)low-fps scan first, high-fps revisit of key segmentsup to −88% tokens on long content (vendor “up to” figure)
Codec-native block selectionMicrosoft Mage-VL (paper stage)motion-vector energy picks high-information blocks over uniform sampling−75%+ tokens (self-reported)
Multi-frame compressionMiniCPM-V 4.5’s 3D-Resampler6 frames → 64 tokens~96× compression (4.5 only; 4.6 is a 1.3B edge model with 4×/16× switching)

The route-agnostic engineering pattern is segmented summary + on-demand retrieval: cut the long video into shots (boundary detection is mature), summarize each segment into an index, and pull back high-fps originals only for relevant segments when queried — the same idea as RAG for long documents.

Three Open-Source Licensing Traps

This ecosystem has a property: permissive code ≠ commercial-safe. The three classic traps:

YOLO is AGPL. Ultralytics’ license page is explicit: AGPL-3.0 is viral — even self-trained weights, internal-only use, never distributed requires open-sourcing the whole project, or buying the Enterprise license. Plenty of teams train an internal YOLO detector and ship it; that’s licensing Russian roulette. Alternatives: RT-DETR and D-FINE are Apache-2.0.

SAM 3’s license is not an open-source license. Meta’s SAM 3/3.1 uses the custom SAM License: royalty-free but use-restricted (military/weapons applications prohibited), gated weights, attribution requirements. If you need a standard license only, use SAM 2.1 (Apache-2.0).

A paper’s license is not the weights’ license. The V-JEPA 2 GitHub repo is MIT (three utility files Apache-2.0); the internet folklore “paper is CC BY-NC-ND, therefore non-commercial” is unverified — and even a non-commercial paper license wouldn’t imply anything about the weights. Confirm weight terms with the rights holder directly; don’t infer.

A Few Common Questions

Can the model count “how many people”? Single frame, sparse targets: just barely. Running counts (total entries, boxes moved): near random. Count with a detector frame-by-frame plus de-duplication, not an LLM.

Can you trust the timecodes? Second-level references drift. Use temporal-grounding models for precise timelines; treat Video-LLM timecodes as navigation (jump to roughly there), never as evidence.

Can public leaderboard scores go into acceptance criteria? No. Vendor self-reporting, protocol drift, data leakage — three layers of water. The right way: build a 50–200 item “golden clip set” (test questions you labeled yourself), split into perception / timing / motion categories, and re-run on every model or sampling change.

All open-source or all API? The precision layer (detection/tracking/ReID) runs locally — cheap, real-time, data stays on-prem; the semantic layer calls APIs by volume — no GPUs to own, models keep improving. Decide each layer separately; don’t marry one vendor.

Want to Try It Yourself

  • Feel the boundary fast: take a sports or worksite clip, ask “what action did he perform” (likely right) then “how many seconds did it last — faster or slower than last time” (likely wrong). A two-minute experiment beats ten reviews
  • Local deployment: start with Qwen3-VL (Apache-2.0, all sizes) or MiniCPM-V 4.6 (1.3B, laptop-grade)
  • The precision layer: RT-DETR + BoT-SORT + FastReID are all Apache/MIT-class; MMAction2 ships ready action-recognition and temporal-grounding implementations
  • A selection POC: run the two-channel comparison on 30 of your own clips — discriminative channel for identity/trajectory accuracy, LLM channel for semantic QA — and let the numbers pick the mix
