MiBeeBell: 30 Alerts In, One Notification Out

November 2, 2026 November 7, 2026 MiBeeOSS Go, Alerting, Self-Hosted, Notifications, Noise Reduction, MQTT, SQLite MiBee Projects Series 1582 words 8 min read
🔊

One camera in my studio watches a hallway. Headlights sweeping past, a moth landing on the lens, tree shadows moving in the wind — all of it gets classified as motion detection. Dozens of alerts hit my phone in a night; once the push volume crosses a threshold I stop registering them, and the one alert that actually matters disappears into the noise.

For years the alerting here was improvised: the NVR pushed its own notifications, Steward’s device alerts went out as SMTP mail, and IoT device anomalies were dropped into an MQTT topic nobody watched. Channels scattered, no aggregation, no silence windows, and nowhere to manage the rules as a whole. MiBeeBell is the alert notification center I wrote for exactly this: every system sends its events to it, it does the noise reduction, and only the part worth interrupting you reaches a channel you picked. It is a CGO-free single binary in Go with SQLite for storage — Raspberry-Pi-class hardware can run it as a resident service. The name joins the MiBee studio brand with “Bell” — the bell a hive rings when something is wrong.

Why write another alert center

On the “events in, notifications out” path, existing tools split into two camps. ntfy and Gotify are pure notification pipes: POST over HTTP and the phone buzzes — they don’t care what the event is, whether it repeats, or whether it’s worth sending; noise control is entirely the sender’s problem. Alertmanager and Nightingale have real alert pipelines (grouping, inhibition, silencing), but Alertmanager is welded to the Prometheus ecosystem and Nightingale needs MySQL plus Redis to run. Lightweight and pipelined — nobody offered both at once.

The market itself is in turmoil: Opsgenie has announced its shutdown timeline, Grafana OnCall’s self-hosted edition has been archived, PushDeer is no longer maintained. “A lightweight self-hosted alert center that is still maintained” has become a scarce position — that is the direct reason this project exists.

Seven ingest types

A notification center is only worth what it can ingest without modifications on the sender side. Bell exposes seven entry types:

  1. Generic webhook: your own system POSTs a JSON — severity, title, body, and dedup key are all part of the contract;
  2. MQTT: a device publishes one message and that’s an alert; cameras and sensors never touch any relay service;
  3. Alertmanager-compatible: point an existing Prometheus alerting rule’s webhook at Bell and it just works;
  4. PagerDuty Events v2-compatible: webhooks from SaaS monitoring products aim straight at Bell;
  5. Grafana-native: Grafana’s alerting UI targets Bell directly;
  6. Simple JSON (ntfy/Gotify shape): scripts that used to post to ntfy or Gotify barely change;
  7. Bark-compatible route: the URL an iOS device already has configured for Bark can point at Bell as-is.

All seven normalize into one internal event model: who sent it, what type, how severe, what the dedup key is, and whether it’s a resolution. The pipeline downstream only knows that model.

The pipeline: four gates + the L0 algorithm layer

The pipeline is what separates Bell from the “notification pipes”. Every event passes four gates, in a fixed order:

The story in the animation: one camera floods 30 motion-detection events in a night. The silence gate lets normal events pass and holds everything inside a maintenance window; the dedup gate merges same-source events into one, tagged ×30; the cooldown gate suppresses repeat re-sends; the escalation gate watches alerts that never resolve and raises severity when the timeout expires. Exactly one notification leaves.

GateWhat it catchesBehavior
SilenceEvents inside a maintenance windowRecorded and tagged, no notification
DedupSame-source repeats within the windowMerged into one, body gains ×N; the first event sends immediately for low latency
CooldownRecurrences after a notification went outCounter updates, no re-send; resolutions are never cooled down
EscalationAlerts that don’t resolveOn timeout: raise severity and add backup channels

Two details are deliberate: resolution events pass through even during a silence window — otherwise you get a wave of stale false alarms when the window ends; and cooldown suppresses only recurrences, never recoveries — when a device comes back, you hear about it immediately.

Beside the gates sits the L0 algorithm layer: pure Go, no model, no network, always on.

  • N-point holdoff: a single-sample spike doesn’t notify; N consecutive points do — built for glitch false positives, and it only applies to warning and below; error and critical are never filtered;
  • EWMA dynamic baseline: tags frequency surges — a camera dropping offline fifty times in one night is a network problem, not fifty independent failures;
  • Sequence-similarity clustering: a motion-detection storm arriving every two seconds merges into one event by sequence similarity;
  • Common-cause merge: a dead switch derives 30 “device offline” events; they merge into one “zone event” keyed on subnet or upstream-device tags.

Windows and thresholds are rule-level parameters, overridable per event type. There is one self-imposed constraint in the implementation: the pipeline is pure functions plus explicit state, and state depends only on SQLite and in-memory windows — acceptance scenarios like “30 motion events in 30 seconds merge into 1 notification” become table-driven tests directly.

AI enhances, it never gates

L0 already covers most of the noise, and Bell still ships three tiers of AI enhancement — with the rule written into the architecture: AI enhances, it never gates. The L0 algorithm layer is always on; L1/L2 are pluggable and switchable; degrading loses only the “enhancement”, never events — when the AI is down, alerts still flow.

The three tiers: L0 is rule reflection; L1 is a lightweight classification model running locally that triages each event in milliseconds to seconds (false alarm? normal fluctuation? needs attention?) and appends its conclusion as a tag — it may only downgrade, never upgrade, and any conclusion below the confidence threshold is discarded; L2 is generative summarization over an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, and a local LLM on the LAN works fine.

“Only downgrade, never upgrade” is the most important line in this design: no matter how confident the triage model is, it cannot raise a warning to critical, and it cannot suppress any event. Semantic edge cases like a human-shaped shadow at night are handled by two safeguards together — the wording of the criteria and the confidence threshold — not by trusting the model.

Channel layer: one adapter per channel

On the outbound side Bell doesn’t pick sides: office collaboration channels (WeCom, DingTalk, Feishu), general-purpose IM channels (Telegram, Slack, Discord), push services (Bark, ntfy, Pushover), and classic SMTP mail are all in the outbound list. Every channel is an independent adapter behind one interface — when a platform changes its policy, only that adapter file dies; the pipeline and every other channel keep working.

The paired rule is true self-hosting: no feature depends on the operator’s cloud for relay, and channel credentials never leave your own disk. Network down, official domain gone, the company behind the service vanished — under all three, every Bell feature stays fully usable. Credentials that never leave the machine also close off a whole class of leak paths.

Camera-offline events from the NVR and device anomalies from Steward arrive over their own webhooks; sensors and DIY devices come in over MQTT; on phones, Scout — another product in the ecosystem — receives the pushes and offers one-tap silencing. The rule pack configured for the NVR shows the pattern: a camera-offline event defaults to dedup plus a five-minute cooldown — you get one notification after ten minutes of silence, one when it recovers, and every flap in between only updates the counter.

The problem Bell solves is not new, and existing tools each cover a corner of it; the position it takes is assembling those corners into one whole that needs no database cluster, no cloud account, and runs on a Raspberry Pi. Every event in the studio worth a “ding” now passes through it.
