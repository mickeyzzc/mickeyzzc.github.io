Adaptive Recording and Storage Design in MiBeeNvr

October 18, 2026 October 18, 2026 MiBeeOSS NVR, Adaptive Recording, Storage, Pixgate, MiBeeVision, Compressed Domain, Open Source MiBee Open Source Projects Series 4390 words 21 min read
🔊

Outside writeups of MiBeeNvr’s adaptive recording mostly stop at “motion-aware, saves disk”: what signal drives the gating, why the switch loses no frames, and which cameras it fails on rarely get covered. This post takes all three apart — plain words first, then a picture, then the parameters, with a full glossary at the end. Mechanism descriptions are based on the v0.13.0 documentation and release records (October 2026); the version-by-version timeline is in MiBeeNvr: From v0.1.0 to v0.12.0.

Comparisons with Frigate, Blue Iris, Synology Surveillance Station, ZoneMinder, and Shinobi appear along the way — for selection reference. Selection is trade-offs, not ranking.

Three-Minute Version

Surveillance scenes do nothing 95%+ of the time, yet continuous recording keeps writing at full frame rate and full bitrate. Adaptive recording exists to fix exactly that:

  • When the scene is quiet, writes thin out to a “timelapse-grade” sparse mode — one keyframe every 30 seconds
  • On detected activity, full-frame-rate recording resumes immediately, with zero frames lost at the switch
  • Measured static-scene disk usage drops by roughly 75%–98% (measurement basis below)

“Detected activity” uses P-frame size statistics: no decoding, no AI model, near-zero CPU cost. That is the biggest divergence from other approaches, covered below.

There is deliberately no server-side “AI verdict → automatic event recording” chain — an active trade-off driven by the hardware positioning; the corresponding answer is MiBeeVision, at the end of this post.

Why Byte Counts Reveal Motion

This section is the foundation for everything that follows.

A video is not a stack of equal-sized photos

An H.264/H.265 camera stream cuts the picture into GOPs (Groups of Pictures). Each GOP starts with an I frame (keyframe) that depends on nothing else and decodes into a complete picture on its own. After the I frame comes a run of P frames, each recording only “what changed since the previous frame”.

The key is the P frame: the quieter the scene, the smaller the P frame. An empty hallway’s P frames might be a few tens of KB; a person walking through forces the encoder to describe their outline, motion, and shadow — the same P frame can balloon to hundreds of KB. A quiet GOP is carried by one or two large I frames with tiny P frames in between. The P-frame size sequence is therefore a cheap activity curve, visible without decoding a single frame.

How much can the write rate differ on one camera

2K H.265 camera pointed at a mostly static studio, from the documentation’s measured basis:

ScenarioWrites per hour
Continuous recording~2700 MB
Adaptive recording (hourly average)688 MB
Fully unattended period (sparse-segment conversion)~81 MB

The sparse conversion: 5.6 MB / 248 s ≈ 81 MB/h, against ~190 MB full-rate for the same period — roughly a 34× gap.

This trick has one natural enemy

If the camera’s bitrate is “flattened” — the same number of bytes per second whether or not anyone is there — P-frame sizes stop revealing motion. Some smart-codec cameras are exactly like this (permanently static picture, constant bitrate). That is the blind spot of compressed-domain gating; the fallbacks are pixgate and tiered recording, below.

Hardware Positioning, and Restraint About Server-Side AI

MiBeeNvr’s design baseline is low-end ARM boards with 1GB of RAM (Raspberry Pi 3B as the verification baseline), a 512MB process-memory cap, one static binary, zero external dependencies. Every feature has to pass this budget line.

Within that budget, a resident inference model is unrealistic — it either eats the memory or forces Coral/GPU hardware, which breaks the “an old device runs it today” premise. So the core path depends on no model at all. AI lives in two places, both outside the budget:

  • Browser side: detection runs on the viewer’s device (ONNX Runtime Web + WebGPU); more viewers share the compute, the server stays within budget — the trade-off being that it isn’t 7×24 unattended
  • External Vision (MiBeeVision): inference runs on other machines (Jetson / Coral / x86); the NVR stays light and dumb, conclusions come back through an API — detailed at the end

The connection to storage is direct: the cheaper the activity check, the more often it can run; the more often it runs, the harder the “nobody’s watching” write density can be pressed down. Compressed-domain signals push the checking cost to near zero, pixgate’s classical CV covers the compressed-domain blind spot, and Vision carries the layer that genuinely needs semantics — three cost tiers, low to high, each minding its own stretch, none crossing the budget.

Core Mechanism: Compressed-Domain Gating

Plain words

The NVR doesn’t decode the picture; it watches each P frame’s byte count against a “rolling baseline”. The baseline is the stream’s typical recent P-frame size, maintained with MAD (median absolute deviation — a jitter-resistant statistic). A P frame suddenly several times the baseline (5× by default) means “activity”: switch back to full frame rate immediately. The other way, after 60 seconds of continuous quiet, drop to sparse mode: one keyframe every 30 seconds, audio recording stops too.

Why a pre-buffer deserves its own design

The biggest risk of the “don’t write everything” route isn’t under-saving — it’s that the first frames of an activity aren’t on disk. The GOP pre-buffer is the insurance for exactly that risk: a 32MB ring buffer always holding the most recent complete GOP; at the spike, flush first, then switch to full rate:

The precedent family is the same idea: Blue Iris has pre-trigger buffer, Synology has 5–300s pre-record — the difference is the source. Theirs pre-decides what to record; this flush uses what happened to be in the buffer. Different constraints, no hierarchy.

What Sparse Mode Writes, and How It Plays Back

Unattended periods present as a compressed timeline: at merge time, dwell samples longer than 2 seconds compress to 0.1s frame spacing. A 122-second sparse segment becomes 0.4 seconds in the merged file — quiet stretches fast-forward, active stretches real-time, one file auto-speeding. Database rows keep the wall-clock timeline; the playback page’s day-timeline seek maps through timeline_map into the correct offset inside the product.

Synology’s Smart Time Lapse and Blue Iris’s Periodic mode do the same family of things: represent longer empty time with fewer bytes. MiBeeNvr folds it into the everyday recording stream rather than a separate feature switch.

pixgate: Pixel-Domain Gating

Why it’s needed

The compressed domain has a principled blind spot: rain on water, swaying leaves, a person walking — all real pixel motion, all enlarging P frames. The bitrate domain can’t tell “rain” from “person”. And a worse category: smart codecs flatten the bitrate so P-frame size simply decouples from motion — compressed-domain gating fails outright on those streams.

Hence the pixel-domain layer: sample, decode, and use classical computer vision to decide “is there blocky, persistent foreground”. The layer isn’t new — ZoneMinder’s zones, Shinobi’s regions, Blue Iris’s motion zones are all this idea.

The chain, and what each stage treats

StageWhat it treats
① Background model (EMA)“a person standing still gets misjudged as background after a few minutes” — slow-learning foreground stays recognized even after standing
② Adaptive diff thresholdnight-gain full-screen grain — the threshold follows the noise median instead of reading it as foreground
③ Illumination-step suppressionheadlights sweeping a wall, IR-cut flips — single steps suppressed outright; only “the light actually stays” rebuilds the background
④ Connected-component min areaseparating rain from people: rain is many small short-lived blobs; a person is one large persistent blob (a mid-distance person occupies ~2–8% of a 480p substream grid)
⑤ ROI masksky, water, streets — the “always flickering” regions, excluded by polygon
⑥ ghost absorptionstatic new foreground — lights turned on, parked cars, lens droplets — merges into background after 5 minutes and stops triggering; moving objects’ centroids keep drifting and are never absorbed
⑦ Persistence hysteresis2 consecutive activity samples to confirm, 3 consecutive quiet samples to release — single-frame noise can’t trigger

Cost and prerequisites, no hiding

  • Requires ffmpeg (the shared optional dependency); when absent the gate disables itself with a one-time warning, everything else unaffected
  • Requires a reachable substream (rtsp / onvif / gb28181 cameras; srt / rtmp push cameras excluded)
  • Cost: 480p-class substream at 1fps sampling, ~10–20ms/frame software decode per camera, under 2% of one core
  • During PTZ motion the gate deliberately goes blind to avoid motion false-positives; the background model rebuilds after the move
  • Results go on the event bus as pixgate.activity (with area_pct / centroid / flood / ghost fields) for UI and automation to subscribe; Prometheus exposes nvr_pixgate_samples_total / nvr_pixgate_triggers_total

It pairs by design with video_exit: false: with video exit disabled, video spikes never leave sparse mode — rain stays in the timelapse no matter how heavy, and only the “hard” signals (pixgate, audio, camera events, external API) restore full rate. Suited to “never raise the frame rate unless someone’s there” scenes like hallways and doorways.

Tiered Recording: The Fallback for Flat-Bitrate Cameras

For cameras whose bitrate is flattened, single-layer adaptive may never demote. Tiered recording walks on two legs:

  • The substream records 24/7 as a layer=1 baseline — 480p-class, 60-second segments, low bitrate, never misses
  • The main stream keeps adaptive density — near-empty scenes pair video_exit: false with pixgate so the main stream records events only, at full quality

Sub-layer segments live in the same directory tree with a sub_ filename prefix; merge_status='sublayer' is a terminal state that never participates in merging and follows normal retention cleanup. The recording-list API switches visibility with ?layer=1 / ?layer=0.

Blue Iris’s “substream continuous + main triggered” (direct-to-disc BVR, substream upsampled on playback) and Synology’s “advanced continuous recording” are the same family — Blue Iris users have measured 3.90GB → 120MB per hour. The difference is the trigger source: theirs relies on configured triggers; MiBeeNvr’s relies on compressed-domain/pixgate auto-detection, plus sub-layer segments can feed external AI directly.

The Noise-Engineering Trio

All three were forced out by real user cases:

ConfigWhat it treats
noise_floor_bytesWhen night encoders squeeze the bitrate to a few hundred bytes/frame, relative-multiple metrics get lit up by jitter — “the log says activity, the playback shows nothing moving”. An absolute byte floor: P frames below it are never spikes
auto_noise_floor (default on)Self-calibrates from timelapse dwell frames: frames in sparse mode are by construction static, so their p99 size ×1.25 is that camera’s own noise ceiling. Applies only to bitrate-starved streams; healthy streams unaffected
video_exit: falseResident timelapse: video spikes never exit sparse; only pixgate / audio / camera events / external API raise the frame rate. Treats rain, water surfaces, leaves

Explicit and self-calibrated floors take the larger of the two — the explicit value is the hard floor, self-calibration raises it on starved streams.

Activity Scores, the Heatmap Timeline, and Content-Based Cleanup

Every segment carries an activity score

The compressed-domain analyzer scores each recording segment (motion score), usable directly in the library: filter by “active / quiet / scene change”, set a minimum score, one-click color the detail-page timeline by score (green=quiet → red=active).

Since v0.13 the scorer has two anti-distortion designs:

  • Absolute-bitrate confidence: pure relative metrics fake high scores on bitrate-starved streams. Confidence anchors on the segment’s median P-frame bytes (~0 below 400B, full trust above ~1200B, linear in between); leaderboards and cleanup read “score × confidence”
  • Pixel-priority routing: on pixgate-enabled cameras with sufficient pixel-domain foreground coverage for a segment, the score is synthesized from the pixel domain instead (0.8 × foreground duty cycle + 0.2 × area magnitude) — immune to night-gain noise and encoder refresh frames

When the disk fills, delete the least useful first

The watermark defaults to 85%. On reaching it, the quietest segments by activity score go first — the same disk retains more “something happened” footage. Frigate’s retain ladder is “keep by class”; this is “rank deletion order by content within a class” — two directions of one idea.

eval-replay: tune parameters without touching production

The gating logic is a pure function of “P-frame size sequence + config”, so a finished recording can answer “what would the gate have done with these parameters” — no camera, no decoding:

corpus is a JSON array; label conventions are rain / lowbitrate / static / active, aggregated into per-label mean rows. Parameter mysticism becomes a regression test.

MiBeeVision: AI Where the Hardware Is

Architecture and data flow

The NVR listens for segment-completion events, checks Vision heartbeat health, and pushes segments to Vision in the window after they land and before they merge (push_mode: notify for consumer pull, or upload to push bytes).

Why push: the early Vision polled the NVR on a timer and 78% of its requests hit 404 — segments had already been merged or cleaned by the time it arrived. After switching to push, the video reaches Vision before the merge process touches it. On heartbeat timeout (default 60s) the NVR pauses pushing; on recovery, missed segments are automatically re-pushed with compensation — bounded: a 2-hour window, at most 500 segments, 200ms pacing, to keep the recovery burst from overwhelming consumer or disk.

Multiple instances and routing

vision.instances supports multiple consumers with independent addresses, identities, and start/stop; cameras pick which instances to feed via vision_targets (empty = all). Different instances can carry different models, routed per scene:

Feeding AI low-resolution: decode cost cut to 1/4–1/16

Two low-resolution channels (landed in v0.12.0, issues #512–#537): the substream analysis layer (sub_layer_cameras) records independent low-res analysis segments on demand — outside the recording library, never merged, deleted on successful consumption; tiered sub-layer segments (tiered_cameras) reuse the recording library’s layer=1 rows directly, with main-stream segments yielding. The 4K main-stream recording is untouched. For the consumer, judging a 480p clip is an order of magnitude cheaper than 4K.

How conclusions come back

Two routes, both ending in the same action — pulling the main stream back to full rate:

  1. Vision writes AI events back to the NVR (recording-list AI markers + events), visible directly in playback
  2. Any external system (Vision itself, home automation, your scripts) calls the external trigger API:
bash

curl -u admin:password -X POST \

hold is the full-rate hold duration; every new confirmation restarts the clock.

MiBeeVision Lite v0.1.0

Released 2026-10-18; the facts:

  • Positioning: MiBeeNvr’s AI detection component — object detection and tracking over NVR-recorded video, events by region / crossing / loitering rules, results written back to the NVR
  • Form: Linux x86_64 / aarch64 single binary, built-in YOLOv8n model + ONNX Runtime 1.17.1 (CPU); the Lite edition is single-camera, CPU inference
  • Deployment: default port 9092 with a web UI — enter the NVR address, paste the mbv_ API key, pick cameras, draw rule regions
  • Auth: API key only (mbv_ prefix); the web session token (mbs_) gets 401 on write-back
  • Transport: http_download when Vision and NVR are on different machines; local_file requires a shared recording directory
  • License: closed-source proprietary, copyright MiBee Studio, distributed free with MiBeeNVR; the bundled YOLOv8n is Ultralytics AGPL-3.0
  • Versioning: an independent vX.Y.Z line, evolving separately from MiBeeNVR

Lite is the first footprint on this road: one camera, CPU. It changes none of the NVR’s budget — it runs on another machine, and the NVR pays at most a little push bandwidth.

One paragraph each: where the effort went

  • Frigate: server-side AI event recording done about as well as anything in the class — detection-driven retention, the review UI, and deep Home Assistant integration are its moat. It wants Coral/GPU and Docker; that’s the price of the route, not a defect. On “AI verdict then auto-store the event”, MiBeeNvr is explicitly not its equal
  • Blue Iris: made “substream continuous + main triggered” a mature feature (direct-to-disc BVR + pre-trigger buffer), works out of the box on Windows with a rich trigger ecosystem. Its measured saving (3.90GB → 120MB per hour) is a persuasive community data point
  • Synology Surveillance Station: the NAS-native monitoring hub — advanced continuous recording, Dual Recording, Smart Time Lapse, edge recording, plus the package-license model. If you already own a Synology, it’s the lowest-friction path
  • ZoneMinder: one of the oldest open-source NVRs, a mature event system; its zone types (Active/Inclusive/Exclusive/Preclusive/Inactive) were the reference for the ROI mask. The LAMP stack strains low-spec devices — the price of its route
  • Shinobi: a Node.js stack with flexible event-driven recording (Trigger Record + Buffer Time) and per-tenant storage quotas as its specialty. Pure event recording is a different road on “continuity”

The role of camera-side smart codecs

H.265+ (Hikvision), Smart H.265+ (Dahua), Ultra H.265 (Uniview), Zipstream (Axis), WiseStream (Hanwha) cut baseline bitrate with background modeling, dynamic GOPs, and ROI coding; vendors claim another 20–40% over standard H.265 at equal quality. They complement NVR-side gating; neither replaces the other. Two pitfalls (detailed in From H.265 to AV1/AVS): smart codecs are proprietary extensions, not standards — across brands they usually degrade to standard H.265, so capacity planning shouldn’t count their savings; and flattened-bitrate smart codecs break all compressed-domain gating — any scheme that judges motion from bitrate variation is affected. Put those cameras straight on tiered recording + pixgate.
