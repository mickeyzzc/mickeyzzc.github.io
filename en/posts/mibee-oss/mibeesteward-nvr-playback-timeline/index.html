MiBeeNvr's Playback Timeline: No Stutter Between Segments, On-Demand Full Days

November 22, 2026 November 26, 2026 MiBeeOSS Playback, FMP4, Streaming, Open Source MiBee Projects Series 1857 words 9 min read
🔊

The annoying part of playback is not finding a recording — it is the hitch halfway through: one segment ends, the picture goes black, the progress bar jumps back to rebuffer, and then the next segment catches up. Fragmented recordings make it worse: a few seconds per segment, hundreds of switches in an evening, each one reloading from scratch. At high playback speeds the whole thing barely holds together as one continuous stream.

Full-day playback adds a second wall. To scrub across 24 hours on a single timeline, the usual approach is to merge the day’s clips into one large MP4 first and wait for the merge to finish before playing. Scrubbing across segment boundaries and across gaps with no footage is harder still.

Segment blackouts and a full-day timeline are two separate problems in long-recording playback. MiBeeNvr handles them separately: segment switches use double buffering, preloading the next segment into a standby <video> and adopting it the moment ended fires; the full-day timeline never generates a whole file at all, cutting fMP4 fragments on demand so hls.js can treat the day as one VOD stream to scrub through.

The moment a segment ends

Playback used to have a single <video>. When a segment ran out, ended fired, the frontend queried the next recording, swapped src, and waited for the browser to reload. The viewer paid for it: black flashes, reload flicker, a progress bar that jumped backwards. The more fragmented the recording and the higher the speed, the longer the wait. Nothing was broken on the backend — every segment sat intact on disk. What stuttered was the player switching sources.

Arming the next segment

The page now stacks two <video> elements in the same CSS grid cell: the active one plays the current segment (preload=metadata), while the hidden standby one (preload=auto) buffers the next. The trigger sits past the halfway point — the release notes describe it as 80%, but the implementation moves it up to 50%, because at 32x the last 20% of a segment is roughly one second, far too late to buffer the next one.

The standby counts as ready on canplay with readyState at 3. When the video ends, handleVideoEnded calls adoptStandby: it flips activeIsA, the old active element pauses and becomes the new standby, and the prepared element calls play() directly. No element is recreated and host state is replaced through oncrosssegment. Event handlers are target-guarded so only the active element drives UI state — a canplay or error from the standby does not move the progress bar. If the standby is not ready yet, the player waits briefly (4 seconds on the ended path, 8 on a manual switch) while the old frame stays on screen; only a timeout or error falls back to a hard src swap.

Metadata updates have their own rate limit. On fragmented cameras recordings can change several times a second, and rebuilding the metadata DOM on every hop reads as flicker, so those updates are clamped to once per second. Playback state itself still uses live data.

One axis for a whole day

Continuous mode no longer opens recordings one by one. The backend returns a VOD playlist (GET /api/cameras/{id}/playback/playlist.m3u8?start=&end=) in which each recording is a period: an #EXT-X-MAP in front of it points at its own init.mp4, #EXT-X-DISCONTINUITY separates periods, and #EXT-X-PROGRAM-DATE-TIME anchors the period to wall-clock time. The frontend gets one continuous media timeline and entryAt(mediaTime) tells it which recording the playhead is in. Scrubbing across a gap with no footage just advances time — no source switch, no reload.

That wall-clock anchor has a practical use too. Once a rolling merge replaces the recording being watched, its old ID no longer exists in a fresh playlist; the frontend finds the nearest period by wall clock and rebuilds the session instead of stalling on a 404.

Cutting fragments on demand

The fMP4 path produces no new file. To plan a cut it reads the source MP4’s sample table and aims for six seconds, landing on a keyframe — recordings start on an IDR by design, and later boundaries are the first keyframe at or after the target. Each fragment carries only a moof plus the mdat header; sample bytes are copied from the source by byte range with io.NewSectionReader, so the full video is never loaded into memory. The init segment (ftyp + moov) is emitted once per recording, and audio carries only the MSE-friendly AAC and Opus — G.711 is dropped rather than letting the browser reject the whole stream.

Keyframe locations come from the file’s own stss table when present. Without stss, the NAL headers are probed: a short linear scan finds the first boundary, then GOP duration predicts the next one and a six-byte read verifies it, with ±4 samples covering frame-rate jitter. Reading every sample would scan the whole media payload on a slow disk; this reads a few tens of KB.

The data_offset field in moof depends on the final box size, so the box tree is serialized twice — once to measure, once to fill in real offsets.

Keeping on-demand fast

On demand does not mean recomputing every time. A fragment plan is persisted on first computation as a .vodidx sidecar (version 2, validated against file size and mtime), so building a day’s playlist only needs a stat, a sidecar read, and rendering — sub-second even on a slow disk. The sample-table LRU keeps just 12 entries; real parsing happens when a fragment is actually requested, and hls.js fetches fragments sequentially, which the cache handles well.

The window is bounded deliberately: at most 48 hours per playlist, 200 recordings per request, and parse parallelism capped at four. A single failed recording is skipped with a warning instead of taking down the whole day; a 404 on a standby fragment rebuilds the session by wall clock, so a rolling merge replacing the file does not interrupt playback.

How others do it

Long-recording playback has several organization styles; they differ in what unit a recording is stored in and what unit it is retrieved by during playback.

Frigate (source: the Frigate record documentation) writes each recording straight from the camera stream as MP4 without re-encoding, laid out in UTC under YYYY-MM-DD/HH/<camera>/MM.SS.mp4 with a fixed segment length of 10 seconds. A new segment is first written to cache and only moved into the real directory once it passes validation and matches the retention policy. Retention is layered across continuous / motion / alert / detection, and whether a segment is deleted is decided by the largest matching retention value among all applicable rules; a clip you want to keep for longer is exported separately, and exports are never removed by retention. Its playback unit is “a collection of 10-second MP4 files”: the timeline lays the segments out by time, the player loads them one by one, and the seek granularity is those 10 seconds.

The cost of that choice is direct: 10 seconds is a convenient length for writing, not a keyframe boundary. A segment is cut at an arbitrary point in the camera stream — the player can decode it on its own, but continuity across the boundary depends on the camera’s own GOP. What it buys is a simple write side: storing, cleanup, and retention are all computed per file, with no index to maintain.

ZoneMinder (source: the ZoneMinder documentation) takes another route: recordings are tightly bound to events, stored and replayed by event, with a state machine maintained in MariaDB. Its playback unit is the “event clip” — you find an event first, then watch the recording attached to that event, rather than scrubbing freely on a continuous timeline by wall clock. That fits a query like “which day did someone walk past the door” and does not fit stitching together yesterday from two to three in the afternoon.

go2rtc (source: the go2rtc README) has a different job again: it is primarily a real-time streaming gateway aiming for low latency. Its HLS output supports fMP4 via the ?mp4 filter on a stream URL, and HLS on Apple devices can play H.265; yet go2rtc itself calls HLS the worst transport for live streaming, kept mainly for iPhone compatibility. It answers “how do I deliver this live stream to a browser now”, not “how do I scrub through a full day of recordings”.

MiBeeNvr does both at once: playback is one continuous timeline, while the segments are cut from the original MP4 by byte range only when they are requested.

What each path pays

Put the approaches side by side and the two hard problems of long-recording playback can be solved separately: segment blackouts by prefetch plus double buffering, and full-day timeline generation by on-demand slicing plus a timeline protocol. #EXT-X-PROGRAM-DATE-TIME is the pin that anchors media time back to wall-clock time — with it the timeline can express “which real-world moment this segment corresponds to”, and a gap with no footage is just time moving forward.

Fixed-length segmentation and on-demand fMP4 are two different roads.

Frigate’s 10-second MP4 leaves the complexity on the write side: no re-encoding, retention per file, new segments validated in cache before landing, and the player pulling segments one at a time on read. The cost is that segment boundaries do not line up with keyframes, the smallest rewind unit is those 10 seconds, and the timeline is a “collection of segments” rather than one continuous stream. For a system with long-lived recordings, layered retention, and event- or motion-first search, this is easy to operate: cleanup and accounting are done against files, with no extra index.

MiBeeNvr’s on-demand fMP4 leaves the complexity on the read side: writing only has to land the camera stream into MP4, while reading parses the sample table, cuts at keyframes, and assembles the init segment plus moof/mdat. What it buys is a timeline you can scrub anywhere and zero pre-generation cost — no slice files are produced ahead of time. The cost is maintaining the sidecar index and sample-table parsing yourself, plus handling cases like a rolling merge replacing the file and invalidating an old ID.

Each road has its own scale. Fixed-length segmentation suits systems with long retention, event- or motion-first lookup, and frequent per-segment deletion; on-demand fMP4 suits systems with shorter retention, continuous-timeline review as the main mode, and no appetite for a second copy of slice storage. MiBeeNvr takes the latter, consistent with its overall shape of targeting home cameras, zero external dependencies, and no separate slicing service.
