MiBeeNvr's Recording Deep-Check and Full-Link Observability

October 19, 2026 October 18, 2026 MiBeeOSS NVR, Go, Recording Integrity, Observability, Deep Check, Open Source MiBee Open Source Projects Series 2136 words 11 min read
🔊

The nastiest failures in a surveillance pipeline are the ones that never raise an error. A frozen live tile could be a dropped camera, network jitter, or the NVR itself — before v0.12, the available diagnostic tools were reading logs and counting frames. The recording side was worse off: ingest, muxing, and writes all reported success, yet playback would corrupt from some point onward, and the write path never said a word about it.

v0.12 gave MiBeeNvr two layers of instrumentation. On the recording side, the deep-check: decode sampled on-disk recordings in the background and take the decoder’s verdict as the verdict. On the live side, full-link observability: stream views, a diagnostic tree, latency badges, three-state liveness, frame traces. The first problems the deep-check caught were in the NVR itself — three classes of silent on-disk damage — and this post takes those apart along with the design of both layers. Mechanisms are based on the v0.12.0 release records; the version-by-version timeline is in MiBeeNvr: From v0.1.0 to v0.12.0.

A successful write is not a playable recording

Every stage of the recording pipeline has its own success semantics: RTSP delivered a frame, the muxer wrote a file, write(2) returned, the file is growing. But that self-consistency proves nothing about playability — playability depends on the integrity of the reference chain: P frames reference the frames before them, and a disordered timestamp, a duplicated POC, or a segment that doesn’t start at an IDR will each make the decoder emit corruption from some point on, while the write side remains oblivious.

“A write succeeded” and “this plays back” are two different claims. The only authority for the second one is the decoder: actually decode the file that landed on disk and see what it reports. The deep-check automates exactly that layer of verification:

  • At most one segment per camera per hour is sampled and decoded — the frequency cap is fixed, so the CPU budget is bounded
  • An ok result only increments a counter; no log line — normal is the norm, and the norm doesn’t get to spam logs
  • stderr is the verdict, but it has to be classified: benign dts warnings are filtered out; reference-frame-loss (POC) errors are what count as damage
  • A segment that happens to be consumed by the rolling merge at sampling time is listed separately as a “vanishing race” — not an error

This animation is the full pipeline: one segment is picked from the hour-spaced segments on disk, the background decoder verifies it frame by frame, and the stderr output goes through classification — benign warnings are dropped, reference-frame-loss errors are counted and alarmed. Sampling rather than full coverage is the trade between detection latency and CPU budget: damage goes from “playback runs into it” to “visible at hourly granularity”.

Three classes of silent on-disk damage

The deep-check was built as an observability feature — and the first things it caught were the NVR’s own bugs. All three classes below came from real machines in long-running tests, and none of them errors at write time. Before the deep-check, their shared symptom was “occasional playback glitches”, and localization meant scrubbing through footage by eye.

Timestamp collapse

Recording timestamps used to be taken from the consumption instant — the moment the recorder pulled a frame out of the buffer. Under normal load this approximation is fine: consumption keeps up with arrival, the two instants nearly coincide, and nothing ever went wrong with it.

What breaks it is a backlog draining in a burst. The upstream hiccups for any reason (network, camera, GC); frames pile up in the buffer; when it recovers, the recorder consumes the entire backlog in one go — and that batch’s consumption instants all squeeze onto the same point. The written timestamps go from “faithfully reflecting arrival spacing” to “fine for a stretch, then collapsed onto a single value”, and the decoder’s reference chain breaks from the collapse point onward.

The fix: anchor timestamps to frame arrival time. The arrival instant is fixed the moment a frame enters the NVR and is immune to any downstream jitter — fast or slow consumption, deep or shallow backlogs can no longer rewrite the timeline.

Duplicate writes on adaptive exit

When adaptive recording exits sparse mode back to full rate, it flushes the in-memory GOP buffer to disk — that is how “recording starts from the moment activity began” is implemented. But the keyframes in that buffer had already been written during sparse mode; the flush didn’t check for that and wrote them again. The same POC appears twice, the decoder treats it as a broken reference chain, and playback corrupts around the exit point.

The fix: reconcile before flushing — frames already on disk are not written again.

Merge-segment misalignment

When the rolling merge stitches small segments into larger ones, the output segments did not have to start at an IDR. A segment that starts mid-chain, at a P frame, forces the decoder to conjure up the missing reference frames; when it can’t, that stretch corrupts. The damage hides at merge boundaries: each source segment is individually fine, only the combined output is bad — another illusion of write-path self-consistency.

The fix: merged output is forced to start every segment at an IDR; the split point moves back to the nearest preceding keyframe.

Verification after the fixes

After the three fixes shipped, deep-check decode errors on the same deployment went to zero. Without the deep-check, the before/after difference was only ever distinguishable as “playback glitches feel less frequent”.

The live side: full-link observability

Recording problems stay hidden until playback; live problems are visible on the spot — but between “I can see it stutter” and “I know where it stutters” lies an entire instrumentation layer. v0.12 instrumented the stream path with one design goal: when any hop misbehaves, the data says which hop.

Three-state liveness

Every stream entry carries a last_frame_age_s: how many seconds since the last frame arrived. It has three shapes, matching three realities:

  • Sub-second: online, frames keep arriving
  • null: this camera has never produced a stream since startup — wrong config, wrong credentials, unreachable address; the failure is at setup time
  • Monotonically growing: was online, now stalled — the value gains one per second and counts exactly how long it has been down

Combined with the frame counter, the three states separate “camera died” from “NVR stuck”: a growing age, a frozen counter, and other cameras healthy mean the problem is on the camera or network side; every camera frozen at once means check the NVR and the host first. The decision needs no extra probes — the data already sits on the stream path.

Stream views and the diagnostic tree

/api/streams outputs statistics for every active stream: per camera, from source to each protocol egress — frame counts, byte counts, drops, consumer counts, main and sub streams tallied separately; /api/cameras/{id}/flow aggregates per camera. Beyond frame counts there are E1–E4 observability counters: frames sent, drops, 15-second step growth, and per-hop residence — how long a frame lingered at each hop from camera to egress.

The dashboard aggregates these into a per-camera diagnostic tree: the path from source to every egress drawn as a tree with status coloring, sub-stream branches carrying differential fps/kbps and consumer types. Troubleshooting goes from “guess from logs” to “find the red node in the tree”:

Latency badges

Every tile in the grid shows a latency badge. Values are tiered by protocol, and approximations are not dressed up as precise:

  • FLV: computed exactly from the first-frame timestamp, end to end
  • HLS / WebRTC: estimated from segments/statistics
  • Player-reported: POST /api/telemetry — the real end-to-end number comes from the player itself; if you want precision, measure it yourself

Frame traces

Per-camera sampling windows and injection-point breadcrumbs: a frame entering the NVR from the camera, passing every hop, reaching the egress — each stage’s timing is recorded. The latency badge says “this stream is two seconds slow”; the frame trace answers “slow at which hop”.

Boundaries and trade-offs

  • Sampling is not full coverage. Hourly sampling means damage that lasts minutes is, at worst, reported at the next sample — it turns damage from “playback runs into it” into “visible at hourly granularity”, not a real-time defense. Full decode doesn’t fit the 1GB design budget.
  • stderr verdicts need classification. Most dts warnings in a decoder’s output are benign; believing all of them turns noise into damage. The classification filter directly determines the deep-check’s false-positive rate.
  • Approximations are labeled as approximations. HLS/WebRTC latencies are estimates; the precise end-to-end path is player reporting.
  • Observability itself has a budget. Counters live on the data path (accumulated while processing frames), decode sampling runs in the background at a capped rate, ok results don’t log — once instrumentation exceeds its budget, it becomes the next thing you need to observe.
