MiBeeNvr v0.12.0: Sub-streams, Adaptive Recording, and Full-path Observability

September 1, 2026 September 1, 2026 MiBeeOSS NVR, Go, Sub-Stream, RTSP, Adaptive Recording, Observability, GB28181, Open Source MiBee Open Source Projects Series 3819 words 18 min read
🔊

MiBeeNvr v0.12.0 is out. This release goes after two everyday annoyances: watching the cameras on a phone, where a thumbnail-sized preview quietly pulls the full-resolution video underneath — eating data and heating the phone; and corridor or doorway cameras writing tens of GB a day that nobody ever replays.

Less disk

  • When nothing moves, the camera records less; the moment anything moves, it snaps back to full recording without dropping a frame. Quiet scenes measured at roughly 3/4 less disk
  • A louder sound, a doorbell, or a sensor signal also kicks it back to full recording
  • The replay timeline carries a green-to-red heat strip — the segments where something happened are visible at a glance
  • When the disk nears full, the quietest recordings are deleted first
  • Phone viewing defaults to a small, smooth stream: no stutter, no heat; one tap switches to HD for detail
  • When nobody is watching, that preview stream disconnects by itself — no camera connection or bandwidth tied up

More freedom

  • Each camera can be assigned its own disk
  • Switching disks needs no restart; old recordings move to the new disk in the background, optionally only at night, never interrupting recording

Easier to integrate

  • Every camera gets a standard playback URL that Synology Surveillance Station and similar monitoring software can just fill in
  • Phones, PCs, and OBS can push video straight into the NVR — an ad-hoc camera position takes a minute
  • fnOS users click into the NVR from the desktop without a second login, and installing no longer means typing commands
  • Cameras connected through Hikvision- or Dahua-style gear: the recordings on their SD cards can be downloaded and replayed

Easier to troubleshoot

  • Latency and the status of every picture are visible right on the live page
  • You can tell a dead camera apart from a stuck NVR
  • Recordings get automatic health checks — problems surface early, not when you hit them in replay
  • When a slower device can’t keep up with HD, it switches to a lighter display mode instead of showing a corrupted picture

The disk you didn’t write is the only disk you really saved.

The above is the user’s-eye view. What follows is principles and implementation detail, for consulting config keys during deployment and for locating implementations when reading the source.

Across v0.9.0 through v0.11.0, MiBeeNvr assembled a complete ingest-record-distribute chain: v0.9.0 paid off the accumulated engineering debt, v0.10.0 became the stable release — H.265 WASM live view, multi-NAS distribution, the AI false-positive fix — and v0.11.0 let Hikvision and Dahua SIP cameras register directly over GB/T 28181 while playback gained the full-day timeline. By that version, four source families (ONVIF, RTSP, Xiaomi, GB/T 28181), three codecs (H.264, H.265, MJPEG), three live paths (HLS, HTTP-FLV, WebRTC), and three deployment targets (bare-metal, Docker, fnOS) were all in place.

The system carried two implicit assumptions: live distribution consumed only the main stream, and recording ran in a single continuous mode. Preview users paid for the first — tiles a few centimeters wide pulled full-resolution streams, spending bandwidth, decode, and phone thermals on invisible pixels; the disk paid for the second — corridors, doorways, and garages wrote at full rate through hours in which nothing happened. v0.12.0 removes the two assumptions with the sub-stream system and adaptive recording, and fills two gaps that had been there all along: third-party platforms that only pull RTSP had no URL to fill in (built-in RTSP output), and the diagnostic toolkit was reading logs and counting frames (full-path observability).

The sub-stream system

Cameras generally emit a main stream (full resolution) and a sub-stream (a few hundred lines). Live distribution previously consumed only the main stream — the right choice for recording, pure waste for previews, grids, and cascade forwarding, where bandwidth and decoding are spent on pixels that end up a few centimeters wide.

v0.12.0 makes the sub-stream a first-class input across five dimensions:

Discovery and configuration. ONVIF cameras need zero configuration: device discovery records the second profile’s token as it goes (fill-once; clearing it re-triggers discovery). Non-ONVIF cameras, or ones where discovery comes up empty, accept a manual RTSP URL in the form.

On-demand pulling. The sub-stream is not permanently held; it is reference-counted: an RTSP pull exists while anyone is watching and is torn down 30 seconds (configurable) after the last viewer leaves. When nobody watches, the stream does not exist.

This is the sub-stream’s full lifecycle: every quality=sub request increments the refcount, the first viewer triggers the RTSP pull, and all viewers then fan out from one hub; after the refcount hits zero, the idle timer disconnects the camera. The lifecycle is invisible to the protocol endpoints above it.

Quality switching. The surveillance grid defaults to the sub-stream with a one-click HD toggle in the header, remembered per browser; the per-camera page has an independent quality switcher per camera, and the live protocol follows automatically.

Cascade forwarding. When cascading to an upper GB28181 platform, each camera in the catalog can opt into sub-stream forwarding: invitations are answered with the low-resolution sub-stream instead of the main one, saving upstream bandwidth and decode; if the sub-stream cannot be pulled, it falls back to the main stream rather than going dark.

Analysis layer. Designated cameras can be set to low-res dual recording: the NVR records their sub-stream as short-retention segments pushed to an external AI backend (e.g. MiBeeVision) while the 4K main recording is untouched; consumers report undecodable cameras via heartbeat and they join a skip list.

Two boundaries: the sub-stream depends on the camera actually providing one — cameras whose profile discovery or DESCRIBE yields no video track fall back to the main stream with unchanged behavior; and WebRTC carries H.264 only, so H.265 sub-stream requests over WebRTC fall back to the main stream with the actual result flagged in a response header.

Built-in RTSP output

The external distribution endpoints were previously the three web paths — HLS, HTTP-FLV, WebRTC — plus RTMP/SRT forwarding configured per camera. For third-party systems whose only ingest method is pulling an RTSP URL — Synology Surveillance Station, SCADA glue, legacy decode-and-wall setups — there was no address to fill in.

v0.12.0 ships a built-in RTSP server, on by default, one URL per camera:

1

rtsp://<NVR-IP>:8554/<camera_id>

Any RTSP-capable software treats it as an ordinary camera source. H.264/H.265 pass through natively with no transcoding; credentials are optional with an open-LAN default; a port conflict logs an error and nothing else. Docker deployments must publish 8554 explicitly.

Adaptive recording

The motivation came out of a 48-hour soak test: a corridor camera writes tens of GB a day, the overwhelming majority of which nobody ever replays. Under continuous recording, disks pay full rate for static pixels.

v0.12.0 adds an “adaptive (motion-aware)” recording mode alongside “continuous” (recording_mode: adaptive; the default remains continuous and it is an explicit opt-in): an activity score is computed in the compressed domain — per-P-frame size deviation against a rolling baseline, no pixel decoding, negligible CPU — with keyframes-only while quiet and a seamless return to full rate on activity. The score only says the picture changed — a person, a car, or shifting light all count alike; deciding whether it was a person is the external AI backend’s job.

This is the gating in full: the score must stay under baseline for a sustained window (60 seconds by default) before the camera demotes to sparse, so one passing car doesn’t; during sparse mode a single encoder-noise spike (static H.265 scenes emit large P-frames at random) does not reset the calm accumulator, while a burst within 2 seconds does; the moment real activity is detected, the retained GOP buffer is flushed whole, so full-rate recording starts at the instant the activity began with no gap at the transition. Sound and external triggers share the same exit path.

Three supplementary inputs to the gating:

  • Audio trigger. G.711 tracks are scored per second (dBFS); crossing the threshold exits sparse mode immediately, and the pre-trigger 3 seconds (configurable) of the audio ring are backfilled into the exit segment. The threshold defaults to -45 dBFS and should be raised for rooms with steady ambient noise — a machine room, for instance.
  • External trigger. POST /api/cameras/{id}/adaptive/trigger with a source and hold duration; any external system — access control, sensors — can pull a camera back to full rate.
  • Activity heat. Every recording carries its activity score, rendered as block color on the recording-detail timeline (green→red; blue for unanalyzed, orange for MJPEG). “Did anything happen around three-ish this afternoon” stops being a blind scrub.

Measured on a static-scene camera: sparse segments land 5.6 MB per 248 seconds against roughly 190 MB at full rate; that camera’s hourly average settled at 688 MB against a 2700 MB full-rate baseline — a 75% reduction. Periods with activity behave identically to continuous mode, and playback stays continuous.

Disk-watermark cleanup is motion-aware as well: segments with near-zero activity scores are evicted first and active ones last, with “keep N days” semantics unchanged. On by default.

Sparse segments are compressed at the merge layer: long-dwell samples collapse to 100 ms frame spacing (100/300/500 selectable), so normal-speed playback yields a natural timelapse and downloaded files behave the same; an optional ambient layer keeps continuous low-bitrate audio in sparse mode and mixes it back by envelope at merge. One default change: audio tracks inside recordings are now off by default (per-camera switch); live view and audio triggering are unaffected.

Per-camera disks

Multi-disk machines previously had exactly one recording root for the whole box — a large HDD and write-heavy cameras sharing one bottleneck. v0.12.0 allows a per-camera recording root: switching is hot (new segments land on the new disk immediately), and history is relocated by a background migrator — rate-limited (15 MB/s default), windowable, verifying each file before deleting its source, never interrupting recording. Root candidates can be added and removed at runtime without a restart. The database has long been decoupled from the recording root, so switching disks cannot strand it; the setup wizard rejects unusable paths with a clear error, and containers fall back to the data volume instead of crash-looping.

fnOS desktop

  • Login-free access. Signed-in fnOS desktop users enter the NVR through the unified-gateway SSO without a second login; base-path deployments and unix-socket gateways are supported.
  • Multi-volume recordings. Authorized directories mount as candidate volumes, which pairs with per-camera disks from the previous section for planning storage across drives.
  • web-install.sh. Drives the App-Center manual-install HTTP path — installing no longer requires SSH.

Ingest and GB28181

  • WHIP push ingest. Browsers, phones, and OBS push WebRTC straight into the NVR as a camera (H.264 + Opus) — an ad-hoc phone camera no longer needs an RTSP server behind it.
  • Bidirectional H.265 RTMP. Standard RTMP carries H.264 only; the enhanced-RTMP hvc1 variant now works inbound and outbound, so H.265 cameras can be forwarded as-is to platforms that support it.
  • Device-side recording download. Recordings on an enrolled device’s SD card can be searched and downloaded for playback (INVITE s=Download sessions), with seeking.
  • Device management commands. Reboot, factory reset, format, config backup/restore via DeviceControl; FI lens control and auxiliary switches.
  • Alarms and mobile position. Device alarm and mobile-position subscriptions have their own web panels.
  • Cascade v2. As a lower platform, audio can go upstream, multiple uppers are supported, and catalog changes push NOTIFY; per-camera catalog exposure gating hides chosen cameras entirely (hidden cameras answer invitations with 404).
  • GB sub-channel probing. Sub-channels of enrolled devices are probed by the Hikvision channel-number offset convention, so GB28181 devices can serve sub-streams too.
  • Cascade media defaults to TCP. The same 48-hour soak also measured GB28181 UDP pulling: ~16% frame loss — IDR-sized bursts overflow the NIC/softirq path, and every loss breaks the P-frame reference chain into macroblock corruption. The default is now tcp-passive; explicit udp configurations are unaffected.
  • Same-IP dual protocol. A device that is both ONVIF and GB28181 no longer has its auto-enrollment suppressed by IP deduplication (allow_same_ip_enroll).

Full-path observability

When a stream stuttered, the available diagnostics were reading logs and counting frames. v0.12.0 instruments the path:

  • Flow view: per-camera frame counts, bytes, drops, and consumer counts from source to each protocol egress, main and sub separated
  • Flow diagnosis tree: the dashboard’s per-camera tree with status coloring, including the sub-stream branch (differential fps/kbps, consumer type)
  • Latency badge: exact end-to-end latency for FLV, close approximations for HLS/WebRTC, visible on the grid
  • Three-state stream health: last_frame_age_s distinguishes online (sub-second), never-streamed (null), and broken (monotonically growing)
  • Recording audits: a background job decode-checks a sample of landed recordings hourly; missing-reference damage counts and alerts instead of waiting for a user to hit it
  • Frame tracing: per-camera sampling windows and producer-ingest breadcrumbs — every hop a frame takes has numbers

The observability manual (zh/en) landed in the repo’s docs/, covering everything from reading the flow view to wiring Grafana dashboards.

Sub-stream implementation notes

  • Target resolution: the manual sub_stream_url wins; otherwise the ONVIF sub_profile_token drives a GetStreamUri call. The token is fill-once, cleared to re-discover. Codec detection never trusts ONVIF profile metadata — one camera in the field advertises H.264 and sends H.265 — and always defers to the RTSP DESCRIBE SDP; SDPs without sprop parameter sets are accepted, with the sets backfilled from in-band data.
  • Lifecycle: configured under server.substream, idle_timeout_s default 30, ready_timeout_s default 8. Entries register under the camID/sub key into the WS/FLV/HLS distribution managers, reused unchanged; targets with no video track self-recycle as permanently failed.
  • Quality negotiation and fallback reporting: when the sub-stream cannot be pulled, endpoints serve the main stream and report the actual result in the X-Stream-Quality header; WebSocket’s 101 upgrade response is not readable from the browser API, so the negotiated result arrives as the first in-band message after connect. HLS segments cannot carry query parameters, so the sub-stream path is /stream/sub/index.m3u8.
  • Camera address changes: the target is resolved once at pull time; after a camera changes address (DHCP drift, a reboot picking up a new IP), the retry loop periodically re-runs the resolver after repeated failures and follows the new address — no restart or reconfiguration needed.
  • Cascade sub-stream: per-camera cascade_sub_stream toggle (API + form); the INVITE session holds a sub-stream reference, released on BYE; the sub-stream codec is sniffed independently rather than trusted from the main-stream hint, with main-stream fallback on failure.

RTSP output implementation notes

  • Configuration under server.rtsp: enabled defaults to true, port 8554, optional username/password. It shares the same hub source as FLV/HLS with zero-copy frame reuse; SPS/PPS changes hot-reload, and a camera reconnect rebuilds the stream.
  • Each pull client gets its own ServerStream and GOP-cache replay (bounded by both frame count and bytes): a mid-GOP joiner starts from the previous keyframe instead of consuming reference-broken P-frames. Sequence numbers of shared packets are re-sequenced per reader and SSRCs are unique per stream, avoiding seq jumps and SSRC collisions across concurrent readers.

Adaptive recording implementation notes

  • Activity scoring: MAD-floored P-frame size deviation against a rolling baseline, with an adjustable spike_factor (field-calibrated — genuinely moving scenes like a rooftop cloud-scape need a higher factor to ever go sparse). calm_threshold defaults to 60 seconds, timelapse_interval to 30.
  • Audio trigger: per-camera audio_trigger {enabled, min_dbfs (-90..0, default -45), pre_capture_s (0..30, default 3)}, effective only in adaptive mode. G.711 (µ/A-law) decodes via a pure-Go lookup table, CGO still zero; AAC/Opus have no pure-Go decoder yet and are skipped with a warning.
  • External trigger: POST /api/cameras/{id}/adaptive/trigger with source, hold, and dbfs in the body; semantic classification is not done in the NVR.
  • Merge layer: timelapse_frame_ms configurable 100/300/500; output rows carry the wall-clock-to-file mapping (timeline_map column) that the SPA’s timeline seek goes through; optional ambient_audio keeps a continuous low-rate track mixed back by RMS envelope (about -16 dBFS).
  • Recording audio: per-camera switch, default false — recordings land video-only by default.

Observability implementation notes

  • /api/streams reports hub statistics and E1-E4 counters for every active stream (frames sent, drop counts, 15-second step growth, per-hop dwell); /api/cameras/{id}/flow aggregates per camera and, since v0.12.0, includes the sub-stream and recording branches.
  • last_frame_age_s three-state semantics: online cameras read sub-second; entries that never streamed read null; broken cameras grow monotonically — combined with frozen frame counters, “camera down” separates from “NVR stuck”.
  • Recording audits run at most once per camera per hour; ok results go to counters only, with benign dts warnings filtered by stderr classification, and only missing-reference (POC-class) errors count as damage. Vanished-segment races (segments consumed by the rolling merger) are tallied separately and do not count.
  • Latency badge data: FLV computed exactly from first-frame timestamps; HLS/WebRTC approximated from segmentation statistics. Players can report true end-to-end numbers via POST /api/telemetry.

Recording integrity: three on-disk damage classes the audits caught first

The recording audit was built as an observability feature; it ended up catching the NVR’s own problems first. Three classes of on-disk damage, all reproduced on long-running field deployments, went from “occasional weirdness on replay” to counted alarms through the decode-level check — none of them raised an error at write time.

  • Timestamp collapse: recording timestamps were previously taken at consumption time. Under normal load, consumption time and frame arrival time nearly coincide, and the approximation never bit; when a backlog burst drained, a batch of frames was consumed in an instant and their timestamps collapsed onto one point, with the reference chain breaking from there.
  • Timelapse-exit double-write: when adaptive mode flushed the GOP buffer on its way back to full rate, keyframes already on disk were written again, producing duplicate POCs.
  • Unaligned merge cuts: rolling-merge output did not force each cut to start at an IDR, so splice points could show macroblock corruption.

The fixes map one-to-one: timestamps now anchor to frame arrival time; the flush skips frames already on disk; merged output is keyframe-aligned at every cut. After the fixes, decode-level audit errors on the same deployment read zero.

The takeaway: recording-pipeline correctness has to be judged by a decoder, not by the write path’s own bookkeeping — “written successfully” and “plays back” are two different claims, and the audit is the layer that verifies the second one.

Live-path stability fixes

  • Slow-client FLV lockup: one client that read a few KB and hung could wedge a camera’s entire FLV pipeline — all writes happened inside the viewer lock, and a half-closed client’s full kernel buffer blocked indefinitely, cascading into every other request and the frame distribution. The lock now covers only admission; writes happen outside it with a 10-second write timeout.
  • FLV black screens: an infinite destroy/rebuild churn in the player and a protocol-switch wedge — two lifecycle state-machine fixes.
  • wasm H.265 overload: when low-power devices cannot keep up, the old player rendered broken reference frames from the backlog (half-screen green/white); it now degrades honestly to a keyframe mode — a stable per-GOP still instead of corruption.
  • Ingest detection: keyframe detection on FLV/push ingest only checked the AU’s first NALU, and multi-slice pictures were being split — two source-side damage classes fixed.
  • Xiaomi cameras: quality-fallback semantics corrected — an SD downgrade now recovers to HD in a bounded way instead of being pinned; recording audio gating got its own switch.
  • Forwarding and legacy devices: relay targets faking a live state now fall under a stall watchdog; the 10-20 second disconnect loop on legacy TUTK cameras traced to a stale handshake write deadline, cleared.
  • Misc: archived cameras can finally have their archived bit properly cleared, with an archived parameter on the list API; login-free loopback access is configurable (auth.local_bypass, default off — enable only on bare-metal installs, and keep it off behind reverse proxies or published Docker ports, where every remote request would arrive via 127.0.0.1 and be let in). The ONVIF client library moved to the v2 service-facade line and the GB28181 stack was extracted into the standalone gb28181-go library (~17k lines net-deleted from the NVR, which keeps only its assembly layer), with test coverage backfilled package by package.

Upgrading

  • New configuration is safe by default: on-demand sub-streams have zero impact on unconfigured cameras; adaptive recording is an explicitly chosen mode, and unopened cameras behave exactly as on v0.11.0.
  • Two default changes: audio inside recordings is off by default (per-camera switch, enable where wanted); GB28181 media_transport defaults to tcp-passive (explicit udp configurations unaffected).
  • The RTSP output server is on by default (port 8554); disable it explicitly if unwanted, and publish the port on Docker.
  • The database migrates incrementally and automatically on first start (the recordings table gains motion_score, activity_flags, and timeline_map); back up nvr.db and mibee-nvr.yaml before upgrading, and hit /api/health after swapping the binary.

Deferred and limitations

  • WebRTC carries H.264 only: H.265 sub-stream requests over WebRTC fall back to the main stream (H.265 WebRTC support is left to browser-side adoption).
  • Audio triggering supports G.711 tracks only; AAC/Opus cameras (some Xiaomi models) do not trigger.
  • Adaptive recording relies on compressed-domain differential signals and works on H.264/H.265 cameras only, not MJPEG.
  • Sub-streams depend on the camera providing one: no second profile or no video track on DESCRIBE means main-stream fallback.
  • Cascade sub-stream forwarding depends on the upper platform accepting non-main resolutions; tested uppers behave, long-tail compatibility under observation.

On multi-disk machines, consider isolating high-bitrate cameras onto their own disk; for low-activity cameras in corridors and doorways, switch the recording mode to adaptive, let it run for a few days, then calibrate thresholds against the activity heat and the storage curve — measured on a real deployment, that class of camera drops to a quarter of its disk usage.
