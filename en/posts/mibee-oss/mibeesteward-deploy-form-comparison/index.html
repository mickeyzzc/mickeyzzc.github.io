Where Should MiBeeSteward Live: Router Form vs Host Form, a Controlled Comparison

September 18, 2026 September 18, 2026 MiBeeOSS Go, OpenWrt, Soft Router, Passive Discovery, Device Discovery, Open Source MiBee Open Source Projects Series 1788 words 9 min read
🔊

MiBee Steward is an open-source network device management system (a single Go binary handling automatic discovery, identification, and the ledger of LAN devices) with three deployment shapes: directly on the router, on an ordinary host in the network, or one center with several lightweight agents covering multiple subnets. This time, on one physical network, all three shapes were compared under the same version and parameters — environment, process, and data laid out to answer “where should it live”.

Conclusions first: scanning capability and identification quality are shape-independent — under the same version and parameters, all three shapes produce identical scan durations and verdicts; what actually separates them is passive discovery and cross-subnet capability, and that is decided by the device’s position in the network.

Test environment

The workshop runs two /24 subnets. The primary one holds the everyday devices: phones, laptops, cameras, Mi Home gear, dev boards — thirty-odd in total; the second is quieter, holding a NAS and a few always-on mini servers. A dual-arm RK3568 soft router (4GB) interconnects the two — it holds one address in each subnet, acting both as the inter-subnet router and as an L2 member that sees both sides. Text and screenshots below are sanitized (documentation IP ranges, generic device names).

The same network happens to hold all three shapes at once:

  • ① the center on the router, spanning both subnets natively;
  • ② the center on an ARM mini server (4GB) in the second subnet, seeing only its own segment;
  • ③ the same mini server as center, with an armv7 dev board in the primary subnet running the agent.

① and ② form the first two comparisons, “router vs host”; ③ is the project’s distributed shape (design doc), as the third group.

Method

  • One source tree, one version, two artifacts: the OpenWrt ipk installed on the soft router, the bare binary under systemd on the mini server. Both ARM64.
  • Both databases wiped; accumulation starts from zero.
  • No artificial leveling of discovery sources: the soft router enables what a router naturally has — DHCP leases, conntrack; the mini server enables ARP cache, multicast listening, and SNMP polling of the gateway’s ARP table. The comparison is each shape’s natural performance.
  • Identical scan parameters (full pipeline, /24 range, 50 concurrency), triggered in the same second.

Order: 8 minutes of purely passive observation (no scans triggered) first, then the full scan, then resource sampling.

Passive discovery: 8 minutes, 42 vs 34

After the wipe and restart, doing nothing, here is the count when the eight-minute passive window closed:

The router’s extra 8 devices come mainly from the DHCP lease table. As the subnet’s DHCP authority, every device that ever took a lease left a record — including dormant IoT devices that answer no active probe at all. Cloud-bound Mi Home devices are the typical case: local channels all closed, no mDNS replies, no open TCP ports, but the DHCP handshake must happen — and the lease hostname (viomi-waterheater-xxx) plus the hostname fingerprint library files these devices with brand and type attached. conntrack adds the real-time view of “who is talking”.

The mini server has no lease table and rolled out its 34 via two paths: recursive ARP-cache expansion (each newly discovered IP triggers an identification scan, and the scan traffic makes the peer’s ARP entry flow back), plus the mDNS/SSDP multicast anyone on the shared L2 can hear. The host shape’s passive discovery is adequate, but it has a ceiling — lease authority and the NAT panorama are physically out of reach.

Active scanning: 107.667 s vs 107.640 s

Full scans triggered simultaneously:

MetricSoft routerMini server
Full scan duration107.667 s107.640 s
Liveness verdicts3535

Twenty-seven milliseconds apart — scheduler noise; liveness verdicts identical to the device. Scan duration is decided by the probe-timeout structure, not compute, so two differently powered machines produced the same answer.

Identification quality aligns too: on the 35 shared devices, type verdicts agree 100%. The only brand difference was one NAS — the mini server happened to catch its SSDP SERVER header and recognized one extra software brand, an accident of passive evidence’s timing window.

After the scan, both ledgers merged cleanly: the mini server’s 34 were all re-covered by scanning, with no blind spots of their own; 7 of the router’s 42 were unreachable — all of them devices on the other subnet.

Cross-subnet: the router’s bonus and its price

Those 7 came from conntrack. The soft router is the route between the two segments, and cross-subnet forwarding entries are laid out in front of it — the router form sees its own subnet fully and sees across subnets too; that is the structural advantage of position.

The price surfaced on the spot: those 7 foreign-subnet devices went straight into this subnet’s list. The passive paths (DHCP, conntrack) currently do no subnet-membership filtering — whatever is passively observed gets filed, whichever segment it belongs to. The issue compounds in the third group: of the router center’s 49-device ledger, 14 primary-subnet devices were recorded under the second subnet. Worth watching in multi-subnet deployments; a fix is on the plan.

Group three: center + agent

The agent is cheap to deploy: a bare binary around 20MB, started by systemd, configured with the center’s address and a token. It scans the primary subnet with the exact same pipeline as the center — one /24 round took about 110 seconds, aligning with the two ARM64 machines’ 107.6 s again, this time with a different instruction set (armv7).

Agent and center talk over a pull model; one full scan round looks like this:

The chain has two practical properties: no inbound ports needed on the router, and an agent behind NAT works as usual; when the network is unchanged, a report just refreshes one round of leases at negligible cost. The primary subnet’s 33 devices entered the ledger through the agent, sharing the same MAC-keyed asset system as the center’s local scans.

The last row is the dividing line between the two shapes. The center’s heartbeats skip the agent network (cross-subnet probes would time out anyway), so liveness in the agent’s segment comes from leases refreshed by scan reports — the scan cadence must beat the lease TTL; that is the parameter to get right at deployment. This experiment failed exactly there: a 6-hour scan schedule paired with the default 5-minute TTL meant the whole segment showed offline 5 minutes after each round, “reviving” only at the next scan. Raising the cadence to 5 minutes and relaxing the TTL to 15 minutes brought it to steady state:

Scan peaks match because the working set is the same; the ~50MB steady-state gap comes from the router side’s passive-source caches (lease tracking, connection tracking, per-IP observation cache). For /24-scale networks, “scan peak ≈ steady state + 50MB” works as a capacity-planning reference.

Memory is a hard gate. In this experiment, a 512MB MT7981 travel router (vendor firmware, ~90MB usable in normal operation) hit OOM with the center installed — its admin page stopped responding; the agent form was OOM-killed during initialization the same way. The project has since added a low-memory configuration template and a free-memory preflight before startup. Budget at least 1GB of RAM for the router form.

Conclusions

  • Scanning and identification: no difference across the three shapes; identification quality tracks the version.
  • Passive discovery is decided by position: DHCP lease authority, the NAT panorama, and cross-subnet visibility are the router form’s structural advantages — host-side tuning cannot make them up.
  • The host form’s passive capability is adequate (34 devices in 8 minutes), with a clear ceiling.
  • For multiple subnets, prefer center + agent: one collector per subnet, clean subnet membership, scaling is just another agent; the router form “can see” across subnets, but passive-path subnet filtering is still in the works and currently misfiles devices.

By scenario: for a single-subnet small network (home or small workshop alike), one memory-rich (≥1GB) soft router doing everything is the most complete experience; with multiple subnets or growing scale, put the center on an always-on host to keep the ledger and give each remote subnet an agent; as for 512MB little boxes — let them keep routing.

If this made you want to take inventory of your own network: the project is open source on GitHub (AGPLv3) — repository here, and the releases page has single-file Linux amd64/arm64 binaries plus OpenWrt ipk/apk packages. Install it and you get exactly the interface in the screenshots. The fingerprint material is plain data files; if one of your devices goes unrecognized, add a rule following the fingerprint spec — the thicker the library, the more it recognizes.
