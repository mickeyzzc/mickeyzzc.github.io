Where Should MiBeeSteward Live: Soft Router, Host, or Center+Agent — Tested

September 18, 2026 September 18, 2026 MiBeeOSS Go, OpenWrt, Soft Router, Passive Discovery, Device Discovery, Open Source MiBee Open Source Projects Series 3038 words 15 min read
🔊

I maintain an open-source network device management system (MiBee Steward — a single Go binary that discovers, identifies, and keeps a ledger of devices on the LAN). It runs directly on a router, or on an ordinary host in the network; when the network has multiple subnets there’s a third option: one center with a few lightweight agents. Which placement is right? I didn’t want to answer from impressions. With two subnets and a few idle devices on hand, I ran all three comparisons.

The conclusion first: on the same version and the same parameters, every shape scans and identifies identically; all differences sit in “passive discovery” and “cross-subnet visibility”, and they come from where the process lives — no amount of tuning makes them up. Along the way I also tripped over two bugs in our own code and one design gap, plus the record of a 512MB router falling flat on its face. All below.

My home network, a.k.a. the test bench

All three comparisons happened on this one physical network, so here it is first (both the text and the screenshots are sanitized: IPs replaced with documentation ranges, device names genericized).

The house runs two /24 subnets. The primary one holds the everyday crowd: phones, laptops, a few cameras, a corner full of Mi Home gear, seven or eight dev boards — thirty-odd devices. The second subnet is quieter: a NAS and a few always-on little hosts. A dual-arm RK3568 soft router bridges the two — that’s the machine playing the “router shape” later. It holds one address in each subnet, acting both as the inter-subnet router and as an L2 member that can see both sides.

Orange is the dual-arm soft router; blue on both sides are the two subnets; the dashed link just means both subnets belong to one physical network — there is no third box in between. The convenient part of this topology: all three deployment shapes fit on the same physical network at once, so even the “environments differ” variable is gone. ① The center on the router spans both subnets by itself; ② the center on the second-subnet host sees only its own segment; ③ the same host’s center again, this time with an agent placed in the primary subnet. The first two are the first two comparisons below; the third is the project’s distributed design (design doc): the center keeps the ledger, every remote subnet gets a lightweight collector that scans and reports back. I never had the conditions to test it seriously — now an idle armv7 board in the primary subnet made it possible.

The first comparison was wrong

Worth recounting the detour first. My initial move was to compare two instances that were already running: an RK3568 (4GB) soft router on iStoreOS, and a 4GB ARM host on Armbian, same subnet. The result looked great — 55 devices on the router vs. 38 on the host, and the host clearly identified more device brands. I nearly wrote that up as the conclusion.

Before writing, I glanced at the version numbers: the router ran a month-old build, the host a same-day build. In that month we had merged a batch of identification material — Mi Home hostname fingerprints, mDNS/SSDP service-advertisement fingerprints, a passive-observation identification layer. In other words, “the host identifies better” could easily have been a version gap with nothing to do with deployment shape; and the device-count difference was confounded by how long each side had been accumulating data.

A comparison with uncontrolled environments flatters your expectations no matter how good the numbers look. So I tore it down and started over.

How the variables were controlled this time

  • One source tree, one version number, built twice: the OpenWrt ipk installed on the soft router, the bare binary under systemd on the host. Both CPUs are ARM64 — no difference at the instruction-set level.
  • Both databases wiped, starting from zero. The old databases held weeks of accumulated migration debris and historical heartbeat state; no fair comparison with those around.
  • The soft router keeps its natural router configuration: DHCP leases, conntrack — the discovery sources “only a router has” all on. The host enables what its side can: ARP cache, multicast listening, SNMP polling of the gateway’s ARP table. No artificial leveling — the comparison is exactly how each shape performs naturally.
  • The scan task used the same parameters (full pipeline, /24 range, 50 concurrency), triggered in the same second, to eliminate drift of network state over time.

Passive observation ran first for eight minutes (no scans triggered), then the full scan, then the memory measurements.

Passive discovery: 8 minutes, 42 vs. 34

The interesting part is not 42 vs. 34 — it’s where each side’s devices came from.

The router’s 42 came from three signals stacked. The strongest is the DHCP lease table — every address in this subnet was handed out by it, so every device that ever took a lease left a record, including the dormant IoT devices that answer no active probe at all. Mi Home’s cloud-bound devices are the classic case: the WiFi module firmware closes every local credential-free channel — no mDNS answers, common TCP ports closed — but the DHCP handshake has to happen. Hostnames in the leases (viomi-waterheater-xxx, chuangmi-camera-xxx) plus our new hostname fingerprint library file these “mute devices” straight into the ledger with brand and type attached. The second is conntrack: as the gateway, whoever is talking right now has an entry in its connection table. The third is the ARP cache paired with per-host identification — every new IP that surfaces gets a full identification scan on the spot, so each device enters the list already named and typed, not as a bare IP.

The host’s 34 exceeded my expectations. It has no lease table, but a mechanism I call the “ARP snowball” keeps rolling: the initial ARP cache holds only the few hosts it has talked to, but each newly discovered IP triggers an identification scan, and the scan traffic itself makes the peer’s ARP entry flow back into the local cache — which yields new “already-communicated IPs” to query. Recursive expansion. Add the mDNS/SSDP self-advertisements any host on a shared L2 can hear (the NAS’s avahi service set, DLNA SERVER headers), and eight minutes roll out 34 devices. The host shape’s passive discovery is adequate — it just has a ceiling: the lease authority and the NAT connection table are two things it physically cannot get.

Active scanning: 107.667 s vs. 107.640 s

Full scans triggered simultaneously, results side by side:

MetricSoft routerHost
Full scan duration107.667 s107.640 s
Liveness verdicts3535

Twenty-seven milliseconds apart — scheduler noise. Liveness verdicts: not one device more or fewer.

I think this number is the one most worth recording from the whole experiment: scanning behavior is deterministic across platforms. Same binary, same parameters, same network — the alive set and the duration line up to the millisecond. The CPUs’ performance difference shows up nowhere, because scan duration is decided by how probe timeouts are designed, not by compute.

Identification quality lines up too. On the 35 shared devices, type verdicts agreed 100% — same fingerprint material, same evidence, same verdicts. The only brand difference was one NAS: the host’s multicast listener happened to catch its SSDP SERVER header (MiniDLNA/1.3.3) and recognized one extra software brand; the router missed that packet. Passive evidence is hostage to timing windows; a single-device difference says nothing about capability.

What about the devices banked during the passive phase? After the scan, both ledgers merged cleanly: the host’s 34 were all re-covered by the scan (no blind spots of its own), while 7 of the router’s 42 were unreachable by scanning — all of them devices on the other subnet.

Unexpected bonus: cross-subnet visibility, and a bug

Those 7 came from conntrack. The soft router is also the inter-subnet router, and forwarding-table entries for cross-subnet traffic are laid out in front of it — an unplanned bonus of the router shape: it sees its whole subnet, and also sees “who is talking across subnets”.

But the bonus immediately exposed a bug of ours: those 7 foreign-subnet devices went straight into this subnet’s device list. The passive discovery paths (conntrack, DHCP) bypassed the CIDR boundary check we built at the reporting layer — that gate only guarded active-scan reports. In a single-instance deployment this just means a few “neighbors’ devices” mixed into the list; with multiple collectors it compounds. Already on the fix list: filter devices into subnets by discovery source at ingestion.

Group three: one center covering both subnets, or center + agent

First, the router shape’s “native cross-subnet”. Same version, same parameters — its center needs no extra components, and the ledger naturally holds devices from both subnets — 49 at sampling time. The cost was visible on the spot: 14 primary-subnet devices were recorded under the second subnet’s network. The passive path bypassed the CIDR boundary check (that same bug from the previous section, live: the backend’s subnet-reconciliation metric jumped straight to 14). Cross-subnet vision is real, and subnet bookkeeping is loose — both sentences true at once.

Now the distributed side. The agent is a bare binary around 20MB, started by systemd; point it at the center’s address with a token and it’s done. It scans the primary subnet with the exact same pipeline as the center — one /24 round took about 110 seconds, lining up with the two ARM64 machines’ 107.6 again. “Scanning behavior is deterministic across platforms” verified a third time — this time across instruction sets too.

Reporting uses a pull model, with the scheduler living inside the center process. One full round looks like this:

The agent asks for new instructions every 60 seconds and picks them up on the next pull; the center just enqueues a scan when it’s due — the whole chain opens no inbound port on either side, so an agent behind NAT works as usual. The anti-entropy hash makes reporting on a stable network nearly free — with no changes, a round costs a few dozen index updates. On the ledger side, 33 primary-subnet devices entered through the agent, keyed by MAC, the same identity system the center scans locally — wherever a device is discovered, it is one and the same asset.

That last row is the real dividing line between the two shapes, and it’s exactly where things fell apart. On deployment day, thirty-odd devices on the primary subnet all went red on the dashboard. Subtraction over the center’s logs finally lined up the clock: each device’s “offline duration” equaled current time minus the last scan round minus 5 minutes, to the second. The mechanism: in the distributed shape the center’s heartbeats correctly skip the agent network (cross-subnet probes would time out anyway — no point pretending), and liveness instead comes from “leases” refreshed by scan reports. Leases expire after 5 minutes by default, and I had scheduled the agent to scan every 6 hours. So 5 minutes after each round finished, the whole subnet “died” in the ledger, lay in state for five-plus hours, and revived at the next round.

A 30-second heartbeat in the single-instance shape translates, in the distributed shape, into “scan cadence must beat the lease TTL”. No document states this constraint; the wall of red taught it. The fix isn’t elegant but it’s honest: the agent now scans every 5 minutes and the lease is relaxed to 15, leaving a two-round margin. The better fix is to let the agent’s passive discovery also renew leases — the code comments claim it already does; it doesn’t. That’s the second gap this experiment left behind.

One more bug shook loose, this one in the rendering layer. During the comparison I kept the router center’s web page open, and that day it happened to be all-green — zero devices offline — whereupon the dashboard froze on its loading skeleton. Root cause: the backend serialized an empty list as JSON null (that’s what a Go nil slice does), and the question mark in the frontend template overview?.abnormal.length only guarded the first hop; null.length threw, and took the whole render scheduler down with it. “All devices healthy” was the trigger — without running two instances side by side, this bug would never surface in daily use.

Identical peaks make sense — the same scan’s working set. The ~50MB steady-state gap is mostly the router side’s passive-source caches (lease tracking, connection tracking, per-IP observation cache). For a /24 network, “full-scan peak ≈ steady state +50MB” is a measured number you can take straight into capacity planning.

The real lesson came from the last two rows. I also own an MT7981 travel router with 512MB of RAM (vendor stock firmware) and wanted to verify there too. After installing the center, the router’s own admin page stopped loading entirely. Inside: 15MB free, the OOM killer had already taken out one of the box’s original overlay-VPN daemons, load at 20, the web server starved. Killing our process restored everything instantly. Not giving up, I tried the lighter agent shape — it was OOM-killed too, at 103MB during initialization.

That box ships with the vendor’s full suite plus two overlay-VPN clients; usable memory in normal operation is around 90MB, and our process — center or agent alike — needs 90–100MB just to load the fingerprint library and identification engine. A 512MB router isn’t “tight”, it physically cannot hold this — and that assumes you haven’t already spent the memory on something else. This failure drove two improvements: the install script gained a low-memory profile (SQLite page cache cut from 64MB to 8MB, concurrency halved, a Go soft memory limit set), and a free-memory preflight at startup that refuses to start with a clear “switch devices” message — better to refuse the install than take down someone’s router.

So, where should it live

With the data laid out, the choice is fairly clear:

  • Scanning and identification: no difference across shapes. Identification quality tracks the version — keeping up with releases matters more than anything, which is also why my first comparison produced the wrong “host identifies better” impression.
  • Every real difference sits in passive discovery. What the router has extra comes from where it sits: the DHCP lease table (the only reliable signal for dormant mute devices), the conntrack connection table (who is talking right now), cross-subnet vision. No amount of host-side tuning makes these up.
  • The host shape does have passive capability — ARP snowball plus multicast listening, 34 devices in eight minutes, most active devices covered — but the ceiling is explicit.
  • Cross-subnet: “can see” (router) and “supposed to see” (distributed) are two different things. The router crosses subnets natively, but its passive path currently doesn’t check subnet membership (the 14 misplaced devices are on the record); the center + agent shape puts one collector per subnet, boundaries correct by construction — at the price of a different liveness mechanism, where scan cadence must beat lease TTL. Work that out before you deploy.

So my advice is by scenario: for a single-subnet home, one soft router with memory to spare (≥1GB) doing everything gives the most complete experience; with multiple subnets or a growing device count, put the center on an always-on host to keep the ledger and drop one agent into each remote subnet — discovery goes where the position advantage is, the ledger stays in one place; once the passive-path subnet-membership filter ships, a dual-arm router covering both subnets becomes a budget-friendly option too. As for the 512MB little box — let it keep being a router.

If this made you want to take inventory of your own network: the project is open source on GitHub (AGPLv3) — repository here, and the releases page has single-file binaries for Linux amd64/arm64 plus OpenWrt ipk/apk packages. Install it and you get exactly the interface in the screenshots. The fingerprint material is plain data files; if one of your devices goes unrecognized, add a rule following the fingerprint spec — the thicker the library, the more it recognizes.
