Hooking AI into a CSI Sensing Stack: The Layers That Fit and the Ones That Don't

September 27, 2026 September 27, 2026 Physical World Development WiFi Sensing, CSI, LLM, Edge Computing, Home Assistant Signals & Image Processing Series 3186 words 16 min read
🔊

Two ESP32 boards, a serial stream rate-limited to 50 packets per second, a sensing engine on the PC side, a few entities in Home Assistant — once this CSI sensing stack is running at home, the next unavoidable question is: where does AI plug in? The question tends to get two extreme answers. One treats the LLM as a universal plugin and tries to attach it everywhere; the other decides that waveform signal processing and language models live in different worlds and doesn’t bother. Neither is needed. What is needed is to split the stack by time constant and check, layer by layer, whether the LLM’s latency, cost, and modality line up.

This post is that split: layer the stack first, then walk through which layers don’t fit and why, what work the layers that do fit should carry; check each point against a shipped open-source project — RuView has already turned the runtime and development-time installation points into working code; and finish with a development-time practice you can use today: synthetic signals as ground truth for AI-written signal-processing code.

The Stack’s Time Constants: The First Yardstick

Lay the sensing stack out from bottom to top, and every layer differs from its neighbors by an order of magnitude in pace:

LayerTypical cadenceCarrier
Sampling and rate limiting20 ms per packet (50 pps)MCU firmware
DSP detection100 ms ~ 1 sOn-device small model or PC engine
Event detectionSecondsDebounce, hysteresis, cooldown
Rule executionEvent-drivenPlatform automation engine
Attribution and reportingHours to daysCurrently unclaimed — the LLM’s slot

A single LLM call takes seconds and is billed by the token. Check these two facts against the table: the real-time path runs at 50 packets per second, 16 complex numbers per packet — you can’t feed that in, and there’s no reason to. Detection at this layer is millisecond-scale math by nature, not reading comprehension. Meanwhile, a question like “why did the sensing act up at home yesterday” runs on a cadence of hours and days, which lands squarely in the LLM’s comfort zone.

The two purple blocks are where each kind of model belongs: sub-second detection goes to the on-device small model, hours-to-days attribution goes to the LLM, and the event layer sits between them — that orange layer is the boundary between big and small models, and the next section explains why.

The Two Layers That Don’t Fit: Real-Time Detection and Event Execution

Real-time detection. Three hard constraints, each more fundamental than the last:

  • Latency. A walking person drives the Doppler shift up to 16 Hz; if detection lags half a beat, the event is already over. The budget for a sensing decision is milliseconds to sub-seconds. A single LLM call starts at seconds — a two-order-of-magnitude gap.
  • Cost. A 50 pps stream is over four million packets a day. Even at one cent per call, that’s five figures a year — an API bill for something an inter-frame difference on a 240 MHz single core already handles. The math doesn’t work.
  • Modality. LLMs consume tokens. CSI is a stream of complex numbers; serializing I/Q arrays into text turns a numerical computation problem into a reading-comprehension problem, and the serialization loses both precision and structure. An LLM has no intuition for what a waveform looks like — it has never seen one.

The AI at this layer was always supposed to be a small model: ESPectre’s high-accuracy detector is a 529-parameter neural network, and my S3 board runs an amplitude inter-frame difference with a double-threshold hysteresis. The division of labor between small and large models here isn’t old being replaced by new — it’s a boundary drawn by the latency budget.

Event execution. Debounce, hysteresis, and cooldown are deterministic logic whose behavior space is small enough to enumerate and test: eight consecutive frames above the entry threshold before reporting presence, sixteen below the exit threshold before reporting departure — every edge can be verified by hand. The baseline requirement for home automation is reproducibility: the same input must produce the same output, or you won’t dare hang alarms and interlocks off it. Put the LLM’s non-determinism into this layer and you get a home that is right most of the time — and an automation that is right most of the time is far harder to debug than one that plainly doesn’t work.

Rules can be generated by an LLM; execution never passes through one. Generation is a one-shot offline action, execution is an online action happening every second, and their requirements for determinism are completely different. That sentence is the conclusion of this entire section.

The Two Layers That Do Fit: Attribution Reports and Rule Generation

Attribution and reporting. The LLM’s correct input is not the waveform but the event stream: timestamps, scores, state transitions, plus the RF-side environment annotations you already know — the frequency signature of microwave-oven interference, the timing of channel changes, the nightly automatic channel rescans routers like to run. Judgment stays with rules and statistics; the LLM organizes conclusions into plain language: a daily report reading “the living-room node false-triggered for ten minutes from 19:40, overlapping with kitchen microwave use; consider moving the sensing channel toward channel 1” beats you digging through three days of logs. The value is real, but don’t oversell it: the implementation is a scheduled task plus one API call, a few dozen lines of code. It saves log-digging time, not detector accuracy.

Natural-language rule generation. CSI’s event types are naturally few: presence, motion, departure, stillness — plus, at most, “no movement for a long time.” A small action space is exactly the comfort zone for LLM-generated rules: the generated automation passes a schema validation before it enters the store, illegal structures never reach the engine, and when something misfires you can see at a glance which rule did it. Home Assistant already wires the LLM into its conversation layer; the remaining work is feeding it the sensing entities and their event semantics — no technical suspense, just work.

The privacy ledger. The waveform encodes the geometry of the whole room — where the walls are, where people are, the rhythm of breathing — so the waveform never leaves the house; that’s the floor. Event-level data (booleans plus timestamps) is an order of magnitude less sensitive, and its volume makes cloud costs negligible — the real trade-off is privacy, not money. To tighten further, the reporting layer can run on a local small model, at the cost of some attribution quality. Keeping waveform, events, and reports as three separate layers, each deciding for itself whether it leaves the house, is far more useful than the single question “should this system be cloud-connected or not.”

Identity is its own tier. RuView’s privacy layer ran one measurement: matching two people’s identity using only the WiFi cardiac-respiratory channels yields a gap of 0.0005 — with its channel design, “recognizing who this is” is physically near-indistinguishable. So it labels identity recognition an experimental capability behind a data gate; if you want it, you enable the channel explicitly. Sensing and identity are two different things in engineering, and the layering is worth drawing with this tier marked separately.

A Shipped Reference: Where RuView Put Its AI

The layering above isn’t armchair reasoning. RuView, the open-source WiFi sensing project (whose signal pipeline this series has already taken apart), has turned every runtime and development-time installation point into code. One section per installation point.

Training Off the Device, Inference On It

Its time-constant ledger matches the one above exactly. Training the self-supervised contrastive encoder happens on servers: 60K CSI frames, 610K contrastive triplets, 12.2 million training steps. What lands on the ESP32 is a different set of numbers — an 8 KB encoder after 4-bit quantization plus a 1,792-parameter MicroLoRA adapter per room, about 55 KB in total, a tenth of the 520 KB of RAM. Training consumes hours of compute, inference delivers millisecond responses, and the two only touch once, at quantization and download.

The per-room MicroLoRA deserves a closer look. Environment baselines drift with rooms and furniture — a shared headache in WiFi sensing — and the fix here is training one pair of low-rank small matrices per room without touching the trunk. That’s the same idea as the threshold learning on my boards: chase environment drift with a small-parameter adaptation, except theirs is the neural version. The labeling answer lives on the same line: contrastive triplets are constructed automatically from temporal structure, zero human labels; only the pose tier needs labels, and there MediaPipe pulls keypoints from a camera to teach the CSI, with cross-modal fine-tuning taking 2.1 seconds for 400 epochs on an RTX 5080 (ADR-079). How expensive your labels are decides whether plugging AI in is smooth or brutal.

The animation walks the train → quantize → deploy → per-room-adapt chain: the server spins through its training round on the left, the purple block gets squeezed by the 4-bit quantizer, and only 55 KB lands on the device; below, the orange adapter chips light up room by room — one pair of 1,792-parameter matrices each.

MCP: Feed the Agent Events, Not Waveforms

For the runtime installation point, RuView ships an MCP server (ADR-124) exposing exactly six tools to AI agents: presence state, breathing, heart rate, a vitals summary, the latest privacy-layer scan, and an event subscription. The return values deserve individual inspection: every one is structured state or numbers — not a single tool returns raw CSI. The transport config follows the same ledger as the privacy section: bind to 127.0.0.1 by default, bearer tokens, Origin validation, read-only. The LLM’s input boundary in this architecture isn’t enforced by prompt wording — it’s drawn in the API. It can see “someone in the living room, breathing 14 per minute”; it cannot see what the waveform looks like.

This is the industrial version of the attribution-report section: wrap the event stream in a read-only interface, and the interface — not a prompt — decides what the LLM gets and what it never touches.

In the animation, green event tokens pass through the upper read-only gate from the node to the LLM; the red waveform token hits the lower gate and bounces back — not one of the six tools returns raw CSI. The boundary isn’t in the prompt; it’s in that wall.

The Honesty Guardrail: Turning “Humans Verify the Numbers” into Tooling

The development-time installation point is the most interesting. This repository — a nearly ten-thousand-word README, 92k stars — openly admits its on-device pose model scores PCK@20 of 3.0%, with an inference path that is still a stub returning confidence=0. To keep AI agents (and human readers) from parroting marketing numbers as measured ones, it ships a MetaHarness: agent run is read-only by default, with writes gated behind both --allow-write and --confirm; claim-check inspects a report for claims that lack measured backing — ADR-182 put the MEASURED versus CLAIMED distinction into code. Behind it sits a chain of evidence: verify.py deterministically replays the whole pipeline and checks SHA-256 hashes, and nothing counts until it prints VERDICT: PASS; measurement records go into an Ed25519 witness chain for tamper evidence; a 33-row capability-claims matrix carries evidence links row by row.

The earlier section said humans verify the constants — that was personal discipline. At this scale, discipline grows into tooling: a claim without an evidence link doesn’t pass validation. That trick is worth copying more than any model in the repository.

Development Time: AI Writes the Code, Humans Own the Constants

That covers runtime. Development time is a different story. The firmware, parsers, and DSP code in this stack were written alongside coding agents from the start, and CSI code has two particular quirks worth calling out.

First, constants need human verification. LLMs write parsing and filtering code quickly and fluently, but they hallucinate constants: subcarrier index tables differ per chip and per bandwidth — HT40’s 128-subcarrier mapping is entirely rearranged, not HT20’s table extended — and an LLM will cheerfully answer an ESP32 question with the Intel 5300’s index table, or invent a sampling rate on the spot. The countermeasure is plain: every constant carries a source comment, the parser and the serial frame format are treated as a pair and changed together, and the technique below backs the whole thing up.

Second, synthetic signals as ground truth. A dozen-odd lines of multipath model produce synthetic CSI with known motion baked in:

import numpy as np

n_pkg, n_sc = 600, 16                      # 10 s × 50 packets, 16 extracted subcarriers
t = np.arange(n_pkg) / 50.0
base = np.random.randn(n_sc) + 1j * np.random.randn(n_sc)   # static multipath baseline
breath = 0.03 * np.exp(1j * 2 * np.pi * 0.3 * t)            # 0.3 Hz breathing: common phase swing
walk = np.zeros(n_pkg)
walk[300:360] = 0.4                                         # walking burst in second 6-7
csi = base[None, :] * (1.0 + breath[:, None] + walk[:, None])
csi += 0.02 * (np.random.randn(n_pkg, n_sc) + 1j * np.random.randn(n_pkg, n_sc))

This is toy-grade synthesis: breathing is modeled as a common in-phase swing across all subcarriers and multipath selectivity is not modeled at all — real breathing signals are path-dependent. But it’s enough to regression-test a detector: motion is reported where it should be and quiet stays quiet, so a change didn’t break the logic. Conversely, when the AI rewrites a filter, one run against the synthetic sequence tells you immediately whether it broke something — no more flashing the board, staring at the serial port, and eyeballing waveforms every time.

The synthesizer itself is pure math with no hardware dependency — exactly the code AI writes most reliably. Let AI write the math that sits above the constants, and humans verify the constants: that division of labor holds at development time.

The human’s slot. AI has no waveform intuition. The judgment that “this segment looks wrong” on an oscilloscope comes from having seen a few hundred real waveforms; AI can only see the judgments you write down. Translating intuition into assertions and statistical features that feed back into the tests is where the human belongs in this collaboration — where human attention goes determines how fast the loop turns. That topic belongs to loop engineering and won’t be repeated here.

At runtime, the LLM stands above the events doing attribution and rule generation, and has no place in the real-time path; at development time, AI already writes the parsers, filters, and synthesizers, while constants and waveform intuition stay human. That boundary is not a conservative estimate of AI capability — it falls out of the latency budget and the modality match. With every layer’s time constant laid out on the table, whether a layer fits is something you can measure. RuView turned this layering into runnable, verifiable code at every point — as good a homework submission for this yardstick as any.
