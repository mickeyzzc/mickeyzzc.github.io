Finding Faults with Thermal and Spectral Imaging: From Radiometry to Equipment Selection

October 30, 2026 October 30, 2026 Physical World Development Thermal Imaging, IR Thermometry, Emissivity, Fault Diagnosis, Multispectral, Hyperspectral, Inspection Standards, Equipment Selection Signals & Image Processing Series 4826 words 23 min read
🔊

Visible cameras answer “is it there, what is it”; thermal cameras answer “where is it hot, how hot”; spectroscopy answers “what material, is its state normal”. Failing equipment leaves different traces in the three channels: a loose joint heats up first, degrading insulation discharges first (UV), damp materials change their spectral reflectance first. My two earlier posts — the dual-spectrum camera survey and the embedded integration walkthrough — covered the cameras and the plumbing; this one covers the last mile: using temperature fields and spectra to find problems, and how to pick equipment for that job. The order: thermometry physics → thermal signatures of faults → industry criteria → spectral methods → equipment requirements and selection → reading methods and automation.

How thermometry works: radiation, emissivity, and errors

From radiation to temperature

A thermal camera does not measure temperature; it measures radiation. Everything above absolute zero radiates per the Stefan-Boltzmann law:

text
1

M = ε × σ × T⁴

M is radiant power per unit area, σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴), ε is the emissivity, T is absolute temperature. The camera measures M and inverts for T using an assumed ε.

Two big consequences hide in this formula. First, the fourth power amplifies temperature differences into radiance differences: a target only 5°C above its background differs by nearly 7% in radiated power — far above detector noise, which is why 0.05°C-level differences are visible. That’s the physical source of thermal sensitivity. Second, the formula contains ε, a coefficient you cannot observe directly: the camera reads the product ε×T⁴, so any error in the ε estimate goes straight into the temperature. Most thermometry error comes from exactly there — not from the detector.

One note on wavelength: a 300K object’s radiation peaks near 10μm by Wien’s displacement law, inside the 8–14μm long-wave window — which is why civilian thermal imaging lives in LWIR; targets at over a thousand degrees peak toward 3–5μm, the MWIR home turf (the survey post covered the window-detector correspondence).

Emissivity: the weakest link in the chain

Emissivity measures how strongly a surface radiates compared to an ideal blackbody, from 0 to 1, set by material and surface condition:

SurfaceTypical εMeasurement feasibility
Human skin, water, rough ceramics, asphalt0.92–0.98Measure directly, accurate
Paint, electrical tape, anodized aluminum0.90–0.97Measure directly, accurate
Oxidized or rusted steel0.6–0.9Workable with the correct value
Smooth plastic film~0.9, but partly transparentThin spots transmit radiation from behind
Polished metal (aluminum, copper, bright stainless)0.05–0.2Not reliable directly

Why is metal so low? Metal is a mirror to infrared — free electrons reflect most incident radiation, so it re-radiates little of its own. That’s a double problem: weak self-emission (small signal, amplified error) plus strong reflection (other people’s heat charged to it). A rough worked example (ignoring the reflection term): the target is really at 100°C with true emissivity 0.5, but the camera is set to 0.95 — the inferred temperature is 100°C × (0.5/0.95)^0.25 ≈ 44°C, a 56°C error. Adding the reflection term makes the error messier but points the same way.

Emissivity is also directional: the same surface has different ε face-on versus at 45°, especially metals. Field practice keeps viewing angles within 45°. Semi-transparent materials like thin plastic films are another trap: the thinner the film, the more radiation from behind passes through — you measure a mix of front surface and background.

Three standard fixes for low-emissivity surfaces: stick a piece of electrical tape or thin paint on the measurement point (turning the surface high-emissivity, waiting for thermal equilibrium, then reading the tape), the most common field move; contact cross-check (a thermocouple on the same spot to correct the ε setting); and for enclosed switchgear, an IR window — germanium or dedicated polymer windows transmit 8–14μm well, so cabinets open less often for routine rounds.

One counterintuitive trap must be memorized: ordinary glass is essentially opaque to LWIR. Measuring through glass reads the glass surface, not the equipment behind — a shorted, glowing-red component behind the pane can still read “room temperature”. To inspect inside an enclosure, open it or use an IR window (which is why IR windows aren’t ordinary glass).

The error checklist: every step before the number

Beyond emissivity, a trustworthy reading passes these gates:

  • Reflected temperature: low-emissivity surfaces reflect ambient sources. Outdoors in daylight the sun is the biggest reflector; rigorously, record and set the reflected-temperature parameter, or use a baffle (hold an opaque board in front of the target; its reading is the reflection reference);
  • Distance and spot size: each detector pixel covers a small target patch — the IFOV. A 640×512 detector with a 24° horizontal lens has an IFOV of about 0.65 mrad — one pixel covers ~6.5mm at 10 meters. Rule of thumb: the target should span at least 3×3 pixels for a trustworthy reading — at 10 m, a 2 cm target. Farther means a longer lens or accepting a large, averaged spot;
  • Atmospheric absorption: water vapor and CO₂ attenuate even inside the 8–14μm window. Negligible within a few meters; at tens of meters, rain, fog, or high humidity, apply the distance correction or readings run systematically low;
  • Wind and convection: wind force-cools a heating joint and hides the defect — record wind speed for outdoor electrical work, and reschedule on windy days;
  • The camera itself: uncooled cores drift with their own temperature and recover via periodic shutter calibration (FFC/NUC) — between calibrations the reading runs “open-loop”, which matters for long continuous recordings; absolute accuracy and long-term stability come from calibration (see the selection section).

Finally, untangle three spec-sheet terms that get confused constantly: NETD is “how small a difference can be resolved” (sensitivity — the ability to see); accuracy is “how close the absolute temperature is” (typically ±2°C or ±2%); display resolution is “how many decimals the value shows” (pure display, meaningless). Good sensitivity does not mean accurate absolute temperature — anomaly hunting runs on NETD, conclusions run on accuracy plus your emissivity homework.

The four thermal signatures of a fault

“Abnormal” on a thermal image falls into four classes. Two questions first: which class is this anomaly, and compared against what?

Class one: absolute rise. Above an allowed-temperature threshold. Motor windings have explicit temperature limits by insulation class — Class A 105°C, E 120°C, B 130°C, F 155°C, H 180°C; exceeding them burns life rapidly. Electrical joints have allowed-temperature-rise values set by material, plating, and code. Absolute thresholds are intuitive, but load, environment, and season all drift — a fixed “alarm above 60°C” never fires in winter and cries wolf in midsummer. Alone, unreliable.

Class two: relative difference. Compare against a peer: phase against phase, idler against idler, module against module. Both sides share the same environment and load, common variables cancel, and the difference that remains is the defect signal. The power industry formalized this (next section); here’s a worked feel for it: a three-phase terminal block reads 95°C on phase A, 45°C on B/C, ambient 25°C. Phase A’s relative difference is δ = (95 − 45) / (95 − 25) = 50/70 ≈ 71% — that high a δ cannot be explained by load variation; it’s a clear thermal defect. The normalization is the point: “hotspot 50°C above the healthy phase” is design margin getting used up under summer high load, but a serious fault under winter light load — δ removes exactly that background difference.

Class three: spatial pattern. The shape of the temperature field is diagnostic information:

  • A round, isolated hotspot centered on a contact → poor connection;
  • A busbar uniformly warm along its length → overload or undersized cross-section;
  • A local hot region on one side of a motor housing → local winding fault; uniformly warm → overload or degraded cooling;
  • Large, sharply-bounded mottling → missing insulation (the inside-outside ΔT projects straight through the gap);
  • A regular single-cell or whole-string bright patch → PV hotspot (regularity itself says cell-level defect, not debris glare).

Class four: time trend. The drift of one measurement point across load cycles and seasons. Trend inflections usually precede thresholds: joint resistance degrades gradually, and a monthly log showing “temperature climbing month over month at the same load” beats any single reading. Trend reading requires comparable conditions (same load, weather, parameters) — which is why the power industry records load current at capture.

One distinction in the table deserves emphasis: current-heating and dielectric-heating are two different mechanisms. Joints heat with current (I²R, load squared), insulation heats with voltage (dielectric loss, hot whenever energized, little relation to current) — so current-induced defects go invisible at low load while voltage-induced ones are visible the moment the equipment is energized. DL/T 664 gives separate criteria tables precisely because the reading logic differs.

Industry criteria: from DL/T 664 to IEC 62446

Signatures are the “see”; criteria are the “decide”. Industries have codified the reading rules; match yours to the standard.

Electrical equipment: DL/T 664 relative-difference grading

China’s power-industry standard DL/T 664 (Application Rules of Infrared Diagnosis for Live Electrical Equipment) is the backbone of electrical IR rounds. It splits heating into current-induced (joints, clamps, connectors — contact and current-carrying parts) and voltage-induced (arresters, capacitors, bushings — insulation parts), with per-part criteria tables in its annexes. For current-induced parts the core is the relative temperature difference:

text
1

δ = (T1 − T2) / (T1 − T0) × 100%

T1 is the hot spot, T2 the same location on the healthy phase (or a peer device), T0 the ambient temperature. The standard maps temperature-rise and δ values per part type into three defect grades — general, severe, critical: general gets logged, planned, and re-checked; severe schedules maintenance soon; critical takes the equipment offline immediately. The numeric tables are per-part in the standard’s annexes — this post deliberately doesn’t restate them, because parts differ widely and a miscopied number is worse than none.

Half of diagnosis reliability is criteria; the other half is capture conditions: the standard sets preconditions on load, wind, and weather (the idea being sufficient load and stable environment, all recorded in the report), and conclusions captured outside those conditions get discounted. Two common operational companions: IR windows on switchgear so routine rounds never open cabinets or de-energize, and archiving every capture with load current and weather — the same image supports different conclusions at 40% and 90% load.

PV plants: hotspots and IEC TS 62446-3

Cells in a module are wired in series. When one cell is shaded (bird droppings, tree shadows, soiling) or cracked, its output drops and it flips from a source into a load inside the series loop, dissipating the power the other cells generate — its temperature climbs far above neighbors. That’s a hotspot. The bypass diode at the module’s end conducts in this situation, shunting the problem sub-string; if the diode itself fails, hotspot temperature keeps climbing, burning the encapsulant and possibly igniting the backsheet. Factory-level hot-spot endurance testing (a standard item in IEC 61215) simulates worst-case single-cell shading to prove the module and diode survive it.

The left half is the module view: a cloud shadow crosses one cell, that cell flips from generating to dissipating, and its temperature rises — on a thermal image, one isolated bright cell. The right half is the circuit view: the reverse-biased cell’s terminal voltage flips polarity, the bypass diode conducts and shunts it; with no diode, or a failed one, hotspot temperature climbs without bound.

For plants in operation, the field workhorse is a drone with a dual-spectrum camera flying waypoint missions. IEC TS 62446-3 codifies outdoor IR inspection of operating PV plants: irradiance conditions (irradiance must be sufficient and stable — overcast and low-sun data don’t count), camera requirements, flight and capture requirements, anomaly classification. Designing your inspection flow against it aligns with acceptance criteria. After thermal finds what’s already hot, cracks that haven’t started heating go to EL for confirmation (the spectra section) — thermal reads operating state, EL reads physical damage; together they make a complete plant checkup.

Buildings and energy

The physics here is you need enough ΔT: insulation defects only show when the inside-outside temperature difference pushes heat through them, and field experience wants 10°C or more — heating season in the north, air-conditioning season in the south. Three common jobs: missing insulation and thermal bridges show as hot/cold mottling from either side; paired with a blower door depressurizing the interior to 50Pa, air-leak paths appear as cold streaks (without the pressure differential, leak points often show nothing); roof leaks separate from dry zones a few hours after sunset through evaporative cooling and thermal mass — evening is early, deep night is right, and it’s the fastest leak survey tool there is.

Specialized scenarios: UV, gas, and hot boxes

Three contactless methods complement thermometry, each watching a different precursor of degradation:

  • Solar-blind UV corona imaging: degrading insulation on HV equipment coronas, radiating UV; the 240–280nm band is absorbed by the ozone layer and effectively absent from sunlight (“solar-blind”), so it works in daytime. UV imagers count photons in that band — stronger discharge, higher count — and overlay the discharge location on the visible image. The value: discharge precedes heating. Insulation coronas long before it burns through, so UV finds problems earlier than IR; IR sees what’s already hot, UV sees what’s actively discharging, and transmission-line inspections often carry both;
  • OGI (optical gas imaging): hydrocarbons (methane, VOCs) absorb strongly at 3.2–3.5μm (the C-H stretch). A cooled detector with a narrow bandpass images only that band; gas clouds absorb background radiation and show as smoke-like contrast — leaks become visible from a safe distance. SF₆ and fluorinated gases use a band near 7.5μm instead. Detection thresholds vary with distance and wind; small leaks dilute and disperse in strong wind — a physical limit that doubles as a scheduling window;
  • Railway hot-box detection: trackside IR detectors spaced along the line measure every axle box of passing trains, alarm on over-temperature and tie into vehicle numbering for location — one of the largest inline IR deployments in existence, essentially this post’s “hotspot pipeline” hardened into railway-side infrastructure.

Spectra: the fingerprint of materials

Thermal imaging exploits “everything radiates”; spectroscopy exploits “everything radiates and reflects differently”. Visible light is a sliver of the spectrum, and materials differ far more outside it — cut the spectrum into fine bands and each material’s fingerprint shows.

Where fingerprints come from: chemical bonds in molecules act like tiny springs with their own vibration frequencies; the energy differences land in near-IR/short-wave-IR as overtone and combination absorptions (O-H, C-H, N-H each at characteristic positions), with stronger fundamental absorptions in mid-IR; solid-state bands and electronic transitions set the visible-to-near-IR reflectance. “Reading spectra” is remote chemical sensing — no contact, no sampling, composition and state from a distance.

Two terms first: multispectral images in a handful to a dozen-plus relatively wide bands; hyperspectral images in tens to hundreds of continuous narrow bands, giving every pixel a full spectrum curve — far more information, at a step change in data volume and cost. On acquisition: multispectral uses filter wheels, beam splitters, or multiple sensor channels; hyperspectral commonly runs push-scan (line-by-line accumulation — high quality, needs relative motion) or snapshot (one exposure, all bands — real-time, lower resolution).

The representative methods, expanded:

NDVI and crop stress. Healthy vegetation reflects weakly in red and strongly in near-IR, with a steep step between them (the “red edge”). The normalized difference vegetation index NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red): healthy stands run 0.6–0.9, and stressed zones (disease, drought) drop first — one to two weeks before the yellowing is visible. A 4–6-band multispectral drone flying a grid produces the NDVI map behind variable-rate fertilizing, spraying, and replanting; the same idea extends to water-stress indices (NDWI and friends) — the core move is always “a ratio of feature bands”.

SWIR: moisture and plastics. Water has strong absorption peaks at 1450nm and 1940nm (the latter the strongest, but sitting in a detector-response dip — more demanding hardware). Food, wood, and textile moisture shows directly as a dip at those bands, so hyperspectral cameras grade product on the conveyor line; plastic sorting rides on the different C-H and O-H overtone positions of each polymer — PET, HDPE, PVC, PP are unmistakable in spectrum, which is what automated sorting lines rely on. Mold and bruising change tissue water and structure, so they show in spectrum before the surface changes.

EL: cracks that haven’t started heating. Forward-bias a PV module and the silicon p-n junction emits near-IR light (peaking near 1100nm — the silicon bandgap); a camera sensitive to that band photographs it — cracks, broken fingers, and burned spots don’t emit and show as dark lines. Thermal finds what’s already hot; EL finds cracks that haven’t started heating. Together they are the standard QC and acceptance pair for PV (EL is codified in IEC TS 60904-13). EL needs the module powered and is usually shot in the dark, so it lives in factories and sampling; in the field, IR leads. Its sibling PL (photoluminescence) excites with a laser instead of current and works in ambient light — mostly a lab and production-line tool.

The cost of hyperspectral. A hyperspectral rig costs tens of thousands upward and produces hundreds of MB to GB per scan, with per-pixel spectral matching to process (spectral angle mapping, PCA dimensionality reduction, spectral-library comparison; end-to-end networks increasingly) — worth it for mineral mapping, food foreign-body detection, precision waste sorting. For most industrial inline work, 4–10 multispectral bands with well-chosen features is where the cost-benefit lands. Scale matters too: civil satellites with a dozen-plus bands cover macro vegetation and land classification, drones cover field-level prescriptions, ground/inline hyperspectral covers line-level sorting — match the scale to the question.

Equipment requirements and selection

Finding-fault capability is half criteria, half hardware. This section goes parameter by parameter, then maps tasks to specs.

The thermal-camera parameter checklist

Resolution and lens — what you can resolve, how far. Together they make the IFOV: detector resolution × pixel pitch on the sensor side, focal length on the lens side. The selection algorithm is fixed: smallest target size ÷ farthest detection distance ≥ 3 × IFOV — derive the required IFOV first, then pick a resolution-and-focal-length combination. Common tiers (12μm pixel pitch, 24° lens reference): 160×120 for close-in overview; 256×192 for switchgear and near rounds; 384×288, the handheld-work mainstay; 640×512, the entry point for substations and airborne work; 1280×1024 for long-range fine reading. A long-focus lens (narrow FOV) shrinks the same detector’s IFOV several-fold — measuring far is cheaper through optics than through resolution: lens before resolution.

NETD — how small a difference you can see. Common tiers: ≤50mK (entry), ≤40mK (mainstream), ≤35mK and below (industrial-diagnostics grade). Hotspot hunting and pattern reading are fine at 40mK; building energy work and low-ΔT scenes want 30mK and under. NETD sets the discovery ability; it does not improve absolute accuracy.

Thermometry — whether readings support diagnosis. This is the dividing line between imaging-grade and radiometric-grade: imaging-grade outputs an image plus at most a few display temperatures for points/regions/whole-frame, with no settable parameters; radiometric-grade supports per-point/per-pixel radiometric thermometry and must expose the four parameters: emissivity, reflected temperature, distance, humidity, and output a temperature-calibrated radiometric image. Defect diagnosis requires radiometric-grade — with a “look-but-don’t-configure” core, criteria have nothing to stand on. Range, accuracy (typically ±2°C or ±2%), and repeatability are on the spec sheet; industrial diagnosis cares about accuracy and repeatability, not display decimals.

Frame rate. Export-restricted versions commonly run at 9Hz; industrial versions 25/50Hz. Manual rounds of static equipment: 9Hz suffices. Airborne, inline continuous, and moving targets want 25Hz+ or frames drop and smear during fast passes.

Shutter (FFC/NUC). Uncooled cores recalibrate behind an internal shutter, briefly freezing the image. Two selection points: how long the interval is (longer = smoother continuous recording) and whether external triggering can lock it (multi-camera synchronized recording shouldn’t have each unit freezing at its own moment).

Interfaces and integration. Three things matter online: a standard stream (RTSP/ONVIF — thermal channels and temperature-event protocols in the survey post); full-frame temperature data access (SDK pulls the temperature matrix or radiometric stream — alarm algorithms feed on this); digital IO or platform rules (over-limit/differential/zone triggers producing switch outputs or events directly).

Environmental fitness. Fixed outdoor installs: IP rating and operating temperature. Chemical and mining sites: hazardous-area (Ex) certification is a hard gate — Ex-rated thermography cameras or ordinary cameras inside explosion-proof housings. Dusty sites: clean lenses regularly (germanium scratches; use the proper tools).

Order-of-magnitude pricing. (2026 e-commerce magnitudes; volatile — read the tiers, not the digits) Phone-attach thermal lenses: hundreds to ~1,000 RMB. Entry handhelds: thousands. Professional handhelds (384×288, settable thermometry): thousands to ten-thousand-plus. Online radiometric cores and dual-spectrum PTZs: thousands to tens of thousands. Cooled MWIR, OGI, hyperspectral: hundreds of thousands.

Calibration and verification

The “±2°C” on a radiometric camera is calibrated in and drifts out. Three practices: run a blackbody comparison on a new device (set a blackbody source to several points, check the camera’s deviations — confirms the claimed accuracy), send online devices for periodic metrological calibration (blackbody as reference, certificate issued — this is what gives inspection reports legal standing), and run in-place checks routinely — a known-emissivity reference or a thermally stable surface photographed periodically; drift shows immediately.

Multispectral/hyperspectral selection

Three steps: problem → bands → mechanism. First, pin down what material state you must distinguish and look up its feature bands (water at 1450/1940nm, a polymer’s overtone position…). Second, decide band count — if two or three feature bands suffice (NDVI needs only Red + NIR), a multi-channel narrowband camera is the cheapest answer. Third, pick the scanning mechanism: inline conveyor lines suit push-scan (relative motion is free), drones suit frame multispectral rigs or snapshot sensors, labs use filter wheels. Detector follows band: visible-to-near-IR runs on ordinary CMOS, SWIR requires InGaAs (an order of magnitude costlier), MWIR needs cooled InSb/MCT. Inline sorting systems also need illumination — SWIR commonly runs halogen or specific-wavelength LED lighting, and illumination stability directly drives spectral reading quality.

Software and platforms

Last, where the data goes: handhelds rely on vendor analysis software for reports (emissivity, criteria, annotations live there); online devices feed your own pipeline — SDK pulls the temperature matrix, algorithms (next section) run on an edge box or the NVR, alarms return to the platform, and the visible channel goes into ordinary recording. Criteria evolve (new equipment, new parts, new thresholds), so archive the temperature matrices — saving only alarm screenshots throws away the raw material for re-judging criteria later.

From hotspot to alarm: reading methods and automation

The human reading checklist is fixed: pick ironbow or rainbow palette (pseudo-color affects only eyes, not data), record emissivity/distance/ambient/load, attach a visible-light reference in the report. The automated pipeline looks like this:

  • Segmentation: fixed thresholds are simplest (hotspot = ambient + N degrees) but need retuning as conditions shift; histogram auto-thresholds, background statistics (model the whole frame, flag outliers), and region growing hold up better. Deep learning works too — IR images are low-texture with relatively fixed target shapes, so small models are often enough, and annotating on “temperature matrix + mask” beats annotating pseudo-color images;
  • Features: for each connected hotspot region compute peak, mean, δ against the reference, area, aspect ratio, circularity — pattern features as computable numbers. “A small isolated round hotspot” and “a large blurry warm zone” take different criteria directly;
  • Registration and identification: a thermal image has temperatures but no semantics. Once thermal and visible frames are registered (dual-spectrum cores with factory parallax calibration handle this — the method is in step four of the embedded post), the visible channel identifies “this is a joint / an arrester / a module” and the right criterion applies — joints use relative difference, modules use cell morphology, arresters use the voltage-induced tables;
  • Grading and alarming: apply the DL/T 664 δ tables or custom thresholds to output a grade. Fire detection is a special case of this pipeline (step seven of the embedded post implemented one); swap the fire criterion for part-specific criteria and it becomes general defect detection. Alarms carry grade, thermal image, visible snapshot, and location to the NVR/platform, and humans review only the high grades;
  • Criteria evolution: feed false positives and misses back into the thresholds — absolute thresholds are brittle and relative criteria stable is the universal experience. A tier up sits the adaptive baseline (auto-thresholds from historical same-condition statistics), which presumes you archived the temperature matrices.

False-positive checklist: sun-warmed surfaces (afternoon re-radiation, unrelated to faults), reflection spots on low-emissivity surfaces, wind cooling real defects (the false-negative direction), wrong emissivity settings, “room temperature” readings through glass, and frozen frames during shutter calibration (the algorithm must skip those).

Closing

IR finds what heats wrong, UV finds what discharges wrong, spectra find what materials become — stack the three images and electrical, insulation, and material degradation each get their own alarm channel. And selection always runs backwards: first decide which faults to find and which standard’s criteria apply, then compute IFOV and sensitivity, and only then consider form factor, interfaces, and budget — criteria determine equipment, not the other way around.

References and further reading

Standards, by need: DL/T 664-2016 (electrical IR diagnosis — grading criteria and capture conditions), IEC TS 62446-3 (outdoor IR inspection of PV plants), IEC TS 60904-13 (PV EL inspection), IEC 61215 (module design qualification including hot-spot endurance). On the software side you don’t need professional suites to start: export the thermal image as a temperature matrix and numpy plus OpenCV handle thresholding, region growing, and dual-spectrum registration — every step of the pipeline is ordinary image processing; the feature-band ratios of multispectral (NDVI-style) are a few lines of code too.
