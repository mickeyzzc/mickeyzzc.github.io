2.4 GHz or 5 GHz: Band, Channel, and Traffic Engineering for CSI Acquisition

September 26, 2026 September 26, 2026 Physical World Development WiFi Sensing, CSI, ESP32, RF Selection, Channel Selection, HT20 Signals & Image Processing Series 3313 words 16 min read
🔊

The principles post covered the physics of CSI and the ESPectre post tore one open-source project down to its config knobs, but the moment you actually deploy a CSI node at home you face four decisions no paper writes down: should the node sit on 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz? Auto channel or manual? Is 20 MHz or 40 MHz better for sensing? And where does CSI come from when the environment goes quiet?

None of these is an algorithm question — all four are RF engineering. This post lays out the physics and the engineering consequences of each choice. Half the conclusions come from published measurement studies; the other half were validated on two node boards of my own: a sensing terminal built on a LuatOS ESP32-C3 with an Air101 LCD (single-core RISC-V, dedicated capture), and an environment station built on a Waveshare ESP32-S3-Zero (dual-core Xtensa, with temperature/humidity and on-device presence detection). Both boards run the same acquisition design, which also makes the compute-split story concrete.

Band: 5 GHz is not a better 2.4 GHz

Start with the question people get backwards: 5 GHz has more spectrum, more subcarriers, less interference — isn’t it strictly better for sensing? No. The band affects CSI through three separate ledgers.

Ledger one: sensitivity. 2.4 GHz has a wavelength of about 12.5 cm; 5 GHz about 6 cm. As the principles post showed, every half-wavelength of path change takes the CSI amplitude from peak to null. Halve the wavelength and you halve the distance over which the same motion cycles the channel: at 5 GHz a walking person flips the signal every 3 cm (versus 6 cm at 2.4 GHz), so the CSI series looks busier; Doppler doubles in step, moving a 1 m/s walk from roughly 16 Hz to 33 Hz. Fine motion benefits even more: a ±2 mm chest displacement produces about 25° of phase swing at 5 GHz — a bit over twice the 2.4 GHz figure of 11.6°. The same millimeter-scale motion reads twice as clearly on the phase layer at 5 GHz.

Ledger two: link budget. Free-space path loss grows with frequency — about 7 dB worse for 5 GHz at the same distance — and obstacles stack on top. Typical figures from deployment guides:

Obstacle2.4 GHz5 GHz
Drywall / wooden door3~4 dB5~7 dB
Brick wall6~8 dB10~12 dB
Concrete wall10~20 dB15~30 dB

(These are typical magnitudes from the literature and vendor deployment guides; thickness, moisture, and rebar all move them.)

That cuts the sensing range directly. Here is where the principles-post conclusion pays off: sensing range is set by whether the dynamic component — the human reflection — still clears the noise floor. A 7 dB weaker direct signal only lowers your data rate, but a 7 dB weaker dynamic component is 7 dB of range gone, and one brick wall compounds it. For millimeter-scale tasks like breathing monitoring, whose reliable range is only a few meters to begin with, 5 GHz shrinks it further.

The animation above puts both band ledgers in one room: at the same walking speed, the 5 GHz panel on the right has half the wavelength, so the person completes a full peak-to-null cycle every 3 cm and the amplitude trace below oscillates twice as fast as the 2.4 GHz one — that is the sensitivity ledger. Through the same brick wall, the right panel’s wave comes out far shorter — that is the link-budget ledger. Neither trace is better; the choice depends on whether the task needs range or fine-motion sensitivity.

Ledger three: environment. 2.4 GHz is crowded: Bluetooth hops across the whole band, microwave ovens radiate straight into 2450 MHz (more below), and Zigbee lives here too. 5 GHz is far cleaner — fewer interferers, fewer devices — at the price of a set of DFS channels that must yield to radar (more below as well).

Put the three ledgers together and the selection logic is clear: 2.4 GHz buys range and penetration; 5 GHz buys sensitivity and quiet spectrum. Motion detection and whole-home presence want 2.4 GHz. For line-of-sight breathing and heart-rate work, 5 GHz’s phase sensitivity is worth considering.

There is also a chip-level reality: every member of the ESP32 family — classic ESP32, S2, S3, C3, C6 — is 2.4 GHz only. The node in your hands has no choice today: configure it with a 5 GHz SSID and a C3’s log answers reason 202 (NO_AP_FOUND), a trap that wastes debugging time. The first dual-band chip in the family is the ESP32-C5 (2.4 + 5 GHz Wi-Fi 6), and CSI still flows through the same esp_wifi_set_csi interface — once it spreads, “dual-band CSI comparison in one room” becomes an experiment ordinary hobbyists can run.

Channel: CSI hangs on the channel, not on the room

With the band chosen, the next decision is the channel. A principle more fundamental than anything about bands: absolute CSI is not comparable across channels.

In the principles post’s channel equation H(f) = Σ β_l·e^(−j2πfτ_l), each path’s phase is set jointly by frequency and path delay. Change the channel — move the center frequency — and every path’s phase re-rolls at once. Channels 1 to 6 differ by 25 MHz; a reflection with 20 ns of excess delay swings by π, and constructive interference flips to destructive. Same room, nothing moved, channel changed — and all 56 complex numbers are replaced by a new set; the static baseline is void. CSI records “this room as seen at this frequency,” not “this room.”

The animation above is the entire physics of a channel switch: the solid blue line is the channel response the node calibrated on channel 1, the dashed line the static baseline it remembers; the moment the router moves to channel 6, the solid line snaps into a completely different shape — not drift, but every multipath phase re-rolled at once. The baseline stays pinned where it was and no longer fits, and for a while the detector sees nothing but violent motion. Fixing the router’s channel is exactly how you protect that curve’s continuity.

For a sensing node this yields a structural fact immediately: the node is a STA, and a STA sits wherever its AP sits — the node has no channel choice; controlling the channel means controlling the router.

When does a router change channels? Three sources, each verified in the wild:

  1. Auto channel selection (ACS): many routers rescan and switch at boot or on a nightly schedule. The user never notices; the sensing node just changed rooms in an instant.
  2. DFS radar retreat: DFS channels in 5 GHz (52~64 in China) must be vacated within 10 seconds of radar detection and stay unused for 30 minutes. A channel that moved once may move again.
  3. Band steering: dual-band routers with a shared SSID move STAs between 2.4 and 5 GHz — not a channel change but a band change plus a channel change, voiding the baseline twice over.

What happens after a switch? The static baseline dies; the detector reads the new channel’s first glance as violent motion and false-alarms for a while. A detector with an adaptive baseline relearns eventually, but during that window real and false events are indistinguishable. So the deployment discipline is two lines: fix the router’s channel manually, and recalibrate the node whenever it detects a jump. On my boards recalibration is an explicit operation — the C3 board’s center button triggers a 30-second empty-room recalibration, and the web UI offers a 60-second arm-and-leave countdown (calibration needs an empty room, but the person opening the page is usually inside — the countdown is how that deadlock gets broken). With the channel fixed, these recalibrations are rarely needed.

The channel also sets the RF environment itself. The 2.4 GHz map is worth memorizing: the non-overlapping 20 MHz channels are 1, 6, and 11; the microwave oven’s radiation centers on 2450 MHz, right in the territory of channels 8–11, with leakage spreading tens of MHz to each side — when the oven spins, amplitude noise rises across the whole CSI band, the classic “nobody moved yet motion fired” source. Put the sensing node’s router on the channel-1 side of the map; every channel of distance from the oven helps. Bluetooth coexists reasonably via adaptive frequency hopping; Zigbee channels 15/20/25/26 sit exactly in the gaps between 1/6/11 and normally do not collide. A neighbor’s co-channel AP is a subtler presence: if the node hears their frames, those frames produce CSI too — but that CSI encodes the neighbor’s room, which is contamination, not signal, for single-link presence detection; filter by BSS when parsing.

The 5 GHz terrain is simpler: in China, channels 36–48 and 149–165 need no DFS; 52–64 are DFS. With many non-overlapping channels, multi-channel stitching (RuView’s 168 virtual subcarriers from three channels) and channel-hopping acquisition both draw from here.

Bandwidth: is 20 MHz enough?

Third decision: bandwidth. Intuition says more subcarriers mean better sensing. The measured answer is far more interesting.

The theoretical gains first. HT20 gives 56 subcarriers, HT40 gives 114; range resolution Δr = c/(2B) tightens from 7.5 m to 3.75 m; the coherence bandwidth stays put (a few MHz, set by the environment’s delay spread), so the number of independent frequency observations doubles — the principles post described ESPectre’s 12 spread-out subcarriers as “independent witnesses”; HT40 doubles the witness pool.

Now the measured evidence. A 2023 systematic study by Cominelli, Gringoli, and Restuccia (controlled comparisons across environments on 802.11ac/ax) concluded: growing bandwidth from 20 to 80 and 160 MHz yields marginal accuracy gains, and in a small cluttered office 20 MHz was actually the best; the variables that contributed more were receive antenna count and subcarrier spacing (802.11ax’s 78.125 kHz versus 802.11ac’s 312.5 kHz). For home deployments this is good news: tasks like motion detection are nearly saturated at 20 MHz — the bottleneck is not bandwidth.

Forcing HT40 on 2.4 GHz carries an extra cost: one 40 MHz network covers nearly the entire band and overlaps channels 1, 6, and 11 simultaneously — in a crowded apartment building that means fighting three floors of neighbors at once; throughput suffers and sensing suffers more. This is why router vendors default 2.4 GHz to 20 MHz. ESP32 firmware adds a smaller implementation trap: the 128 subcarriers of HT40 are not “HT20’s 56 plus 56 more” — their frequency positions are remapped. Parsing code must follow the chip’s index table; slicing with 20 MHz assumptions produces misaligned “subcarriers.”

So the bandwidth decision is short: stay HT20 on 2.4 GHz; go to 5 GHz when you truly need bandwidth (80 MHz gives 242 subcarriers), provided the link-budget ledger from the band section still balances.

Traffic: no packets, no CSI

The principles post covered the three traffic strategies (passive listening, self-made multicast, active probing). Building it into real boards forces one concrete decision, and mine was a fourth variant: ping the gateway as stimulus. The node sends ten small probes per second toward the gateway; the gateway’s replies and the link-layer ACKs are all downlink unicast frames, each carrying an LTF, each producing a CSI record — and with dump_ack_en even the ACKs count. On quiet nights when the router has nothing else to carry, these ten self-made opportunities per second hold the sampling floor. It is less fuss than ESPectre’s self-made multicast (no multicast group to maintain) and far lighter than RuView’s mesh probing (a ping session is a few lines of code) — the best cost-benefit for a single-node home deployment.

The animation above turns “no packets, no CSI” into a sample stream: blue dots are sporadic ambient downlink frames, each carrying a free CSI record; orange dots are the node’s ten pings per second, whose replies and ACKs (green) are downlink unicast frames too and also produce CSI. On quiet nights when the router has nothing to carry, the steady rhythm of the sample stream at the bottom is held up entirely by that column of orange dots.

The other half of sampling-rate work is rate limiting: the CSI callback runs in the WiFi driver’s task and does exactly one thing — extract 16 subcarriers’ I/Q into a depth-64 queue and return immediately. A stream task drains the queue at a 50 packets/second ceiling, encoding serial-line frames and dropping the excess. The principles post mentioned the “callbacks never do heavy work” discipline in passing; in practice it matters more than expected: on a busy channel callbacks arrive hundreds of times per second, and any printing, floating-point math, or lock in the callback drags the whole stack down.

One counter-intuitive power-save trap deserves its own paragraph: to raise the sampling rate, instinct says disable WiFi power save (WIFI_PS_NONE) and keep the radio awake. Measured result: on a busy channel the single core saturates processing WiFi frames, the IDLE task starves, and the watchdog resets the board in a loop — the board looks dead because it is too diligent. Both boards ended up on the default MODEM power save, with stimulus filling the sampling-rate gap instead of brute-forcing it.

The two boards’ acquisition ledger

With physics and trade-offs settled, back to the boards. Their division of labor is itself the fourth engineering decision — beyond band, channel, and traffic — namely where the compute goes:

The C3, single-core at 160 MHz, cannot run any real DSP, so it owns only the left end of the chain: capture, rate limiting, serial output (#S1 text lines — 16 subcarriers’ I/Q plus RSSI and a timestamp per packet); all heavy computation lives in the PC-side sensing engine. The S3, dual-core at 240 MHz, has headroom: alongside the same capture chain it runs an on-device coarse presence estimator (amplitude frame-difference with dual-threshold hysteresis), so it still reports presence when the PC is gone. The acquisition design is shared across both chips; what differs is how much the board trusts itself versus what the PC adds.

Several parameters settled by experiment — more concrete than any generic advice:

ParameterValueRationale
Subcarrier extraction16 evenly spaced from [nsc/8, 7nsc/8]Skips guard bands and DC; spans coherence bands for independent observations; adapts to nsc, chip-independent at 20 MHz
CSI configL-LTF and HT-LTF both onTwo CSI opportunities per frame — free sampling rate
Stimulus10 Hz gateway ping + ACK CSIHolds the sampling floor in low-traffic hours
Rate cap50 ppsWalking Doppler is 16 Hz — 3× Nyquist headroom
Serial frame#S1 text lineParser is field-by-field contract; change one side, change the other

Subcarrier extraction is the coherence-bandwidth physics turned directly into engineering: of 56 subcarriers, a dozen adjacent ones see “the same change,” so 16 evenly spaced picks across the whole band (trimming 1/8 off each end to skirt guard bands and DC) are 16 independent witnesses. Write the logic once, adapt to nsc being 52 or 56, and the S3 board copied it verbatim — both boards emit identical #S1 lines, and one parser on the PC consumes both.

The last engineering fact: every board’s thresholds differ, and should. CSI statistics are set jointly by the room, the channel, the antenna position, and the board’s orientation; the same thresholds fail across rooms, across channels, even after rotating the board. The on-device coarse detector’s factory dual thresholds (motion enters above 25, exits below 12 — hysteresis, with 8 consecutive frames to enter and 16 to exit) are guesses; while the PC is connected, the engine samples the board’s motion metric every 5 seconds, compares it against its own DSP verdicts, learns this board’s personal enter/exit thresholds, pushes them back, and stores them in NVS — afterwards the board works standalone with its learned values. Learning lives on the PC for the same reason the DSP does: statistical learning needs long stretches of data, and that is the PC’s home turf.

Band buys range or sensitivity, the channel decides whether your baseline means anything, bandwidth is nearly saturated at 20 MHz, and ten pings per second hold the traffic floor — once the four decisions are made, what remains is the checklist’s discipline. Every parameter on the two boards came out of these rules, and validating them never required physics beyond them.
