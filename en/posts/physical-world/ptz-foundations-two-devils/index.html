The Two Devils of PTZ Cameras: Long-Focus Amplification and Dead Time

July 30, 2026 Physical World Dev PTZ, Gimbal, Stability, Latency, Control Theory Signals & Image Processing Series 1970 words 10 min read
🔊

If you’ve used a security PTZ dome long enough, you’ve probably seen these symptoms:

  • Rock-steady at wide angle, but a persistent jitter once you zoom into telephoto.
  • Preset recall is spot-on at wide angle, but off by half a screen at telephoto.
  • Joystick control always feels “half a beat behind” — you push, wait for the frame to change, but by the time it does, the gimbal has already overshot. So you correct, overshoot again, oscillate.
  • AI auto-tracking works fine at wide angle, but the target starts swinging left-right once you zoom in.

Same camera — why is it fine at wide angle and broken at telephoto? Why is joystick control never precise?

These symptoms look varied, but they boil down to two root causes. One is long focus linearly amplifying angular error into pixel error — invisible jitter at wide angle becomes glaring at telephoto. The other is an irreducible ~300 ms time gap in the closed loop — the frame you see always lags behind the gimbal’s true position, so you’re always operating on the past. The rest of this series revolves around suppressing these two devils.

Long Focus: An Amplifier for Everything

Let’s start with the first devil.

The Z (Zoom) in PTZ doesn’t just let you see farther — it simultaneously amplifies every angular error (mechanical backlash, wind load, motor cogging, control-loop residual) into pixel jitter on screen, linearly proportional to the magnification ratio. The smaller the field of view (FOV), the more pixels the same angular error occupies. The conversion is straightforward:

1

N_px = θ / FOV_H × W_px

θ is the angular error (degrees), FOV_H is the current horizontal field of view (degrees), and W_px is the image width (pixels). For example: at the 36x telephoto end, horizontal FOV is about 1.7°, image width is 2688 pixels, so 0.01° of angular jitter translates to:

1

0.01 / 1.7 × 2688 = 15.8 pixels

15.8 pixels means visibly jumping. And the same 0.01° jitter at wide angle (FOV 90°) is only 0.3 pixels — completely invisible.

This table maps the amplification effect across focal ranges:

ScenarioHorizontal FOVImage Width (px)0.01° jitter = how many pixels
Wide end (4 mm prime)90°26880.3 px (invisible)
30x dome telephoto2.3°268811.7 px (noticeable)
36x telephoto1.7°268815.8 px (unacceptable)
775 mm ultra-telephoto pointer0.5°268853.8 px (image jumping)

The conclusion is direct: any PTZ stability metric must be defined at the maximum focal length. A report that only gives wide-angle data is meaningless — a camera being “very stable” at the wide end proves nothing.

One more corollary worth remembering: at 36x telephoto, a single pixel corresponds to 1.7° / 2688 = 0.00063°. To achieve sub-pixel stability, residual angular jitter must be pressed below 6×10⁻⁴ degrees — into the single-digit arcsecond range. Can a standard stepper-plus-gear setup achieve this precision? That leads to the next question.

Resolution Is Not Accuracy

This is the most easily confused pair of concepts in PTZ selection.

Resolution is “how many steps can the motor divide into” — for example, a 0.9° stepper with 1/32 microstepping and a 30:1 gear ratio gives a theoretical resolution of 0.9° / (32 × 30) = 0.00094°. Astonishingly small.

Accuracy is “how much does it actually deviate when positioned” — mainstream dome preset repeatability is ±0.1°.

These differ by 100×. Why? Because accuracy is determined not by resolution but by backlash (gear mesh play during direction reversal), gravity droop (tilt-axis imbalance), structural stiffness (mount deformation), and thermal deformation combined. No matter how high the resolution, 0.1° of backlash is still 0.1°.

Real product data:

SpecDome (Hikvision DF6A)PTU (FLIR D48E)Heavy-duty (BIT-PT510)
Pan speed0.1–300°/s, preset 300–540°/s0.003–100°/s0.1–30°/s
Tilt speed0.1–150°/s, preset 250–400°/s0.003–50°/s0.1–15°/s
Preset repeatability±0.1°Worm-gear rigid design±0.1°, backlash <0.1°
Preset count256–300Programmable—

Note the speed range: 0.1°/s to 540°/s spans 3.7 orders of magnitude. A single drive strategy cannot cover this range — low speed demands smoothness (high microstepping, S-curve), high speed demands pulse frequency (low microstepping, trapezoidal), and the transition must be seamless. This is one of the core challenges of PTZ motion control, covered in the next post on stability.

A zero-cost trick for improving repeatability: single-direction approach. Always enter presets from the same direction — overshoot slightly then pull back, freezing backlash as a constant that can be calibrated out in software. This is industry-standard practice, requiring no hardware changes.

Dead Time: You’re Always Operating on the Past

The second devil is more subtle, but anyone who has controlled a PTZ with a joystick has felt it.

Here’s what happens after you push the joystick, in chronological order: you push → the command travels through the control protocol to the camera (a few to tens of milliseconds) → the camera’s main controller processes it and sends it to the driver → the motor starts turning → the gimbal actually begins to move. The time from command issuance to actual gimbal start is called dead time in control theory.

Academic measurements (AXIS 233D / Q6034 domes) put it at: dead time from command to actual gimbal start is approximately 300 ms, independent of movement amplitude; a 20° pan-tilt takes 400–550 ms end-to-end.

But the real killer isn’t the 300 ms itself — it’s the combination with video-chain latency. A PTZ is a closed-loop system: the operator (or AI tracking) watches the frame → sends a command → the gimbal moves → the frame changes → watch again. Three delay segments are strung through this loop:

1

Video chain latency τ_video (200~500 ms) + Control chain latency τ_ctrl (5~150 ms) + Mechanical dead time τ_mech (≈300 ms)

The frame the operator sees lags the gimbal’s true position by a full 300 ms. So:

  1. Operator sees the target hasn’t reached center → keeps pushing;
  2. The gimbal has already passed the target;
  3. After releasing the joystick, the gimbal is still “catching up” to inertia;
  4. Target crosses the center → operator reverses → oscillation.

This is structurally identical to the “over-digging” problem in teleoperated excavators — a pure dead-time stability problem.

Dead time is the worst type of element in control theory. Ordinary delay (like first-order inertia) at least changes the magnitude response, so you can compensate with gain; dead time only consumes phase margin without changing the magnitude — you cannot tune it away with PID parameters. This is why:

  • Operators always “overshoot” with the joystick, hunting back and forth;
  • AI auto-tracking oscillates left-right at the telephoto end (called “limit cycle” in control theory);
  • Increasing control gain for faster response leads directly to divergence.

The AI tracking post will cover Kalman filter forward prediction and Smith predictors for dealing with dead time. For now, remember: dead time is a hard constraint — PID cannot tune it away.

Three PTZ Form Factors

Now that we understand the two devils, let’s look at PTZ hardware. The market has three main form factors, each targeting different scenarios:

Box PTZBroadcast/conference, interchangeable lensExternal dual-axis bracket, Tilt often single-side cantilever; interchangeable lens (e.g., Sony FR7 full-frame E-mount), prioritizing low-speed smoothnessBroadcast, live, conference, studio
PTU (Pan-Tilt Unit)FLIR PTU-D48E: payload 6.8 kg, self-weight 5.4 kg, IP67, −30–70°CWorm-gear rigid shaft; power-off self-locking; Pan resolution 0.006° / Tilt 0.003°Military/police aiming, long-focus precision tracking, heavy-duty

The dome is the most common security form — integrated sealing, low cost, good protection, 360° continuous rotation via slip ring. Box PTZ leans broadcast, where the core requirement is smooth movement (Sony FR7 down to 0.02°/s), not sweep speed. PTU is a “separate gimbal + camera” design with the highest stiffness and precision, but larger size and higher cost, used for precision aiming and positioning.

System Diagram: Four Chains

Inside a PTZ, four chains run in parallel: optical, video, control, and electromechanical. Their relationship is the map for understanding all PTZ problems.

The most critical insight from this diagram is the severe delay asymmetry between the video chain and the control chain: video is ~300 ms, control is ~10 ms — a 30× difference. The operator sends commands through the fast control chain (10 ms), but sees results through the slow video chain (300 ms later). This asymmetry is itself the root cause of overshoot — the dead-time problem discussed earlier is fundamentally this delay gap.

The control chain also has fast and slow protocols: VISCA over IP is ~2–10 ms end-to-end (UDP direct), while ONVIF PTZ is ~30–150 ms (SOAP/XML parsing overhead). The latency post will break down each protocol.

Broadcast vs. Security: Two Opposing Design Philosophies

If you take a security dome and try to use it for broadcast, or take a broadcast PTZ for security, you’ll likely crash and burn. These two scenarios have almost completely opposite PTZ requirements.

DimensionBroadcast / Live / ConferenceSecurity / Surveillance
Speed targetUltra-low-speed smooth (Sony FR7 down to 0.02°/s, Bolin 0.08°/s)Ultra-high-speed sweep (preset 240–400°/s)
Typical peak speed60°/sJog 160–210°/s, preset 250–400°/s
Core requirementCamera movement must be flawless; the frame is the final productArrive at preset fast to capture; in-transit frames can be blurry
Drive orientationLow-cogging direct drive / FOC, high microstep, timing belt silentHigh gear ratio, high torque, worm gear self-locking wind resistance
Latency targetGlass-to-glass < 100 ms (switcher sync)< 300–400 ms acceptable (YD/T 1171 Class 1)
ProtocolsVISCA / VISCA over IP / NDI / SRT / FreeD / ST 2110ONVIF / Pelco-D/P / vendor proprietary SDK
StabilizationOIS 2-axis + Roll EIS (e.g., Panasonic AW-UE160)Gyro GIS + OIS hybrid (Hikvision)

Speed differs by an order of magnitude — broadcast 60°/s vs. security 400°/s. Drive solutions are opposite — broadcast wants silent smoothness (timing belt, direct drive), security wants wind-resistant self-locking (worm gear). Even latency requirements differ by 3× — broadcast needs <100 ms for switcher sync, security accepts 300 ms because frame-level sync isn’t required.

Selection rule: a broadcast gimbal capable of 0.02°/s smooth movement typically cannot do 360°/s sweeps, and vice versa. This is not vendor corner-cutting — it is the physical constraint of motor-gear ratio-inertia matching. A high gear ratio gives you torque and self-locking but sacrifices top speed; direct drive gives speed but zero holding torque — power off means position lost. You can only pick one side.

What This Series Covers

These two devils — long-focus amplification and dead time — run through every aspect of PTZ engineering. The following posts address them:

  • Four-layer stability defense — from mechanical stiffening to IMU feedforward, progressively suppressing jitter. If the mechanical layer isn’t done right, all the control and algorithms are just patching holes.
  • Full-chain latency breakdown — how many milliseconds each encoder, packetizer, and jitter buffer contributes, what can be optimized, and what’s a protocol hard limit. Multi-second latency is 99% from the packetizer and player buffer.
  • AI auto-tracking — how gain scheduling solves telephoto oscillation (30× gain difference), and what engineering tools exist for dead-time compensation (Kalman forward prediction, Smith predictor, inertial inner-loop cascade).
  • Industry problem map — the current status and boundaries of 19 pain points including WDR, thermal management, cybersecurity (CVE-2025-65856), privacy compliance, and edge AI.
  • PTZR roll axis — when a PTZ is mounted on a vehicle, ship, or drone, a two-axis gimbal structurally cannot keep the horizon level. The fourth degree of freedom (Roll) solves this — at what cost?
