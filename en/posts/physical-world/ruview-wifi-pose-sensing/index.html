RuView: WiFi-Based Pose Estimation and Contactless Vital Signs

September 6, 2026 September 7, 2026 Physical World Development WiFi Sensing, CSI, Signal Processing, Pose Estimation, Edge AI, ESP32 Signals & Image Processing Series 3214 words 16 min read
🔊

The previous post covered ESPectre, which uses WiFi CSI to answer “is anyone moving in the room?” RuView wants more: not just motion, but 17 body keypoints, breathing rate, heart rate, and through-wall sensing, all decoded from the same signals. It is written in Rust by ruvnet (the author of claude-flow), MIT-licensed, and with 92,000+ stars as of September 2026 it is the hottest open-source project in this space.

The conclusion first, so you don’t over-expect: this is a beta-stage research platform. The gap between the pose model’s benchmark-dataset score and its on-device score is large, and APIs and firmware are still moving. It still deserves a serious write-up for two reasons. First, the pipeline is a textbook-grade WiFi-sensing architecture — from multi-band fusion to vital-sign extraction, every stage carries something transferable to other sensing problems. Second, the project labels “which numbers are real and which are scaffolding” in an explicit three-tier maturity table — that level of honest self-labeling is rare in open-source AI projects.

Architecture: from one-dimensional signal to spatial awareness

mermaid
flowchart TD
    MESH["ESP32 mesh<br/>4-6 nodes"] --> FUSE["Multistatic fusion<br/>168 virtual subcarriers"]
    FUSE --> DSP["Signal processing<br/>filtering / AoA / Fresnel"]
    DSP --> VITAL["Breathing & heart rate<br/>bandpass + zero-crossing"]
    DSP --> NN["Self-supervised encoder<br/>128-dim fingerprint"]
    NN --> OUT["Pose & room<br/>identification"]

After fusion the data flow splits in two: the signal path goes straight into DSP, where breathing and heart rate are extracted with pure filtering and no neural network; the learning path feeds processed features into the self-supervised encoder, which produces pose and room fingerprints. Both paths share the fused front end — and the split itself is informative: “vitals” and “pose” place very different demands on signal quality, one needing only one-dimensional statistics, the other needing spatial structure.

Where ESPectre needs one board listening to one router — motion detection only asks “is there disturbance” — RuView needs spatial structure, and a single link does not carry enough information. Step one is adding density.

TDM multi-node mesh

4-6 ESP32 nodes form a mesh and take turns probing on channels 1, 6, and 11 via time-division multiplexing (TDM) — only one node transmits on one channel at any instant, so the nodes never interfere with themselves. This answers the synchronization question as a side effect: per-node motion detection needs no synchronization at all; only phase-level fusion does — espargos’s phased array solves it in hardware with a shared clock plus a phase reference signal, while RuView sidesteps hardware sync with TDM slot scheduling in software. Each channel carries 56 subcarriers; one round across three channels yields 168 “virtual subcarriers” per link, tripling the effective sensing bandwidth.

Why does bandwidth matter? Spatial resolution in sensing roughly scales inversely with bandwidth — a 20 MHz channel cannot distinguish path-length differences below about 1.5 m, and wider bandwidth means more sensitivity to “where exactly is the object”. There is also a free bonus: while the mesh hops across channels, the neighbors’ routers broadcast there too, and their signals cross your rooms and get perturbed by your body just the same — other people’s WiFi works as free radar illuminators, extra viewpoints at zero cost.

In the animation the time axis is divided into slots, blue/green/orange for channels 1/6/11, with the black frame advancing along the axis to mark the currently active capture. The three nodes take turns sampling on different channels; by the end of each round, the fusion layer holds three independent “views” of the same scene.

Multistatic fusion

N nodes give N×(N-1) transmit-receive links: A→B, B→A, A→C… Each link is a “radar” viewing the same person from a different geometric position. The fusion layer uses attention to weight and merge these cross-viewpoint embeddings — links with good signal quality and complementary views get high weight, occluded or interfered links are automatically downweighted. The essence: turning several monostatic radars into one multistatic radar network.

How much multistatic fusion buys you depends on how the nodes are placed. The principle is one line: areas of high link density are areas of high sensitivity, and the target’s activity zone should sit where node link lines cross. A few corollaries: nodes spread around the target zone beat a straight line or everything bunched on one side of the router — a collinear layout has zero resolving power in the perpendicular direction; stagger the heights to give the fusion layer a vertical dimension; for larger homes, add density at roughly one node per 50-70 m² rather than hoping a single node sees farther.

The signal-processing layer

After fusion, the signals enter classic DSP territory. Besides the familiar Hampel filter, three names deserve unpacking:

SpotFi (angle of arrival). The classic MIT algorithm from 2015: the same signal reaches each antenna of an array with a tiny phase offset. Arrange those offsets across the “subcarrier × antenna” dimensions into a matrix, run MUSIC super-resolution spectrum estimation, and you can separate the direct path from the multipath morass and estimate its angle of arrival (AoA) — informally, work out “which direction is the reflector in”. RuView uses it to lock the direct path and estimate signal direction.

Fresnel zones (through-wall geometry). This is the physics behind sensing position through a wall — substantial enough for its own section below.

BVP vital-sign extraction. Breathing and heart rate take the pure-signal route, no neural network — also below.

Through-wall sensing geometry: Fresnel zones

Fix a transmitter and a receiver. All points in space where the “transmit → reflection point → receive” path is longer than the direct path by n·λ/2 form a family of ellipses with the two radios as foci — the nth elliptical ring is the nth Fresnel zone. Reflected waves from the first zone add nearly in phase with the direct wave; a body entering the first zone perturbs the signal most; zones two and three perturb it in different patterns. Read backwards: measuring the pattern of disturbance tells you which zone the body occupies — that is the geometric basis of through-wall localization, with no “imaging through the wall” required.

The first-zone radius has a closed-form formula: r₁ = √(λ·d₁·d₂/(d₁+d₂)), where d₁ and d₂ are the distances from the reflection point to each radio. To feel the scale, plug in numbers: at 2.4 GHz, λ = 12.5 cm, and at the midpoint of a 5 m link r₁ = √(0.125 × 2.5 × 2.5 / 5) ≈ 40 cm. The “position-sensitive core” of an ESP32-plus-router link spanning 5 meters is an ellipsoid under one meter across — which explains why a single node’s spatial resolution is limited, and why RuView’s documented through-wall range tops out around 5 m and depends heavily on signal conditions.

In the animation, the three translucent ellipses are the first three Fresnel zones, and the person crosses them vertically: near the axis (zone one), the signal fade in the lower curve is deepest; off-axis in zones two and three, the fade shallows and changes shape. The red marker rides the curve to show the current fade depth. Fade depth maps one-to-one to body position — that is the signal-level explanation of “knowing where you are through a wall”.

Vital signs: breathing in the phase

Breathing is chest movement, typically 0.1-0.5 Hz (6-30 breaths per minute); heart rate falls in 0.8-2.0 Hz (40-120 BPM). The previous post noted that amplitude is nearly blind to millimeter motion while phase is not — here is the full arithmetic. Chest movement of ±2 mm changes the two-way reflection path by 4 mm; at 2.4 GHz that swings the phase by 2π × 0.004 / 0.125 ≈ 0.2 rad ≈ 11.6° peak-to-peak — measurable on every packet, with entirely adequate SNR.

The extraction pipeline is textbook signal processing: bandpass the CSI phase at 0.1-0.5 Hz so only the breathing component remains, then count zero crossings and convert to BPM. Two engineering details matter. First, phase wrapping: phase lives on ±π, and adjacent packets can jump from +π to −π; filtering wrapped values directly creates fake spikes, so RuView works with wrapped phase and circular statistics (circular variance). Second, heart rate is harder: the 0.8-2.0 Hz band carries a signal an order of magnitude weaker than breathing, polluted by breathing harmonics — which is why the README states heart rate’s credible range more conservatively than breathing’s.

The three-panel animation runs left to right: raw CSI phase, where the breathing sinusoid is nearly invisible inside noise; after a 0.1-0.5 Hz bandpass, only the breathing component remains (about 0.2 Hz — twelve breaths per minute); on the right, zero crossings (red dots) are counted and converted to BPM. The neural network never appears — vital-sign monitoring is pure filtering and counting.

The ceiling of this filtering chain is SNR, and SNR decays with distance — a single-link breathing setup stays reliable within roughly 1-5 m. The literature has a ready-made idea for pushing out: FarSense (IMWUT 2019) skips raw single-link CSI and takes the ratio of two antennas’ CSI — noise common to both antennas (hardware offsets, most of the static background) cancels in the ratio, leaving the motion component, and breathing monitoring reached 8 m. RuView’s multistatic fusion is the same idea scaled up: N×(N-1) links serve as references for one another, pulling the motion-correlated component out of the environmental background.

The other detections have their own signal-level recipes: fall detection uses a phase-acceleration threshold with 3-frame debounce and a 5 s cooldown; person counting uses adaptive P95 normalization with a tunable dedup factor. Plain combinations of “one physical quantity + one threshold set” — reliable, and fast enough to run in real time on an ESP32.

The models: which numbers are real

This is, to me, the most valuable part of the whole repo. The README carries a section titled “Model weights: what’s real, what’s not” that sorts the models into three tiers by evidence:

TierModelMeasured numbers
ValidatedCSI encoder + presence head (on Hugging Face)82.3% held-out temporal-triplet accuracy (self-supervised)
Validated17-keypoint pose model on the MM-Fi datasettorso-PCK@20 = 82.69%, ensemble 83.59%, above the prior SOTA

First, PCK (Percentage of Correct Keypoints): the fraction of predicted keypoints falling within a radius (here 20 pixels) of ground truth, a standard pose-estimation metric; torso joints are scored separately.

The self-supervised line: what 82.3% means

The encoder is trained with contrastive learning, requiring zero human labels. Training samples are “temporal triplets”: one CSI frame as the anchor, temporally adjacent frames as positives, temporally distant frames as negatives — neighboring frames of the same motion should sit close together in embedding space, distant frames of different motions should sit apart. The 82.3% held-out temporal-triplet accuracy is the model’s success rate at that discrimination on unseen data (raw CSI manages only 66.4% — the encoder learned a better representation).

The resulting 128-dim vector is a “room fingerprint”: different rooms, activities, and anomalies map to different locations, so room identification, anomaly detection, and activity classification all fall out as by-products. Deployment is equally restrained: the 4-bit quantized model fits in 8 KB (the q4 variant is the recommended one), plus a per-room MicroLoRA adapter of 1,792 parameters for environment tuning — it trains only a pair of low-rank matrices, never the backbone. The whole stack is about 55 KB, comfortably inside the ESP32’s 520 KB of memory. The per-room tuning is not decoration: cross-environment generalization is a widely acknowledged hard problem of WiFi sensing — the channel baseline shifts with the room, even with furniture placement, and a model moved to a new environment needs recalibration.

The pose line: benchmark versus device

MM-Fi is a public WiFi-sensing benchmark (multi-person, multi-room CSI captured alongside visual keypoints across many actions), and 82.69% torso-PCK@20 was measured under its fixed split protocol — a genuine result, ten points above the prior SOTA MultiFormer at 72.25%.

But moving the model onto a real “one ESP32 + single antenna + 56 subcarriers” stream drops PCK@20 to 3.0%. The project’s own attribution is precise and worth restating: research NICs like the Intel 5300 are 3×3 MIMO, and their nine “antenna-pair × subcarrier” groups supply the rich spatial dimensions that pose decoding feeds on. An ESP32 single-antenna SISO stream has only the frequency dimension, and 56 subcarriers simply do not contain the fine-grained spatial information. On the real device the coarse torso structure survives (right hip PCK@50 reaches 77%) while distal limbs and face keypoints are near-random — the signal carries no such detail. So the honest statement of “real-time WiFi pose” is: SOTA on the benchmark, below target on-device, and the project explicitly asks that no summary drop that caveat. This habit of labeling what can and cannot be boasted about is worth more than the numbers themselves.

While we are at it, the backstory of this gap deserves completing: the star papers of this field never ran on household hardware. MIT’s Wi-Vi (2013, through-wall motion detection), WiTrack (2014, through-wall 3D tracking), RF-Capture (2015, antenna-array body-outline capture), and RF-Pose (2018, through-wall 2D skeletons) were all built on dedicated software-defined radios or custom FMCW radar platforms operating in the 2.4/5.8 GHz ISM bands, with hardware costing tens to hundreds of times an ESP32 node. “Reproducing paper results on ten-dollar hardware” was never the premise of those works; what RuView deserves credit for is quantifying the gap and putting it on the table instead of pretending it does not exist.

Note the hardware floor: RuView explicitly does not support ESP32-C3 or the classic ESP32 (single cores cannot carry the CSI DSP) — the exact opposite of ESPectre’s full-family support. The research-grade route also accepts 3×3 MIMO NICs like the Intel 5300 or Atheros AR9580 (about $50-100 — the spatial-dimension advantage described above lives there), and any laptop can run RSSI-only coarse presence. One rung further out: flash the nexmon_csi firmware patch on a Raspberry Pi 3B+/4B and intercept CSI straight from the Broadcom chip’s channel-estimation table at up to 80 MHz of bandwidth — the academic standard tool for high-bandwidth CSI, at the cost of firmware reverse-engineering and specific old hardware.

Pretrained weights come from Hugging Face: huggingface-cli download ruvnet/wifi-densepose-pretrained. To check whether a deployment is trustworthy, the repo ships a deterministic verification: python archive/v1/data/proof/verify.py replays a fixed pipeline against a published SHA-256 hash — no VERDICT: PASS, no credibility.

Smart-home integration: Home Assistant connects with a single --mqtt flag via MQTT auto-discovery; Apple Home, Google Home, Alexa, and SmartThings connect through a Matter bridge. Each node exposes 21 entities — 11 raw signals plus 10 semantic states (someone-sleeping, possible-distress, bathroom-occupied, elderly-inactivity-anomaly, and so on), with 3 starter HA Blueprints. The semantic states are a rule layer above raw signals, each with its own precision/recall report.

Privacy gets a dedicated BFLD layer (Beamforming Feedback Layer): raw beamforming feedback never leaves the node, identity embeddings live in RAM only, cross-site correlation is made cryptographically infeasible with per-day-rotated keyed hashes (BLAKE3), and an Ed25519 witness chain provides tamper-evident attestation for measurement records. A WiFi sensing device is by nature a sensor that can see your daily patterns — this project treats that with engineering rather than leaving it in a disclaimer.

The official use-case table is long, from elderly fall monitoring and sleep-apnea screening to warehouse robots avoiding humans behind shelving. Every row carries the same boundary: research/prototype use, not a medical device, and vital-sign outputs need independent validation on the exact hardware, room, and population — that sentence is verbatim from the README, not me being modest on its behalf.

Industry progress helps calibrate expectations. Origin AI, the company that invented commercial WiFi motion sensing, was acquired by security giant ADT in February 2026 for about $170 million; Canada's Cognitive Systems licenses its WiFi Motion to ISP platforms like Plume, shipped in the gateways of over a hundred operators; and on the standards front, IEEE 802.11bf-2025 was published in September 2025, writing WLAN Sensing into the protocol proper. RuView itself is walking toward a commercial ecosystem — a companion Cognitum Seed persistence node puts a full system at about $140, with a module store on the software side. Against that backdrop the gap is clear: open ESP32 platforms and commercial systems differ not in principle but in dedicated firmware, room-scale data volume, and whole-network coordination — exactly the things a home lab cannot supply.

How it compares with ESPectre

The two projects share the same physics (ESP32 CSI) and take opposite engineering directions:

ESPectreRuView
Positioningsingle-room motion sensorspatial-sensing research platform
Maturityproduction-ready (3.0.0-rc1)beta; APIs and firmware will change
Outputmotion state + movement scorepose, vitals, room fingerprints, occupancy
Accuracy evidencepublic performance report, split by chip/linktiered honest labeling; on-device pose below target
Hardwareall ESP32 variants (incl. C3/classic)ESP32-S3 / C6 and research NICs only
LicenseGPLv3 + commercial dual licenseMIT
On-ramp costfive minutes in a browserDocker or a Rust toolchain

For a stable presence sensor driving home automation, pick ESPectre — it works out of the box. For studying the full WiFi-sensing stack, reproducing paper-grade pipelines, or experimenting with robot spatial awareness, RuView’s codebase and 200+ ADRs are ready-made teaching material. Hardware costs on both sides are in the ten-dollar range: run ESPectre first to get a feel for CSI, then dive into RuView’s deep end — the path flows naturally.
