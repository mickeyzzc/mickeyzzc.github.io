The Edge AI Inflection: New Approaches to Motion Detection and Face Recognition Once NPUs Became Standard

August 8, 2026 Physical World Development Computer Vision, Edge Computing, NPU, AI Hardware, Face Recognition, Motion Detection, Selection Signals & Image Processing Series 2203 words 11 min read
🔊

This is the sequel to “Motion Detection & Face Recognition: The Resource–Accuracy Trade-off.” That post’s central tension was compute scarcity — spend it carefully with frame differencing and ViBe, sprinkle in lightweight AI only when needed. This post tells the opposite story: once NPUs/TPUs move from optional to standard and compute stops being scarce, how does the playbook change.

The shift is real: a few years ago an edge IPC had only a CPU and you hoarded every cycle with background subtraction; now even cheap IPC SoCs ship a 6-TOPS NPU (Rockchip RK3588), Hailo-8 gives you 26 TOPS for 2.5W, and the Jetson Orin Nano Super — a firmware unlock on the same silicon — pushes sparse compute to 67 TOPS at $249. Detection is cheap enough to “always run,” so the two-tier “classical ROI gate, then AI” pipeline from the previous post begins to invert.

New freedom brings new traps, though. This post walks the 2024–2026 reality in order — hardware, algorithm–hardware co-design, latest models, toolchains, the architectural shift, and selection — and clearly separates independently reproduced numbers from vendor marketing.

A few things to state up front:

  • Stop choosing the Coral Edge TPU. Google quietly retired it in 2025, pivoting to an open-source Coral NPU built with Synaptics; Frigate, the biggest downstream project, is already discussing removing it from docs. Don’t spec it for new designs.
  • There’s a memory-bandwidth wall between advertised TOPS and real throughput. TOPS = peak MACs × freq × 2, assuming ideal batching. The real bottleneck is usually memory bandwidth at batch-1 (mobile NPUs typically sit behind 64–137 GB/s). For small detectors (YOLOv8n, SCRFD-0.5G) compute really is the bottleneck, so TOPS is meaningful and NPU offload genuinely pays off; for large Transformers / big recognition backbones it’s weight-fetch-bound and TOPS often goes unmet. Hold this rule and every “benchmark” below becomes readable.
  • Vendors that publish only relative gains (Qualcomm 8 Elite) or bundle NPU+CPU+GPU into one number (MediaTek) are marketing — treat as such, not as hard figures.

Algorithm–hardware co-design: the same YOLO meets different fates on different NPUs

This is the easiest trap in practice: the same model flies on a GPU and crawls on an NPU. The reason is that an NPU’s operator coverage, quantization path, and memory model are entirely different from a GPU’s.

The decisive factors:

  • Ops NPUs dislike accelerating: deformable convolution usually has no NPU kernel and falls back to a slow path; depthwise / group conv support varies wildly — the single biggest cause of “fast on GPU, crawls on NPU,” which is exactly what MobileNetV4 and FastViT are designed around; Transformer attention / LayerNorm / softmax support is uneven, which is why attention-heavy YOLOv12/v13 and RT-DETR lose to YOLO11 on commodity NPUs.
  • Activations: swish/sigmoid need exponentials, expensive on NPU, so the industry standardizes on hardswish / ReLU6 (the MobileNetV3 convention).
  • Quantization: production defaults to PTQ (post-training + calibration); if INT8 isn’t enough, mixed precision (Qualcomm QNN exposes A8W8 and A16W4 — 8-bit activation/8-bit weight, or 16-bit/4-bit), and if that still drops accuracy, QAT. Weights per-channel, activations per-tensor.
  • 2:4 structured sparsity is Nvidia-only: Ampere/Tensor Cores have mma.sp instructions (this is exactly how Jetson Orin doubles its sparse TOPS), but the measured speedup is ~1.27×, not 2×. Mobile NPUs (Hexagon, RK, Hailo, ANE) have no equivalent — sparsity is not a general-purpose edge lever today.
  • Selection rule of thumb: on a small 6-TOPS NPU, pick a model whose INT8 weight footprint clearly fits in available SRAM, or you stall on DMA and never reach the advertised TOPS.

Face detection

SCRFD remains the InsightFace default, tiered 0.5G / 2.5G / 10G / 34G — edge uses 0.5G/2.5G for speed, small/far faces use 10G/34G. No real successor in 2024–2026; SCRFD + RetinaFace still define the field.

Two misperceptions to avoid: the “FaceX-ViT” circulating online is not a standalone paper (it conflates the FaceX-Zoo toolbox with ViT backbones); and YOLOv12/v13 are often misattributed to Ultralytics — their current line is YOLO11 / YOLO26.

Citable scale for small recognizers today: EdgeFace is the sub-2M competition winner; AdaFace/MagFace losses are the common training objectives; for commercial licensing, pick AuraFace (ArcFace-class, from fal.ai, open commercial license).

Mobile backbones (the skeleton under detection/recognition)

MobileNetV4 (2024; UIB universal inverted bottleneck, pure CNN, NPU-safe), FastViT (structural reparameterization — complex at train time, folds to plain conv at deploy, purpose-built to bridge research→NPU deploy), EfficientFormer (NeurIPS 2022; ~2.2× faster than PoolFormer on an iPhone NPU). These routes land Transformer benefits on an NPU far more reliably than a vanilla ViT.

The architectural shift: once detection is nearly free

This is the change the post is really about. The previous post’s two-tier pipeline — “classical ROI gate, then AI” — was motivated by AI being expensive. When detection is cheap enough to always run, that logic partially inverts — but background subtraction isn’t replaced; it’s repositioned as “motion gating for multi-stream setups.”

The key judgment: detection is nearly free “per stream,” not “per pixel across many streams.” A 6-TOPS box serving 8 cameras can’t run a full-image detector at 30fps on every stream — so background subtraction / frame diffing acts as a zero-cost motion gate, deciding which streams and frames deserve the detector; the detector then decides which ROIs deserve the recognizer. It becomes a three-tier cascade: motion gate (CPU/ISP) → face detection (NPU, motion frames only) → recognition (NPU, faces only). This is the dominant multi-stream IPC shape of 2024–2026, and it echoes the earlier hybrid pipeline, except the three tiers now sit across CPU/ISP and NPU and multiplex across streams.

Two more shifts:

  • On-device learned optical flow becomes usable: RAFT is too heavy, but EdgeFlowNet (arXiv 2411.14576) delivers dense optical flow at 1W / 100fps, making “learned flow” practical on tiny robots/cameras for the first time.
  • Temporal/action recognition is not yet truly at the edge: full video Transformers (TimeSformer, VideoMAE, tubelet encoders) still exceed a sub-10W NPU budget in 2026. What’s actually deployable: two-stream RGB+flow with a light backbone; or “per-frame detection + heuristic tracking (IoU) + temporal rule aggregation” (the de-facto IPC approach — detect→track→count/loiter/zone rules on CPU); or small clip CNNs (X3D) on Jetson-class hardware. Treat any “temporal Transformer at the edge” pitch as vendor claim.

How to read benchmarks + platform recommendation stack

One ground rule for reading these numbers: always ask for the conditions — how many cores, what precision, raw inference or full decode+postprocess. Here are the common traps, then the stack.

  • RK3588: YOLOv8n/YOLO11n INT8 @640 is ~33–48fps on one core, ~52fps on three. A number without core count is meaningless. Authoritative table: airockchip/rknn_model_zoo.
  • Hailo-8: a full SCRFD + recognition pipeline is ~3–18ms/inference (Seeed); but the “105fps” floating around is a synthetic hailortbench number on a trivial op — real YOLOv8m multistream is only 8–17fps per stream.
  • Jetson Orin Nano Super: YOLO end-to-end (decode+postprocess) is ~47fps; the “INT8 189fps” is raw model in an optimized C++/TensorRT path, not a usable camera pipeline.
  • Qualcomm Hexagon: no independently reproducible YOLO fps; the vendor publishes only relative gains ("+98% / +45% perf-per-watt") — don’t invent a specific fps.
