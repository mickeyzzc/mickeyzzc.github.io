Turn an ESP32 into a WiFi Motion Sensor: ESPectre Principles and Deployment

September 6, 2026 September 6, 2026 Physical World Development WiFi Sensing, CSI, Signal Processing, ESP32, ESPHome, Home Assistant Signals & Image Processing Series 3024 words 15 min read
🔊

If you want to know whether someone is moving in a room, the two off-the-shelf options are both awkward. Cameras raise privacy problems and are off-limits in bedrooms and bathrooms. PIR sensors rely on infrared, fail behind a glass cabinet, and false-trigger on air-conditioning drafts. ESPectre takes a different angle: the room is already full of WiFi signals, and human movement disturbs them — so just read the change in the signal. It turns a ten-dollar ESP32 into a motion sensor with no camera, no microphone, and no dedicated mmWave radar; the router is the one you already own.

The project is by Francesco Pace, trended on the Hacker News front page, and sits at 9,300+ stars. As of writing (September 2026) the latest release is 3.0.0-rc1. The license is GPLv3, with a separate commercial license for closed-source use.

Honestly, I was skeptical of WiFi sensing at first — it smelled like a well-produced demo. Reading the algorithm doc (ALGORITHMS.md), the feature ledger, and the ADRs changed my mind: every design decision comes with evidence — why these features, why exactly 12 subcarriers, how the threshold adapts — each backed by an ADR and an auto-generated performance report. This post walks three layers: the physics and signal theory of CSI (why body motion ends up written into WiFi signals), ESPectre’s pipeline and detector math, and finally deployment and limits.

WiFi CSI: how body motion gets written into the signal

OFDM and subcarriers

On 2.4 GHz, WiFi 4 uses a 20 MHz channel that is not one solid block of spectrum: OFDM (orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing) splits it into 64 orthogonal subcarriers of 312.5 kHz each. The transmitter spreads data bits across them in parallel. “Orthogonal” is not decoration: the subcarrier spacing equals the reciprocal of the symbol length, so neighboring subcarriers integrate to zero over one symbol and do not interfere — that is what lets an FFT separate them cleanly.

Not all 64 subcarriers are usable: the band edges keep guard bands to avoid leaking into adjacent channels, and the center (DC) subcarrier stays null because of carrier leakage. Count them: 4 low-edge, 1 center, 3 high-edge removed — 56 usable subcarriers remain. Each one is an independent narrowband channel that travels the same physical path, yet reacts differently to how that path changes the signal — and that difference becomes the sensing information.

From a received packet to a set of CSI

How does the receiver learn what the channel did to each subcarrier? From a known sequence in the preamble. Every 802.11n packet carries an LTF (Long Training Field) in its PHY header — an agreed-upon known OFDM symbol. After the receiver’s FFT, it computes one division per subcarrier:

text
1

H(k) = Y(k) / X(k)

X(k) is the known training symbol on subcarrier k, Y(k) is what actually arrived, and the quotient H(k) is that subcarrier’s channel frequency response — a complex number whose magnitude says how much the subcarrier was attenuated or amplified and whose phase says how much it was delayed. One packet yields one complex value per subcarrier, 56 in total: the CSI (Channel State Information).

In other words, CSI is not an extra measurement — it is an intermediate quantity the WiFi chip already computes to demodulate every packet, then normally throws away. ESP-IDF hands it out through a callback (Espressif’s official esp-csi project is exactly this entry point), and MicroPython mainline now merges direct CSI access (contributed by ESPectre, PR #18460). The barrier to reading CSI is much lower than most people assume.

How a human body modulates those complex numbers

Signals in a room never travel one path. Waves from the router reach the ESP32 directly, but also after bouncing off walls, the ceiling, and furniture — each path with its own length and attenuation, all summing as complex vectors at the receive antenna. CSI is the net result of that superposition:

text
1

H = direct term + Σ(each reflection path × its complex gain)

Now put a person in the room. A still person is furniture — reflection paths are fixed, H is stable. A moving person keeps changing the length of the path that reflects off them, and the question becomes: how much does H move per centimeter of movement?

The wavelength at 2.4 GHz is about 12.5 cm. A person moving 3 cm changes the two-way reflection path by 6 cm — close to half a wavelength, about 173° of phase. A reflected wave that was adding constructively with the direct wave flips to destructive. H’s amplitude can swing from peak to trough even though the person blocks only a small slice of the energy. ESPectre receives roughly 100 packets per second, re-measuring this superposition with every one — body motion gets written into the CSI sequence a hundred times per second.

Here is the counter-intuitive part worth dwelling on: amplitude is nearly blind to millimeter motion, but phase is not. Phase φ = 2π·Δd/λ, so a 1 mm path change rotates the phase by about 2.9° one-way (5.8° two-way). Chest movement of ±2 mm drowns completely in the amplitude curve but shows up as a clean periodic signal in phase — which is exactly why the next post’s RuView measures breathing in phase. ESPectre’s detectors use amplitude only: the ESP32’s phase is polluted by carrier and sampling frequency offsets, so on cheap radios phase is unreliable, and amplitude-only is a deliberate engineering trade-off (the project keeps AGC active and designs every feature to be scale-invariant around that decision).

One more question at the sampling level: what frequencies does motion occupy? Breathing stays below 0.5 Hz, ordinary walking concentrates its energy within a few Hz. By the Nyquist theorem a few samples per second suffice. ESPectre targets 100 pps (packets per second) — two orders of magnitude of headroom. The bottleneck is never sampling rate; it is whether the room has enough packets to measure.

The animation above is the whole principle in motion: the router keeps sending packets (blue), and the signal reaches the ESP32 along a direct path, a wall reflection (gray), and a person reflection (orange), all summed as vectors. While the person walks, the orange path length keeps changing and the subcarrier-amplitude trace on the lower right jitters broadly; with a still room, the left trace is just tiny noise around a flat baseline. Distinguishing those two states is the detector’s entire job.

The pipeline: from raw CSI to a motion bit

mermaid
flowchart TD
    PKT["CSI packets<br/>100 pps target"] --> GRID["Time grid<br/>10 ms slots"]
    GRID --> CV["Fixed 12 subcarriers<br/>CV turbulence"]
    CV --> FILT["Hampel<br/>despike"]
    FILT --> WIN["1 s window<br/>autocorr + IQR"]
    WIN --> OUT["Fusion score<br/>threshold = motion"]

Five steps from raw packets to a motion decision: the time grid admits packets into 10 ms slots so samples stay evenly spaced; turbulence is computed as a coefficient of variation over 12 fixed subcarriers; a Hampel filter removes spikes; a one-second window yields autocorrelation and IQR statistics; a weighted fusion produces a probability, and crossing the threshold reports motion. Each layer removes one specific interference for the next. Stage by stage:

Time grid: remove arrival unevenness first

Packet arrivals in a real network are bursty: ten packets in one millisecond, then nothing for a second. Feeding packets into a window in arrival order distorts the statistics — the measured jitter mixes with the arrival-rate variation. ESPectre targets 100 pps and cuts time into 10 ms slots: at most one packet per slot; among same-slot candidates the one closest to the slot center is kept and the rest count as excess; duplicates, stale, and out-of-order arrivals are rejected. Window statistics then rest on evenly spaced samples, and the “uneven arrival” interference is stripped before the detector ever sees the data. The design has its own ADR (fixed temporal admission), down to details like “a window-spanning gap clears detector history immediately”.

Fixed 12 subcarriers: independent observations beat strong signals

Which of the 56 subcarriers to use? The intuitive answer is the strongest ones. ESPectre’s answer is a fixed, spread-out set:

[4, 8, 13, 18, 23, 28, 36, 41, 46, 51, 56, 60]

The rationale comes from measured channel coherence (fully argued in an ADR): motion disturbance stays coherent across roughly ten adjacent subcarriers — they are too close in frequency to see anything but “the same change of the same path” — while quiet noise is nearly independent per subcarrier. Adjacent subcarriers are redundant information. Spreading 12 samples across the whole band buys 12 mutually independent observations. For statistical detection, independent samples are worth far more than strong ones — the detector wants many witnesses, not one loud witness.

CV turbulence: a ratio immune to gain

Per packet, the coefficient of variation (CV) over the 12 amplitudes:

text
1

turbulence = std(amplitudes) / mean(amplitudes)

Why not amplitude itself? Because the ESP32’s AGC (automatic gain control) runs continuously: it raises gain when the signal is weak and lowers it when strong — equivalent to multiplying every amplitude by an unknown, time-varying factor k. Any absolute-amplitude feature tracks the AGC and needs recalibration. A ratio does not — std(kA) / mean(kA) = std(A) / mean(A), the k cancels. Turbulence sees the proportion of jitter, not the signal strength. This is the “scale-invariance” the docs keep stressing: one set of coefficients works across rooms, distances, and AGC states.

The animation shows the same jitter waveform twice: raw amplitudes on the left, AGC-amplified 3× on the right — the wave is visibly taller, but the CV below each panel is identical (0.12). The detector consumes the ratio, so gain changes are invisible to it.

The turbulence stream then passes two filters: a Hampel filter (window 7, threshold 5×MAD — median absolute deviation) replaces rare spikes with the window median, so a one-off RF burst like a microwave starting up does not read as “someone just jumped”; an optional first-order Butterworth low-pass (off by default) trades responsiveness for noise suppression, with the trade-off documented in TUNING.md.

Two detectors: lightweight and high accuracy

The filtered turbulence enters a detector. There are two production lines, both maintained; the choice comes down to how much headroom your chip has:

Parametersno neural net529

PositioningCPU/memory-constrained chipsaccuracy first

lightweight: two features tell one story

The first feature is the lag-1 autocorrelation of the turbulence series — the correlation between adjacent turbulence values in the window. The intuition: quiet-room noise is uncorrelated over time (this millisecond’s RF noise says nothing about the next), but sustained human motion is correlated — you cannot walk in one millisecond and teleport in the next. So high autocorrelation tolerates interference better than high magnitude: a neighbor’s brief RF burst can push turbulence up, but it cannot push autocorrelation up.

The second feature is the robust IQR of neighborhood-aggregated turbulence: each selected subcarrier is first replaced by the mean amplitude of its 5-bin neighborhood (suppressing single-bin noise), then the window’s interquartile range divided by the mean is computed. It catches spatially broad disturbance — a person crossing the whole sensing area perturbs many subcarriers at once.

Both features are standardized (z-scored against training statistics), pass through a fixed-weight linear model, go through a sigmoid into a 0–1 probability, and crossing the threshold means motion. The entire decision is two multiply-adds and one exponential — negligible on any MCU.

Startup calibration and down-only threshold recovery

Every room has a different RF floor: rooms full of metal furniture run a naturally higher baseline turbulence. lightweight handles this with startup calibration: at boot it assumes a quiet room, compares the session’s q95 turbulence quantile against the training idle reference, and shifts the decision threshold halfway:

text
1
2

adapted_logit = logit(base_threshold) + 0.5 × (startup_q95 − train_idle_q95)
threshold = sigmoid(adapted_logit)

Shifting only halfway is conservative — trusting ten seconds of one room’s samples fully would be risky, so they meet in the middle. During operation there is a second layer, “settled-level recovery”: every 20 evaluations it records a block maximum, and once 12 blocks accumulate (about 60 s at nominal cadence), if the median of those maxima sits 2.7 logits below the current threshold, the room is quieter than at boot and the threshold comes down to hug the actual floor. Down-only is the safety boundary: real activity pushes the block maxima back up and blocks any decrease, so recovery can never hide true motion. The converse limitation is documented too: if the room gets noisier later, the threshold will not climb back — only recalibration does that.

high_accuracy: a neural network with 529 parameters

high_accuracy is a proper MLP: 8 scale-invariant features into Dense(24) → Dense(12) → Dense(1, sigmoid), 529 parameters in total, probability threshold 0.5, no calibration (the trained default threshold works out of the box). Each of the 8 features has a physical meaning:

FeatureWhat it captures
turb_iqr_over_mean_aggrrobust spread of aggregated turbulence — broad spatial disturbance
turb_autocorrtemporal autocorrelation of turbulence — continuity of motion
turb_zcrzero-crossing rate of turbulence — the “texture” of the jitter
l1_delta_lag_rationormalized subcarrier-profile displacement — the overall shape changing
chan_shape_spread_subbandwithin-subband spread of channel shape
chan_shape_coherent_innovation_energycoherent innovation energy — the unpredictable part of shape change
chan_shape_excess_pathexcess path components — reflections that newly appeared
chan_shape_subband_kendall_lag_excessKendall lag correlation of subband shape — propagation of shape change

The first three are still turbulence-family; the last four are “channel-shape” family: instead of scalar turbulence at 12 sample points, they watch how the full 56-bin spectral shape evolves over time. Objects appearing, leaving, or moving in the room leave measurable textures on that shape.

I did a double-take at the 529 figure — not every sensing problem needs a large model. With the feature engineering done right, a few hundred parameters suffice and run on an MCU. The weights are open-sourced in the repo (a C++ header and a Python copy), along with the training dataset, the feature ledger (FEATURES.md — failed experiments included), and the performance report. The material for reproduction is all there.

Deployment: four paths, five minutes at the fastest

Hardware support is broad: ESP32-C6, C5, C3, S3, S2, and the classic ESP32 all work — broader than RuView (S3/C6 only), because statistical feature detection demands little compute. The network requirement is one line: an ordinary 2.4 GHz WiFi 4 (802.11n) HT20 network. 5 GHz links run, but the docs say detection quality there is unvalidated.

The fastest path is browser flashing: open espectre.dev/tools/flash in desktop Chrome 151+, connect the board over USB, pick the firmware, configure WiFi in the page — no toolchain install. The Monitor tool then shows live motion state and tuning controls — threshold and detector profile are adjustable online, no reflash needed.

For Home Assistant, pick a path by scenario:

A few parameters deserve comment. csi_traffic_mode: internal: CSI can only be extracted from received packets, so a room with no traffic gives nothing to read — the device generates measurable traffic itself by sending UDP packets to a multicast group at the target rate; external mode rides on existing traffic at the cost of an uncontrolled packet rate. motion_on_hits: 4 / motion_off_hits: 3 are debouncing: four consecutive “motion” evaluations set the state, three consecutive “clear” evaluations reset it — a single misjudgment never reaches the entity, so lights do not flicker. After flashing, Home Assistant discovers the device automatically — motion binary sensor, movement score, threshold controls appear as entities. For troubleshooting, the diagnostics entities include a set of CSI link metrics (callback rate, receive rate, admitted rate, slot occupancy) that localize whether the problem is traffic, capture, or the detector — TUNING.md gives the step-by-step order.

Boundaries: what it can and cannot do

The README is blunt about limitations, and these are worth knowing before deploying:

  • One board covers one sensing area — the area is effectively the space near the “transmit-reflect-receive” link; room-level coverage effectively means one board per room.
  • It detects environmental change — it cannot identify people, count them, or prove a room is empty.
  • It is not a certified security or medical device and cannot replace one.
  • Detection quality is validated on 2.4 GHz HT20 only.

There is also an ethical boundary that is easy to overlook: WiFi sensing takes no photos, but motion and routine data still expose patterns of life. ESPectre processes locally and keeps raw CSI upload off by default, and the docs include a security and responsible-use guide. Deploy only on networks you have the right to deploy on, and inform the people affected — those two are not optional.

What impresses me most is not algorithmic novelty but the transparency of the research process: a ledger per feature, an ADR per decision, an auto-generated performance report per version. It drags “WiFi sensing” out of paper demos into a ten-dollar board that runs in five minutes.
