PTZ Stability Four-Layer Defense: From Mechanics to Maintenance

July 31, 2026 Physical World Development PTZ, Stability, Mechanical Design, Control Theory, Optical Image Stabilization Signal and Image Processing Series 8176 words 17 min read
🔊

The most frustrating problem in security projects goes like this: a 36x PTZ camera mounted on a 6-meter pole, rock solid at the wide end, starts to show fine jitter the moment you push to the telephoto end—like someone gently tapping the viewfinder every few seconds. The project team replaces the pole with a thicker one—it works for a few days then comes back. They add an independent stabilization module—useless. Finally they blame the camera head, the manufacturer comes on-site, tests everything, and says all specs are normal. Both sides deadlock, the problem hanging in the middle.

Where’s the real problem?

Anyone who has wrestled with such projects will tell you: it’s not that one component is broken, but that stability itself is layered. From the pole, pan-tilt mechanics, control algorithms to image stabilization, each layer covers only a certain frequency range, and only all four layers stacked together can suppress telephoto-end jitter to invisible levels. Missing any layer leaves the rest patching holes for it—and patching holes has a ceiling. Without a solid mechanical foundation, all upper-layer efforts just turn “jumping” into “small jumping,” and even “small jumping” can’t be hidden at the telephoto end.

This post peels back these four layers one by one: first understand the excitation spectrum (where the enemy is), then examine the four-layer defense architecture (how to defend), then expand each layer—mechanical layer, control layer, imaging layer, and maintenance calibration layer.

Where Jitter Comes From: First Understand the Excitation Spectrum

To understand what each layer does, first figure out what the enemy looks like. All jitter has two attributes: source and frequency. Source determines why it exists, frequency determines who can suppress it—low frequency absorbed by control algorithms and imaging layer, mid frequency avoided by mechanical layer, high frequency handled by imaging layer and wide-bandwidth inner loop. Arrange common security scene excitation sources by frequency:

Excitation SourceTypical FrequencyCharacteristics
Pole 1st-order sway (wind-induced)~1 HzLarge displacement, doesn’t damage pole, but causes overall image drift at telephoto
Pole 2nd-order / Vortex shedding resonance3–6 HzSteady low-speed wind can excite, main cause of structural fatigue
Gantry / Short cantilever “whip effect”8–16 HzHeavy truck crossing bridge expansion joint triggers, measured exceeding limits 5x, hundreds of times daily
Vehicle / machinery / pedestrian stepping10–50 HzBroadband random

Let me explain a few terms that will be used throughout. Pole 1st-order sway means the pole acts like a cantilever beam, being blown left and right by wind—very low frequency (~1 Hz), large displacement, at telephoto end manifests as overall image drift, doesn’t hurt structure but looks annoying. Vortex shedding resonance is when airflow around a cylindrical pole, vortices alternately shed from both sides, generating periodic transverse force—shedding frequency determined by wind speed and pole diameter, calculated with the formula below. Whip effect refers to amplification at short cantilever ends: short cantilever, high stiffness, but when root is excited, end acceleration is amplified many times, typical frequency 8–16 Hz.

Corresponding three formulas:

1
2
3

Wind load      F = ½ ρ v² C_d A
Vortex shedding frequency  f_s = St · v / D    (circular cross-section St ≈ 0.2)
Structural natural frequency  f_n = (1/2π) √(k/m)

In the wind load formula, ρ is air density, v is wind speed, C_d is drag coefficient, A is windward area. Note wind speed is squared—wind speed doubles, wind load quadruples, that’s why telephoto end “can’t hold up with big wind.” In vortex shedding formula, St is Strouhal number (circular cross-section ~0.2), D is pole diameter: thinner pole, faster wind, higher vortex shedding frequency. Calculate with it: a 100 mm diameter pole, 4 m/s wind, f_s = 0.2 × 4 / 0.1 = 8 Hz—exactly in the whip effect band. Structural natural frequency f_n treats structure as spring-mass system, determined by stiffness k and mass m—this corresponds to the mechanical layer “frequency elevation” approach: thicken, reinforce by increasing k, k larger means f_n higher.

How to quantify this table on-site? Use vibration analyzer with spectrum analysis (or accelerometer + FFT) to measure data at pole root, middle, camera mounting surface, see which frequency band peaks concentrate, then compare with table. Measuring data is much faster than guessing—many projects struggle for weeks, finally discover it’s trucks passing triggering 10–15 Hz.

Vortex shedding resonance is the most easily ignored killer because it doesn’t need big wind. When f_s exactly equals pole’s f_n, a steady breeze of 4~6 m/s can continuously excite large vibration. Its diagnostic signature is jitter showing non-monotonic relationship with wind speed—worst at certain wind speed, gets better with higher wind speed. On-site seeing “breeze days jitter worse than windy days,” basically it’s this. Engineering experience: wind speed >8 m/s should enable anti-wind control strategy (reduce tracking sensitivity, increase deadband, limit zoom ratio).

Don’t Forget Internal Excitation

External excitation is only half the enemy. Motors and transmission mechanisms create their own jitter, and frequencies often fall in hardest-to-compensate range:

  • Cogging torque / torque ripple: T = K_t·I, at low speed ripple proportion amplified, manifests as “crawling” (stick-slip);
  • Stepper motor mid-frequency resonance zone: typical 100–200 Hz pulse frequency band, must “quickly cross” in velocity planning;
  • Transmission backlash + high gain → limit cycle oscillation: backlash increases with wear, return error measured in arc minutes;
  • Inertia matching: recommend J_load / J_motor < 5, beyond this velocity loop hard to tune;
  • Zoom/focus motor start-stop impact, slip ring friction.

External excitation determines “what to prevent,” internal excitation determines “if structure itself is clean enough.” Both excitations together form the complete enemy profile the four-layer defense must handle.

Four-Layer Defense Architecture

Countermeasures divided by frequency form a four-layer progressive defense system. Order cannot be reversed—without good mechanics, control and algorithms just patch holes.

Reading the diagram: arrows point to “built on top of,” bottom is mechanical layer, the foundation of the whole system. Mechanical layer pushes natural frequency above 25–30 Hz, avoiding 1–16 Hz main excitation band, while eliminating backlash and gravity torque—this is one-time money for structural work, all upper layers built on this foundation; control layer suppresses residual vibration within velocity loop bandwidth (100–300 Hz), cost is algorithm complexity; imaging layer provides final physical compensation at exposure instant, but its travel only about ±1°, can’t cover large swing; maintenance layer keeps everything from decaying with time and temperature. Four layers stacked together cover full spectrum jitter sources from DC to 50 Hz.

Why must follow this order? Because each layer’s working conditions depend on the layer below. Imaging layer OIS compensation range only ±0.5°~±1.5°, if mechanical layer doesn’t suppress low-frequency large swing, OIS hits travel limit immediately, becomes decoration. Control layer velocity loop bandwidth locked by transmission stiffness (gear system <20 Hz), if mechanical layer doesn’t use direct drive, DOB can’t even reach 10–16 Hz whip vibration. So order isn’t style, it’s physical constraint.

Acceptance criteria also set accordingly: previous article’s conclusion still holds—any stability indicator must be defined at maximum telephoto end. 36x telephoto end 1 pixel corresponds to 0.00063°, for sub-pixel stability, residual angular jitter must be suppressed to 6×10⁻⁴° within, arc-second level. Every mechanical layer selection decision ultimately validated at telephoto end.

Layer 1: Mechanical Layer — Stable Foundation

Shaft Architecture & Gravity Torque: Tilt Axis’s Hidden Killer

Tilt axis holds the entire camera core, core center of mass rarely lands exactly on rotation axis. As long as it’s offset, gravity produces torque on the rotation axis—this is gravity torque. Lens horizontal (θ=0) torque maximum, tilt angle larger torque smaller:

1

T_g = m · g · L · cos θ

L is camera core center of mass to Tilt rotation axis offset, θ is tilt angle. Calculation example: camera core 1.2 kg, center of mass offset L = 30 mm—already well designed number—output shaft torque:

1

T_g ≈ 1.2 × 9.8 × 0.03 = 0.35 N·m

Converted to motor side through reduction ratio i=30, efficiency η=0.7: 0.35 / (30 × 0.7) ≈ 17 mN·m. This value is motor’s continuous holding torque output.

What does 0.35 N·m mean? Equivalent to hanging about 35g weight at end of 1m arm. Looks light, but it’s applied before reduction ratio—motor must carry it every revolution, continuous output at motor side is the 17 mN·m calculated above.

17 mN·m sounds small, but it’s “permanent”—motor must always carry this torque to hold lens, triggering a chain of disasters:

  1. Motor continuous current → heating → thermal drift → precision degradation. Metal expands with heat, encoder reading stays same, lens pointing shifts;
  2. Static friction coupling with holding torque → low-speed crawling (stick-slip). Low-speed section motor torque insufficient, moves one step then pauses;
  3. Direction change gravity switches from “resistance” to “assistance” → backlash amplified into asymmetric error. Backlash is gap between gear teeth when changing direction—one direction overcomes gravity, other direction gravity helps, both sides arrival precision different, error direction asymmetric.

Engineering solutions (by priority):

  1. Design Tilt rotation axis to pass through camera core center of mass (L→0)—only fundamental cure;
  2. Zoom lens extension makes L dynamically change, should balance at longest focal position;
  3. Add counterweight (same logic as DJI RS series handheld gimbal three-axis balancing);
  4. If really can’t balance, use worm gear self-locking, trade efficiency for zero holding torque.

Bearings & Stiffness: Second Thing Easily Overlooked

Pan axis uses pair of angular contact/deep groove ball bearings with preload, eliminating axial runout; Tilt axis prioritizes double-sided U-fork support, not single-side cantilever. Cantilever cost is real: measured data shows, single-side hanging 3kg continuous 10 minutes, tilt axis backlash can increase 0.3°—this magnitude at telephoto end equals hundreds of pixels. Budget allowing, Tilt axis support structure is one of most worth spending places.

Three Truths About Microstepping

Microstepping splits stepper motor’s full step: 1.8° motor with 1/32 subdivision, one step becomes 0.056°. Larger subdivision number, theoretically finer, but three truths must recognize:

Truth 1: Microstepping ≠ Precision. nth microstep incremental torque proportional to sin(π/2N). At 1/256 subdivision, single microstep torque only about 0.6% rated torque—completely overwhelmed by static friction, motor can’t move this tiny step. Actual effective resolution usually only 1/16~1/32, nominal 1/256 is marketing number.

Truth 2: Subdivision’s real value is reducing torque ripple and noise. ADI/Trinamic schemes use 1/256 microstepping (51200 microsteps/rev) with StealthChop fixed-frequency voltage PWM, approximating current waveform to ideal sine, significantly suppressing vibration and whine. This is essential for security dome camera quiet requirements and broadcasting low-speed smoothness.

What is torque ripple? Ideal motor output torque constant, actual because cogging and current commutation, torque periodically fluctuates each revolution. Large ripple, low-speed motor moves stuttering, accompanied by high-frequency whine. Subdivision turning stepper into approximate sine drive effect is suppressing ripple at source.

Truth 3: High subdivision cost is pulse frequency explosion. 10 rev/s × 51200 = 512 kHz—MCU software generating pulses can’t reach this frequency, must use hardware ramp generator (Trinamic up to 4 MHz).

Practical approach is dynamic subdivision: low-speed segment use 1/256 (want smooth), high-speed segment reduce to 1/8~1/16 (want frequency), seamless switching in between. This is only realistic solution for PTZ 0.1°/s ~ 400°/s spanning 3.7 orders magnitude dynamic range. Previous article mentioned: one dome camera speed range from 0.1°/s to 540°/s, single drive strategy can’t cover all, dynamic subdivision splits this range into two segments to handle separately.

Direct Drive Tradeoffs & Mainstream Status

Direct drive (directly by torque motor driving load, i=1) benefits: efficiency 9095% (gear system 7085%), zero backlash, high back-drivability; cost: inertia ratio fixed 1:1, load inertia change easily causes control loop oscillation; low-speed segment high current, needs forced cooling. But direct drive has one attribute other schemes can’t provide—velocity loop bandwidth can reach >100 Hz, while gear system usually <20 Hz. This is key threshold for active stabilization realization, control layer section will touch on it again.

Mainstream status: security dome camera mainstream still 0.9° hybrid stepper + timing belt one stage + gear one stage, total reduction ratio 20~40:1. Lumens etc. manufacturers use 400-tooth hybrid stepper with curved tooth timing belt. This isn’t backward, it’s balance point between cost, quiet, precision, and wind resistance—dome camera positioning is “seeable, fast rotation, cheap,” not “observatory-level precision.”

Transmission Mechanisms & Backlash

Mentioned backlash before: gear changing direction, driving gear switches from touching one tooth side to the other, the empty path is backlash (also called return error). It decisively hurts precision—no matter how high resolution, must complete backlash first before image moves, when changing direction. Different transmission mechanisms have huge backlash level differences:

TypeSingle-Stage Reduction RatioBacklashEfficiencyCharacteristics
Timing belt (HTD/GT curved tooth)1:1~8:1No rigid backlash, but belt elastic return lag95~98%Lowest noise, CR material absorbs vibration; curved tooth increases contact area reducing gap
Spur/helical gear3:1~6:1Standard >30′, precision 3~15′95~98%Direction change “rebound jitter” obvious
Worm gear20:1~100:115~30′ (precision parts can press to ~6′)48~80%Self-locking, no position loss on power off; 45~55 dB(A)
Planetary reducer3:1~100:1Precision <3′, standard >30′95~97%/stageHigh stiffness, high cost-performance
Harmonic reducer50:1~160:1<1′ (near-zero backlash)65~85%Highest torque/mass ratio; flexspline fatigue life limit; high cost

In table, harmonic reducer’s “flexspline fatigue life limit” worth separate explanation: harmonic transmits through flexspline elastic deformation, flexspline repeated deformation causes fatigue, life limited by deformation count not rotation speed. For long-life design, harmonic’s “near-zero backlash” has usage period.

Backlash unit is arc minute (′), 1° = 60′. Backlash conversion ruler must remember: 1′ backlash ≈ 0.29 mm on 1m arm. Convert to pixels calculate clearly: 36x telephoto end HFOV 1.7°, image width 2688 px, 1′ = 0.0167°; while telephoto end 1 pixel only corresponds to 1.7°/2688 = 0.00063°, so 1′ ≈ 0.0167/0.00063 ≈ 26 pixels. A “precision grade” 3′ backlash gearbox, at 36x telephoto end changing direction produces 78 pixel image jump—image directly “pops.” This is why high-magnification telephoto observation products must use harmonic or direct drive.

Selection decisions:

  • Outdoor wind resistance + power-off position hold → worm gear (FLIR PTU, BIT-PT510 both like this, latter claims backlash <0.1°, repeatability ±0.1°);
  • Quiet + low cost → timing belt (+ one gear stage);
  • Military/police observation / telephoto precision tracking (>30x, angular resolution <0.01°) → harmonic reducer or direct drive;
  • General mid-range → precision planetary (3′).

Feedback & Closed-loop: Encoder Placement Determines Error Magnitude

Mechanical structure set, still need to know “where actually turned to.” Mainstream uses magnetic encoder, following AS5047P / AS5048A level measured parameters:

MetricValue
Resolution14 bit = 16384 steps/rev = 0.0219°/LSB
Nonlinearity INL (optimal installation)±0.8° @25°C, full temp ±1°
RMS noise0.068°
SPI read latency90~110 µs
DAEC dynamic angle error compensation0.02° residual at 1700 rpm

LSB is least significant bit, smallest angle encoder can resolve. INL (integral nonlinearity) is encoder actual reading relative to ideal line maximum deviation—note this value: ±0.8°. Magnetic encoder resolution high, absolute accuracy poor, INL two orders higher than resolution. If encoder directly measures output shaft, ±0.8° at 36x telephoto end is thousands pixels deviation, completely unusable.

SPI read latency 90~110 µs also worth noting: this is dead time on feedback path. Velocity loop bandwidth 100–300 Hz, one read latency 100 µs takes 1%~3% of one cycle; want to increase bandwidth further, must switch to parallel interface or reduce read cycle.

Key conclusion: Encoder must be mounted on motor-side high-speed shaft. Mounted on high-speed shaft, reduction ratio synchronously shrinks error: 14 bit + i=30 → equivalent resolution 0.00073°/LSB, INL converted ±0.8°/30 = ±0.027°. ±0.027° exactly falls within ±0.1° preset position indicator—this is mainstream solution design logic: high resolution amplified by reduction ratio, terrible absolute accuracy shrunk by reduction ratio. Reduce position resolution from ±0.8° to ±0.027°, without spending one penny more hardware.

Advanced encoders can further improve: AS5147U 16 bit → 0.0055°; TLE5012B 15 bit, absolute accuracy 0.05°.

High-end solution is dual encoder: motor-side incremental (responsible for direction change and velocity loop) + output-side absolute (responsible for position loop). Position loop feedback point after backlash, can directly eliminate backlash influence on final positioning accuracy.

Zero calibration: traditional solution is optical slot/hall switch + power-on return-to-zero, takes several seconds self-test action at power-on; absolute encoder can avoid return-to-zero—BIT-PT510 claims recover pre-power-off angle within 3 seconds after power off.

There’s also solution between open-loop and closed-loop: closed-loop stepper / FOC hybrid. Encoder real-time compares command angle with actual angle, deviation exceeds threshold (typically determine stall when electrical angle ≥90°), switches from constant current microstepping to current vector control, drives stepper as two-phase PMSM. Benefits: keep open-loop stepper low cost; gain stall self-recovery; torque output on demand—static current can drop to 10~30%, directly solves heating problem (thus thermal drift).

Motion Planning: Trapezoidal vs S-Curve

Mechanical and feedback ready, still care how to move. Core concept is jerk—rate of acceleration change.

Trapezoidal velocity curve (3 segments: accelerate-constant-decelerate) at segment transitions jerk is infinite, acceleration instant jump, equivalent to giving mechanism an impact; S-curve (7 segments) jerk bounded, impact spread. Larger jerk, larger vibration energy injected into mechanism and wider spectrum, easier to hit structural resonance mode—like hammering table excites various vibrations, slowly pressing with palm doesn’t.

Why S-curve called “7 segments,” break it down:

1
2

Trapezoidal: accelerate-constant-decelerate, 3 segments, segment transitions jerk = ∞
S-curve: jerk-accelerate-constant-accelerate-decelerate-constant-jerk-accelerate-constant-decelerate-jerk-decelerate, 7 segments, jerk bounded

PMD measured data: well-tuned S-curve can shorten “effective transfer time” (including settling time) 25%+. Note, S-curve looks “slower,” but because no need to wait oscillation decay after arrival, total time actually shorter.

Engineering compromise is Trinamic’s SixPoint ramping: add A1/D1 two segments of secondary acceleration/deceleration on trapezoidal basis, low acceleration at start-stop. Lower cost than complete 7-segment S-curve, designed for PTZ image perception—perception wants “start-stop no stuttering.”

Why PTZ especially needs SixPoint this kind compromise? Because dome camera requires both 540°/s swing and 0.1°/s low-speed jog—complete S-curve at ultra-high segment calculation overhead high, trapezoid not smooth enough. SixPoint keeps S-curve smoothness at low-speed segment, returns to approximate trapezoid at high-speed segment,正好 fits this use case.

Mechanical Layer Key Measures Summary

(1) Elevate 1st-order natural frequency (preferred strategy): Push bracket/pole 1st frequency above >25–30 Hz, avoid 1–16 Hz main excitation band. Methods: carbon fiber/thickened wall pipe, shorten cantilever, cross-section shape tapered pole better than straight round pipe better than square pipe. This is only way to “avoid” excitation band, one-time money, long-term effective structural measure.

(2) TMD Tuned Mass Damper: Remedy when structure already built, can’t change stiffness. Hang mass block on structure, tune to resonance with structure, let mass block absorb vibration energy. Mass ratio μ = 2% ~ 5%, Den Hartog optimal frequency ratio f_opt = f_n / (1 + μ), optimal damping ratio ζ_opt = √( 3μ / [8(1+μ)] ). Engineering case: Citylink project uses different stiffness rubber pads + stackable counterweight plates covering 8–16 Hz, field measurement tuning first-time success. Commercial TMD can cover 0.15 Hz – 600 Hz. Experience rule: pole >7.6 m (25 ft) and EPA <2.0 recommend adding chain/serpentine damper.

(3) Isolation pads (use cautiously): Effective isolation requires f_exc / f_iso > √2, engineering take ≥3. But cost is isolation pads reduce pointing stiffness, directly worsens preset position repeatability. High precision positioning instruments usually abandon soft isolation, choose “stiffening + active compensation” route. This is mutually exclusive tradeoff: stiffening elevate frequency vs isolation reduce transmission, can’t have both.

(4) Backlash elimination & balancing: Dual gear spring preload; worm gear pre-tightening; harmonic reducer (backlash <1′); counterweight makes center of mass pass through tilt axis, eliminating gravity disturbance torque.

Three Core Engineering Tradeoffs

Mechanical layer reviewed, repeatedly appearing are three tradeoffs, compare when selecting:

  1. Resolution ≫ Precision, 100x difference. Calculated 0.0009° resolution differs two orders from ±0.1° preset position precision. Precision not determined by resolution, but by backlash + gravity return error + structural stiffness + thermal deformation, together. Previous article’s unidirectional approach here still fastest remedy: all preset positions fixed enter from same direction, overshoot slightly then come back, solidify backlash as constant, software层面 calibrate away.
  2. Dynamic range contradiction. 0.1°/s ~ 540°/s spans 3.7 orders (separate PTU more exaggerated, 0.003~100°/s, about 33,000x). Single drive strategy can’t cover all, must rely on dynamic subdivision segments.
  3. Self-locking vs efficiency. Worm gear efficiency only 50~80% (high heat, needs larger motor), but trades for power-off self-locking and wind resistance no position loss. Outdoor telephoto, wind resistance prioritize it.

These three aren’t independent multiple choice, are three constraints on same machine: want cheap and high precision, must work on transmission (precision planetary + backlash elimination); want wind resistance and efficiency, worm gear first calculate heat bill; want all, budget directly goes direct drive + dual encoder. Mechanical layer has no free lunch.

Layer 2: Control Layer — Algorithm Compensation

Mechanical layer done what it can, remaining vibration交给 algorithms. Control layer is three-loop servo cascade: current loop manages torque, velocity loop manages speed, position loop manages angle. Each loop bandwidth limited, three loops arranged by bandwidth high to low, like three defense lines: outer loop command is inner loop reference, any loop跟不上, error leaks upward.

Three-loop Bandwidth Ladder

1

Current loop 1–2 kHz  →  Velocity loop 100–300 Hz  →  Position loop 20–50 Hz

Current loop innermost fastest, position loop outermost slowest, each takes 1/4 ~ 1/5, guaranteeing phase margin. Phase margin is control system stability metric—insufficient margin, system oscillates. Taking 1/4~1/5 each level is servo tuning golden ratio, violation必然 oscillation. For PTZ, velocity loop bandwidth is key connecting up and down: determines how high frequency disturbance can suppress, also determines how far DOB can compensate—this point connects in IMU feedforward section.

How three loops串成 chain: position loop output is velocity loop reference input, velocity loop output is current loop reference input, current loop directly determines drive voltage. Outer loop slow, inner loop fast—position loop issues “target speed,” velocity loop issues “target torque,” current loop converts to actual current. Any loop bandwidth跟不上, error leaks upward, so bandwidth must progressively decrease reserving margin.

Notch Filter

Mechanical systems always have resonance peaks. Notch filter puts narrow-band notch in control loop: near resonance frequency press down gain, other frequencies basically unchanged, thus while keeping high control gain “digging out” mechanical resonance peak. Second-order IIR, three parameters:

  • Center frequency: 30–2000 Hz
  • Bandwidth: 3–2000 Hz (default ~10 Hz)
  • Depth (dB)

Depth determines how hard to notch (-20 dB means this frequency gain pressed to 1/10 original); bandwidth determines how wide to notch, too wide cuts useful frequency band together. Q value = center frequency / bandwidth, higher Q notch narrower sharper, resonance frequency estimate must be more accurate.

Limitations (must know):

  1. High Q narrow band has small control impact, but resonance frequency drifts with load/wear becomes ineffective—factory tuned notch, half year later mechanism stiffness changed no longer accurate;
  2. Only applicable to high-frequency resonance—low-frequency resonance notch filter introduces too much phase lag,反而 worsens stability;
  3. Practical solution: 1~2 notch + 1 low-pass combination; or use adaptive notch (ANF) online track frequency drift.

Adaptive notch (ANF) principle is let notch center frequency follow measured resonance frequency: controller real-time estimates resonance frequency, notch center automatically aligns. Cost is algorithm complexity and extra frequency estimation loop—correct solution for long-term drift, unnecessary for one-time calibration system.

Input Shaping: Zero-Hardware-Cost Oscillation Suppression

After large-angle preset position swing, structure oscillates at natural frequency for a while—security dome camera “swung to preset position image still shakes 1 second” is this problem. Input shaping思路 is split original single pulse movement into two small pulses, second delayed half damping cycle issued, let two excitations’ oscillations exactly cancel. ZV double pulse:

  • A₁ = 1/(1+K), A₂ = K/(1+K)
  • K = exp( -ζπ / √(1-ζ²) )
  • Δt = π / ( ω_n √(1-ζ²) )

Cost: introduces half damping cycle delay. ZVD (three pulses) better robustness but delay doubles. This is zero-hardware-cost jitter suppression means, preset position swing到位后 image shake problem basically solved by it—manufacturer doesn’t need change any mechanical structure, only change motion command shape.

In formula, ω_n is system natural frequency (mechanical layer elevation elevates this), ζ is damping ratio. These two parameters aren’t guessed: do step response test on system, identify frequency and attenuation rate from oscillation waveform. ZVD比 ZV one more pulse, less sensitive to ω_n estimation error—field parameters measured rough still usable.

S-Curve / Jerk Limit + Anti-Windup

Motion planning S-curve (explained in mechanical layer section) here simultaneously serves control: suppress start-stop impact on structural mode excitation. With anti-windup (clamping or back-calculation), avoid long-time stall integrator accumulated energy one-time release, causing explosive overshoot—imagine motor stuck PID integral item keeps rising, once released it猛冲一下.

IMU Feedforward + Disturbance Observer

Active stabilization core. Feedforward means “act before error appears”: gyroscope (IMU) measures base shaking, directly superimpose angular rate reversely to velocity command, let pan-tilt actively rotate opposite direction—error not yet formed is cancelled, not wait for error appears then chase. Disturbance observer (DOB) responsible for compensating unmeasured, unmodeled disturbances.

  • IMU measured base angular rate as feedforward, directly reverse compensation;
  • Disturbance observer compensates low-frequency disturbance, Q filter cutoff typical 20–50 Hz;
  • Gyro-stabilized platform line-of-sight stabilization accuracy level 10–100 µrad RMS (1 µrad ≈ 0.2 arc seconds).

µrad有多小? 1 µrad = 1 meter out 1 µm displacement, about 0.2 arc seconds. 10–100 µrad RMS means line-of-sight at 1 km out drift only 1–10 cm magnitude—military/police observation and laser ranging platform level.

DOB design essence is “nominal model + residual compensation”: first use model calculate expected output, actual output minus expected视为 disturbance, compensate back reversely. Q filter decides compensation bandwidth vs noise tradeoff—cutoff open high, gyro noise amplified into new jitter.

Note one chain: DOB compensation bandwidth limited by velocity loop bandwidth (100–300 Hz), velocity loop bandwidth limited by transmission stiffness. This is why high-end stabilization platforms must use direct drive—gear system <20 Hz velocity loop bandwidth根本 can’t suppress 10–16 Hz whip vibration. Mechanical layer section direct drive “velocity loop bandwidth >100 Hz” value realized here: not resolution, is bandwidth.

Zoom-Proportional Speed Control

Though strictly this belongs to user experience not stability, but it closely related to control layer, and many products get it wrong. Operator perceives screen angular velocity, not mechanical angular velocity. Take Hikvision DS-2DF6A436XG1 (6~216 mm, HFOV 56.6°→1.7°) for example, wide/telephoto FOV ratio about 33:1. If joystick pushed full at wide end is 100°/s (screen about 1.8 screens/second), at telephoto end same 100°/s is about 59 screens/second—target instantly flies out of screen.

Control law: ω_cmd = ω_joystick × FOV(z) / FOV(wide), that is telephoto end manual speed automatically reduces to wide end’s about 1/33.

Similarly, AI tracking proportional gain Kp must also schedule with FOV—these two are essentially same thing: keep screen angular velocity constant. Next article AI tracking will expand gain scheduling.

Practical Degradation Means

Manufacturer official suggestion: reduce preset position switching speed; avoid long-term work at maximum zoom ratio. Essence is trade responsiveness for stability. At site where can’t change hardware, this is fastest见效 means—though doesn’t cure root, but can first suppress symptoms, buy time for troubleshooting.

Layer 3: Imaging Layer — Hybrid Stabilization

Mechanical layer and control layer suppressed jitter “physical part,” remaining交给 image itself. Before entering imaging layer, first look at hard constraint: blur < 1 pixel requirement is

1

θ̇ · f / p < 1 / T_exp

θ̇ is angular velocity, f is focal length, p is pixel size, T_exp is exposure time. This constraint at night more harsh than stabilization itself: 1/30 s exposure, jitter already becomes irreversible motion blur, electronic stabilization helpless with already blurred single frame, only optical stabilization can physically compensate during exposure. This is OIS irreplaceable core reason.

Inequality expansion read: left side θ̇·f/p is “pixels screen swept per second,” right side 1/T_exp is “pixels allowed to sweep per frame exposure.” Exposure longer, right threshold smaller, more harsh on jitter—this is why night scene long exposure blurs most obvious.

OIS (Optical Image Stabilization)

Optical image stabilization (OIS) relies on physically moving optical elements to cancel jitter. Three implementations:

  • Lens group shift: Move one lens group, change optical path—most mainstream, lens internally reserves one shiftable lens group;
  • Sensor shift: Move sensor itself, doesn’t touch lens optical path, but limited by sensor’s travel range;
  • Variable-angle prism (VAP): Double glass夹 high refractive index liquid bellows structure, changes optical axis direction by changing vertex angle.

Three implementations tradeoffs: lens group shift needs reserve lens travel, telephoto end has larger travel demand; sensor shift travel limited by packaging, but fast response, doesn’t introduce lens aberration; VAP doesn’t occupy lens group travel, highest bandwidth, cost is complex structure—liquid sealing long-term stability is difficulty.

VAP is三者里 compensation bandwidth best: Canon binoculars measured correction angle ±0.7°, manufacturer explicitly states “compared to shift-type can cover wider vibration frequency range.”

MetricTypical Value
Compensation angle range±0.5° ~ ±1.5°
BandwidthAbout 10–20 Hz

Advantages (irreplaceable): physical compensation during exposure, simultaneously solves motion blur and rolling distortion. Limitations: limited travel; only compensates angle not translation; can’t resist large low-frequency swing.

EIS / GIS (Electronic Image Stabilization / Gyro Image Stabilization)

Electronic image stabilization (EIS) relies on cropping screen, frame-by-frame shift compensation to cancel motion, cost is losing field of view. Gyro image stabilization (GIS) is EIS type, but motion information comes from gyroscope not image. Gyro-assisted EIS significantly better than pure image EIS—Axis explicitly states its solution “based on gyroscope information not video content calculates motion,” thus:

  • Scene with moving target still stable (pure image EIS gets biased by large target);
  • Low illumination weak texture still stable;
  • Can distinguish “platform jitter” from “target movement”—critical for auto tracking.

Cost: crop loss FOV, typical 10%~20%. Evaluation metric is pixel retention rate 100·W_s·H_s/(W·H), that is stabilization后保留 effective pixels占原画面比例。

10%~20% crop isn’t small number: 4K sensor crop 20%, effective resolution drops to about 3.3K. EIS isn’t “free” stabilization, is trades resolution for it—so its metric isn’t “stable or not,” but pixel retention rate.

Rolling shutter problem: EIS must逐行 shift correction, and must read at least one row before can start processing, thus for wave distortion (jello effect) naturally weaker than OIS.

Rolling shutter essence is “time difference”: each row exposure moment different, same frame different rows record different moment jitter, screen twists into wave. OIS at exposure instant stabilizes whole frame together, doesn’t have this problem.

OIS is hardware, limited travel but high bandwidth, responsible for high-frequency small amplitude (>5 Hz, amplitude within travel) and exposure-time blur suppression; EIS/GIS is software, low bandwidth but unlimited travel (by cropping), responsible for low-frequency residual, large drift, and rolling逐行 correction. Both together cover complete band—this is “hybrid stabilization” meaning.

Why can’t rely on only one? OIS travel only ±1°上下, low-frequency large swing几下 hits limit; EIS逐行 correction limited by rolling readout delay, high-frequency vibration one frame来不及完成 shift compensation. Two schemes each block frequency band other missed.

LayerResponsible
OISHigh-frequency small amplitude (>5 Hz, amplitude within travel) + exposure-time blur suppression

Product examples: Hikvision PTZ uses OIS + GIS; Fujinon SX800 (20–800 mm, 40x) uses OIS + EIS, manufacturer notes “especially effective at ultra-telephoto end”; Panasonic AW-UE160 uses OIS (Pitch/Yaw 2-axis) + Roll EIS—roll axis direction has no lens optical stabilization, can only rely on cropping.

这套分工 already industry standard configuration, not because manufacturers follow trend, but because single scheme has physical limits in cost, travel, bandwidth. When selecting, check if product specification lists both OIS and EIS/GIS, can quickly judge its stabilization approach.

Two Must-Know Conflicts

Conflict 1: Stabilization vs Auto Tracking. EIS global motion compensation conflicts with tracking algorithm’s target motion estimation—algorithm can’t distinguish if camera jittering or target moving. Solution: rely on gyroscope prior decoupling, feed IMU data to both stabilization and tracking modules, let tracking module know “this motion camera caused, target itself didn’t move.”

Conflict 2: Low-Illumination Long Exposure vs Stabilization. 1/30 s exposure jitter already irreversible motion blur, EIS helpless. Can only: rely on OIS exposure-time compensation; or shorten exposure + increase gain (sacrifice SNR). Axis’s approach is stabilization with short-exposure low-illumination technology combination.

Layer 4: Maintenance Calibration Layer — Continuously Maintain

First three layers made “factory condition” good, but PTZ precision decays. Axis official attributes preset position drift etc. to vibration/impact destroying factory calibration, prescription is re-execute PTZ calibration and establish periodic scheduled calibration. Maintenance layer is making this automated, doesn’t require people climb up middle of night to site:

  • Periodic automatic recalibration: e.g., daily 3AM return-to-zero calibration once, clear accumulated error;
  • Backlash trend monitoring: backlash increases with wear, plot backlash measured each calibration into trend, is mechanical wear health indicator—backlash suddenly increases means gears/bearings start wearing, can提前 schedule parts replacement, not wait for complete failure;
  • Temperature compensation lookup table: record environment temperature vs precision correlation, build temperature compensation lookup table, temperature changed directly look-up修正—many “summer accurate winter inaccurate"疑难杂症, check this table understands.

One more experience: environment temperature剧烈变化的前后, is preset position deviation high period. If project can record each calibration’s temperature and deviation, one year can plot temperature-deviation curve—both can use for compensation table, also explains “why winter always偏差” type field puzzles.

Back to Opening Project

Back to opening 36x dome camera. Thicker pole worked few days then relapse—thickening pole indeed elevated natural frequency, but pole gets old, foundation gets loose, natural frequency drops back to excitation band; add stabilization module useless—telephoto end jitter major source from mechanics and structure, imaging layer can’t compensate enough. Correct troubleshooting order: first quantify excitation spectrum, figure which frequency jittering (vortex shedding “breeze more jitter” signature can help quickly locate); then solve what mechanical layer can solve (frequency elevation, backlash elimination, balancing); finally use control layer and imaging layer each manage one frequency band.

Four-layer priority one sentence: first mechanics, then control, then imaging, finally maintenance. Mechanical layer makes way 1–16 Hz main excitation band, control layer suppresses residual vibration within velocity loop bandwidth, imaging layer bottom-line at exposure instant, maintenance layer keeps everything not decaying with time and temperature. Missing one layer, problem degrades from “jumping” to “small jumping”—and telephoto end even “small jumping” can’t be hidden.

Finally add troubleshooting口诀: first look spectrum, then change structure, finally tune algorithm. Don’t start by adding stabilization module—this is third layer imaging layer tool, can’t solve first layer mechanical layer problem.
