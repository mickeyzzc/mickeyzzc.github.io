Dual-Spectrum Thermal Cameras: Principles, NVR Integration, and the Market

October 12, 2026 October 14, 2026 Physical World Dev Thermal Imaging, Dual-Spectrum, Video Surveillance, NVR, Selection Guide Signals & Image Processing Series 3485 words 17 min read
🔊

Two in the morning, a fence line on a business campus. A visible-light camera with IR illuminators shows you the ten meters its light reaches — beyond that, black. A chemical tank farm is worse: the camera is supposed to watch, around the clock, not “is anyone there” but “which tank is running hot” — point a visible camera at a steel tank and it tells you nothing.

Thermal imaging fixes exactly these two scenes: it needs no light at all, receiving infrared radiation emitted by the objects themselves, so image brightness maps directly to temperature. But thermal has its own blind spot — no texture, no readable faces, no license plates. So the industry bolts two lenses into one housing: thermal finds things, visible light identifies them. That’s a dual-spectrum (bi-spectrum) camera.

This post walks through the technology, how NVRs and platforms ingest these cameras, the state of the industry, and representative products. Figures verified October 2026; specifications defer to vendor datasheets.

Why Thermal Sees Heat

Everything above absolute zero radiates electromagnetic waves, and the peak wavelength scales inversely with temperature — an object at room temperature (~300 K) peaks near 10 µm by Wien’s displacement law, right inside the 8–14 µm long-wave infrared (LWIR) band, which happens to be one of the best atmospheric windows. No illumination needed: people, vehicles, running machinery are all sources.

The sensor for this band is a microbolometer: each pixel is a tiny heat-absorbing membrane whose temperature rises by a few thousandths of a degree under IR, changing its resistance. Read out the whole array and you have a temperature map. It needs no cooling and works the moment power is applied — that’s the physical precondition for thermal cameras costing a few thousand yuan. The other family, cooled detectors (HgCdTe and similar materials, with a micro-cooler dropping the sensor below −200 °C), is far more sensitive but runs tens to hundreds of thousands per unit — military and ultra-long-range territory. Security is uncooled.

Uncooled sensing materials come in two mainstream flavors: VOx (vanadium oxide) and amorphous silicon (a-Si). VOx offers better sensitivity (NETD down to ~30 mK); a-Si is cheaper (Lynred is the flag-bearer). Security dual-spectrum cameras are almost universally VOx.

NETD: Thermal’s “ISO Rating”

NETD (Noise-Equivalent Temperature Difference) is the core spec: scene temperature difference folded down to noise level. ≤50 mK / ≤40 mK / ≤35 mK means a 0.05 °C / 0.04 °C / 0.035 °C difference emerges from the noise. Lower is better — the picture resolves finer temperature layers for the same lens. A useful calibration: with NETD of 50 mK, a person 5 °C above background gives a signal-to-noise ratio of 100, and the silhouette is crisp; a cheap 300 mK sensor has to fake it with post-processing.

Read resolution together with pixel pitch: security runs from 160×120 through 384×288 and 640×512, with high-end PTZ reaching 1280×1024. Pitch has shrunk from 17 µm to 12 µm (today’s standard), with 8 µm and 6 µm already demonstrated by Raytron. Smaller pitch means wider field of view at the same focal length — and smaller, cheaper germanium lenses.

One more thing that trips people up: thermal images have no color. The “ironbow” and “rainbow” looks are pseudo-color — grayscale mapped to a color ramp, because eyes read color gradients faster than gray shades. The most common WhiteHot palette is pure grayscale: brighter is hotter.

The animation replays one scene across day, deep night, and fog: the visible camera works on reflected light — no light, no picture — and smoke scatters it into a white wash. The thermal camera receives the target’s own emission; at 10 µm the wavelength dwarfs smoke and dust particles, so scattering is weak and the human body stays visible in the dark. The flip side: the thermal frame carries no color and no texture — you will never read an expression or clothing off the right-hand panel. That’s exactly why it needs a visible partner.

Dual-Spectrum: Two Pairs of Eyes in One Housing

The construction is straightforward: a thermal lens (germanium — ordinary glass is fully opaque at 8–14 µm, which is one reason thermal lenses are expensive; germanium itself isn’t cheap) mounted beside a visible lens, each with its own sensor and imaging path, outputting two independent encoded streams plus an optional front-end fusion stream.

Four output modes:

The visible channel has the richest detail; the thermal channel works around the clock but shows only temperature; alpha fusion overlays the thermal hot zones back onto the visible frame — detail and heat at once; picture-in-picture is the more common engineering layout — the two streams stay independent and the overlay is purely a display-layer concern, so recording and analytics never fight each other.

Side-by-side lenses bring an unavoidable problem: parallax.

The two optical axes are separated by baseline B, and the closer the target, the larger the misalignment between the two frames (misalignment is inversely proportional to distance). So dual-spectrum cameras are factory-calibrated at the working distance: the mapping between the two frames (optical-axis registration) is stored as a lookup table and applied at runtime. The further the scene is from the calibration distance, the larger the residual error — the “optical axis alignment” spec on a datasheet refers to this, and well-built units quote under 1 mrad.

Detection range (the most marketing-flavored number on a spec sheet) has a standard algorithm. The industry uses three DRI tiers: Detection (something is there), Recognition (it’s a person, not a car), Identification (which person it is), mapped by the Johnson criteria to 2, 8, and 13–16 pixels across the target’s critical dimension. Given target size, focal length, and pitch, range is computable:

Worked example: 640×512, 12 µm pitch, 25 mm lens → IFOV = 0.48 mrad. Against a 1.7 m person: theoretical detection ≈ 7.1 km, recognition ≈ 1.8 km, identification ≈ 1.1 km. That’s the 50%-probability laboratory figure; real atmosphere, weather, and thermal contrast halve it or worse, and vendor claims usually come in at half the formula or lower. Treat advertised ranges as a relative scale within one vendor, not a cross-vendor comparison.

Temperature specs deserve their own note: security radiometry is typically -20150 °C at ±8 °C or worse (trend monitoring only); industrial radiometry is -20550 °C at ±2 °C or ±2% (whichever is greater); fever-screening cameras reach ±0.5 °C — but only inside a narrow 33–39 °C calibration window. Error sources and calibration belong to the second post in this pair.

How NVRs and Platforms Keep Up

Once the camera is on the wall: what does it look like to an NVR, how do temperature alarms get out, and do third-party platforms care? This side of the industry is messier than the cameras.

One Camera, Two Channels

The industry convention: one device, two NVR channels. Hikvision, Dahua, and Uniview NVRs all ingest a dual-spectrum camera as channel 1 (visible) + channel 2 (thermal); HikMicro’s official FAQ instructs you to add both channels, and Uniview brands the scheme “single-IP dual-channel.” GB/T 28181-2022 carries matching directory fields: PhotoelectricImagingType tags a channel as thermal (1-visible, 2-thermal, 3-radar, 4-X-ray, 5-depth), and PTZType values 6/7 cover multi-lens devices’ panorama/split channels — the standard explicitly anticipates one device reporting multiple channels.

The camera emits two independent streams; the NVR records and plays them back as two channels. The fusion picture is an optional third stream — whether it gets recorded depends on the channel schedule.

Fusion Lives at the Camera; Rules Are Pushed Down

The fusion and PiP modes from the animation are composed in the camera — NVRs and platforms only display them. Temperature rules (where to measure, what threshold, what emissivity) follow the same pattern: draw the rule in the NVR UI → push it to the camera → the camera computes → it returns events and snapshots on trigger. Temperature computation does not happen on the NVR.

Standards: ONVIF Has a Dedicated Thermal Service

ONVIF supports thermal not through a profile but through a standalone extension: the Thermal Service Specification (current version 22.06). It configures color palettes (WhiteHot/BlackHot/grayscale), polarity, NUC tables, and radiometry parameters; devices supporting radiometry must provide the base RadiometryAlarm event. Temperature readings travel in the Analytics service’s metadata stream: spot measurements map to the RadiometrySpot analytics module with readings delivered as SpotTemperatureReading events — which rides on Profile T’s metadata streaming and Profile M’s analytics metadata configuration. The spec defines eight radiometry parameters: emissivity, reflected ambient temperature, distance to object, atmospheric temperature, atmospheric transmittance, relative humidity, and ext-optics temperature/transmittance.

GB/T 28181 is far more conservative: the directory can tag a thermal channel, but the alarm enumerations carry no fire or over-temperature scene types (only the device’s own high/low-temperature alarms), and no in-stream temperature metadata is defined — under H.264/H.265 + 28181, thermal events ride vendor-private AlarmType extensions or side channels, with poor cross-vendor interoperability. The Chinese standard ecosystem’s answer is SVAC encoding (GB/T 25724) with its surveillance extension metadata, but that ecosystem is limited to domestic SVAC cameras.

At the RTSP layer there is no thermal-specific URL convention — channels are distinguished by number: Hikvision visible main stream rtsp://user:pass@ip:554/Streaming/Channels/101, thermal .../Channels/201.

Vendor SDKs: The Real Interfaces Live Here

What the standards expose is a subset. Temperature rules, per-pixel temperatures, and fire alarms — the actual features — sit in vendor SDKs:

  • Hikvision HCNetSDK: NET_DVR_THERMOMETRY_COND pushes measurement rules (point/line/polygon + threshold + emissivity/distance/reflected temperature); NET_DVR_THERMOMETRY_UPLOAD streams live readings; fire alarms arrive via the alarm callback with type COMM_FIREDETECTION_ALARM (0x4991); NET_DVR_CaptureJPEGPicture_WithAppendData grabs a JPEG with a per-pixel float temperature matrix appended (4 bytes per pixel). On the HTTP side, ISAPI mirrors this at GET/PUT /ISAPI/Thermal/channels/{id}/thermometry/{ruleId}.
  • Dahua NetSDK: radiometry data callback fRadiometryAttachCB, analytics callback fAnalyzerDataCallBack (event code EVENT_IVS_TEMPERATUREMEASURE), config via CLIENT_GetDevConfig/CLIENT_SetDevConfig.

Caveat: trust the header files you actually have. Names like NET_DVR_THERMOCAMUPLOAD circulate online but return zero hits in official documentation — they’re garbled.

The industry consensus is clear: Western VMS platforms treat thermal as an ordinary video stream — temperatures are computed in-camera, pushed as events, burned into the frame, or bolted on via plugins. The NVRs that actually put temperature rules, fire alarms, and event search in the recorder itself are Hikvision’s and Dahua’s own. If radiometry is the core requirement, “camera + same-vendor NVR” is the path of least resistance.

Storage and Bitrate

Thermal streams are low-resolution (384×288 or 640×512), typically 1–2 Mbps ≈ 11–22 GB/day; a 4MP visible stream in H.265 runs ~4 Mbps ≈ 43 GB/day. The fusion stream is a third — recording it counts a third channel’s storage. The standard disk-saving move is event-linked recording: thermal frames are mostly static so the stream bitrate is low anyway, and recording only alarm windows keeps the thermal channel’s footprint modest. Playback search is by event type — filter time ranges by over-temperature or fire alarms, not by temperature values.

The State of the Industry

Market: $7B Overall, Security ~40%

Institutional estimates vary but agree in magnitude: Yole’s Thermal Imaging & Sensing 2025 puts the 2024 thermal imaging and sensing market above $7 billion; the uncooled detector segment alone is ~$1.3B in 2024, heading to ~$1.9B by 2030 (6–7% CAGR); security accounts for 37.6% of thermal imaging systems (Mordor Intelligence, 2025). The military market is larger, but civilian is growing faster.

Landscape: Chinese Vendors Ship 60% of Sensors

The hierarchy inverted over the past five years. Per Yole, roughly 60% of the world’s thermal imaging sensors in 2024 came from Chinese vendors; the top five (Teledyne FLIR, Raytron, HikMicro, Lynred, Seek Thermal) hold 88%. In shipment volume, Raytron grew from 472k units in 2022 to 1.05 million in 2024 — first place in both volume and revenue for the first time; HikMicro shipped 645k for second; GuideIR around 184k units at ~8% revenue share. As recently as 2020 the story was still FLIR leading — the reversal happened in the uncooled civilian segment, not the military one.

Cost Curve: Packaging and Pixel Pitch

Over half the cost of an uncooled detector is packaging, and the packaging route — metal → ceramic → wafer-level (WLP) → pixel-level — drops a price tier at each step. 12 µm + WLP is now in volume production, which is what opened the consumer market: 256×192 modules put phone-attached thermal imagers at the thousand-yuan level. Smaller pitches (8 µm, 6 µm, already demonstrated) keep shrinking germanium lens diameters — germanium and diamond-turning are unavoidable thermal-camera costs, with metasurface optics as a potential future replacement.

Trends, 2024–2026

  • Resolution climbing: security mainstream is shifting from 384×288 to 640×512; heavy PTZ platforms have reached 1280×1024 (Raytron IRS-PT4S4, HikMicro dual-spectrum heavy PTZ).
  • Radiometry standardizing: ±2 °C/±2% is the factory-calibration baseline for industrial dual-spectrum cameras, with range extending from 150 °C to 550 °C.
  • Edge AI: thermal detection + visible identification + on-camera classification is now standard equipment (person/vehicle classification and dynamic fire detection ship on Hikvision DS-2TD by default); in September 2025 Hikvision announced 96% accuracy for multimodal-LLM wildfire detection — large models are entering thermal analytics.
  • Price stratification: Chinese 640×512 dual-spectrum PTZ has been bid down to the ~$600 level in B2B channels — a one-to-two-order-of-magnitude gap against Western units at $20k+.
  • Export-control chess: cooled-detector materials (HgCdTe) and micro-coolers remain under export controls, while China’s own export controls on germanium and gallium now push back on the global supply chain — both directions are squeezing.

FAQ

Can thermal see through walls? No. 8–14 µm doesn’t even pass ordinary glass (hence germanium lenses), let alone walls. What you see on a wall is the wall’s own surface temperature — heating-pipe routes and the warmth of someone recently standing there show up as surface temperature, not X-ray vision.

Can it tell who someone is? Under the Johnson criteria, security-grade thermal typically reaches “recognize the silhouette”; identity comes from the visible channel. That division of labor is the whole reason dual-spectrum exists: thermal raises the alarm, visible light points at the face.

How accurate is the temperature, really? The factory figure is ±2 °C or ±2% (whichever is greater), measured against a calibrated blackbody. The dominant field error is emissivity: polished metal can be as low as 0.1–0.3, and a wrong setting is off by tens of degrees — the camera isn’t broken; you’re just not measuring the object you think you are.

Can a consumer thermal imager do security duty? No. Frame rate (many overseas consumer units run 9 Hz due to US export controls; security units run 25/50 fps), radiometric calibration (consumer units don’t ship calibrated or are coarsely calibrated), and ingress protection (a phone accessory has no IP66) are different worlds.

Why is dual-spectrum expensive? Two optics + two sensors + a factory registration line; germanium lenses are the big line item. With domestic 12 µm + WLP modules pushing core prices down, more and more of the system premium sits in radiometric calibration and analytics.

Getting Started

Buy-a-system route (you want it working): a 640×512 radiometric dual-spectrum bullet (something like the Dahua TPC-BF5601-T) + the same vendor’s NVR (thermal-channel support is most complete within one vendor) + a platform/alarm linkage. Budget from a few thousand to tens of thousands of yuan; half a day to install; draw measurement rules in the NVR UI.

DIY route (you want to understand it or build a product): a thermal core module (Raytron MicroIII, FLIR Lepton/Boson, GST or HikMicro cores) + an embedded SoC board + your own imaging pipeline, radiometry chain, and protocol stack. Modules run from hundreds to thousands of yuan; the work is in software. How to walk that path is the subject of the companion post, “Building Dual-Spectrum Thermal Cameras on Embedded: From Core Module to NVR.”
