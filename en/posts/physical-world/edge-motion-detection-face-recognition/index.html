Motion Detection & Face Recognition on Edge Devices: The Resource–Accuracy Trade-off, from Classical to Lightweight AI

August 6, 2026 August 12, 2026 Physical World Development Computer Vision, Motion Detection, Face Recognition, Edge Computing, Model Compression, Selection Signals & Image Processing Series 2700 words 6 min read
🔊

On a Raspberry Pi, an IPC SoC, or an AI box with only a fraction of a TOPS, the hard part of motion detection and face recognition isn’t whether you can do it — it’s how much resource you spend for how much accuracy. Once the compute and memory budget is fixed, every choice answers the same question: what can this tier solve, what can’t it, and what does it cost to close the gap.

This post walks both pipelines (motion detection, face recognition) in order of increasing resource cost. At each tier it covers what a classical method can do and where it breaks, then which lightweight model happens to close that exact gap and at what precision. A tiered selection decision tree closes it out.

Motion detection: four resource tiers

Motion detection separates “moving foreground” from “static background” across frames. The more complex the scene (lighting changes, dynamic backgrounds, shake), the heavier the algorithm. From cheapest to heaviest, roughly four tiers.

Tier 1: frame differencing (no model, minimal cost)

Take the grayscale difference between adjacent frames; pixels above a threshold are foreground. Three-frame differencing ANDs |f(t)-f(t-1)| with |f(t+1)-f(t)| to fill the “holes” two-frame differencing leaves.

  • Does: real-time “is anything moving” at very low power — 80–120 fps at 1080P, a dozen-odd MB of memory.
  • Breaks: stationary targets vanish (a person who stops disappears), holes, noise sensitivity.

This is the ceiling of zero-model approaches — cheap, but it can’t see things that stop moving.

Tier 2: background subtraction (mainstream, fills holes and dynamic backgrounds)

Maintain a background model and subtract the current frame from it. The differences are in how the model is built.

  • ViBe: non-parametric; each pixel keeps N random samples (paper defaults N=20, R=20, #min=2, φ=16). The core is integer comparison + random sampling, no floating point — ARM-NEON friendly, cache-friendly, which is the real reason it suits embedded devices. Lowest cost among background subtractors. Breaks: sensitive to the purity of the first frame (don’t update the model or use median init for the first 30–100 frames), weak under sudden lighting changes.
  • MOG2 (GMM): 3–5 Gaussians per pixel with dynamic weights. Good dynamic-background adaptation, adaptive variance, built-in shadow detection. Mid cost, ~45 fps at 1080P.
  • KNN: each pixel keeps a history queue; distance to K nearest decides. Best dynamic-background handling and catches briefly stationary objects, but memory clearly higher than MOG2 (ratio depends on history and mixture count — no fixed multiplier).

One shared catch: no background-subtraction method handles stationary targets — once an object stops, the background model absorbs it. Closing that gap requires stepping up to AI.

Tier 3: optical flow (highest accuracy, heaviest)

Track pixel-level motion vectors to get direction and speed; the most accurate. Lucas-Kanade (sparse) is lighter; Farneback/DIS (dense) are heavy. Optical flow is expensive — dense flow especially — and generally reserved for high-performance devices or detailed motion-field analysis.

Tier 4: lightweight AI (closes the classical gaps)

Classical methods hit a precision ceiling and struggle with occlusion, small objects, crowds, or anything needing classification. Deep learning fills that, at a cost. The idea: use a lightweight detector to replace or assist background subtraction:

YOLOv5s (original)~7.2M~90%goodgeneral detection
NanoDet + attention~1M~88%excellentmobile real-time detection

Hybrid pipeline: deep-learning accuracy on a classical budget

On edge, the practical move isn’t running AI alone but a two-stage cascade: a classical method does cheap first-pass filtering, and AI runs only where there’s motion.

The effect is to cut deep-learning compute sharply — AI runs on foreground regions, not the full frame. On devices like Jetson Nano, end-to-end throughput rises several-fold over full-frame AI with almost no precision loss.

Face detection: an accuracy-vs-resource scale

Face recognition has two steps: face detection (where the face is) → face recognition (whose it is). Different models, very different cost and accuracy. On the detection side, models line up on a scale by params/mAP:

ModelYearParamsmAP (WIDER Hard)Edge fitNotes
Viola-Jones (Haar cascade)2001tinywell below DLusable, datedclassical; breaks under pose/lighting change
MTCNN2016~millions~75% (third-party)usablethree-stage cascade, 5 keypoints; original paper reports FDDB, not WIDER
BlazeFace2019tiny (hundreds of thousands)—excellentGoogle, for phones; short-range 128×128, sub-ms on GPU; very small
SCRFD-500M20210.57M68.5%excellent0.5 GFLOPs, the floor
SCRFD-2.5G (no keypoints)20210.67M77.9%greatno-keypoints variant
SCRFD-2.5G_KPS20210.82M77.1%greatwith 5 keypoints (for recognition alignment)
SCRFD-10G20213.86M83.1%goodhigh-accuracy tier
YuNet202375,856 (~0.076M)~70.8%极致/ultimateOpenCV Zoo native, sub-MB weights
RetinaFace (R50)2019~25–30M~91% (needs extra data)heavybest on small/occluded faces, needs GPU

A few traps:

  • YuNet’s “81% mAP” has no source. The OpenCV Zoo model card’s WIDER Hard figure is about 70.8%; some local reproductions reach ~75%; the widely quoted 81.1% can’t be traced — don’t cite it. Its real selling point is extremely few params (76K), sub-MB weights, native OpenCV loading — resources pushed to the floor while staying “good enough.”
  • Distinguish SCRFD “2.5G” with vs without keypoints. SCRFD_2.5G (0.67M, 77.9%) has no keypoints; the 5-keypoint SCRFD_2.5G_KPS is 0.82M, 77.1%. Recognition needs keypoints for alignment, so pick the _KPS variant.
  • RetinaFace’s 91% top number depends on extra training data. A bare RetinaFace-R50 doesn’t reach 91% on WIDER Hard; that figure comes from added data, and the model is ~100MB with hundreds of milliseconds on edge CPU — realistically server or GPU-edge only.

Face recognition: the cost of an embedding

Recognition maps a detected face to a fixed-length embedding; identity is decided by vector distance.

    L --> M["Cosine similarity vs gallery"]

Skip keypoint alignment and accuracy drops 15–20%. Matching is cosine similarity on L2-normalized vectors: sim = dot(query, gallery) / (‖query‖ × ‖gallery‖), with thresholds typically 0.5–0.65.

ModelLFW accuracyModel sizeEmbedding dimEdge fitLicense
FaceNet99.63%35–220MB128 (original) / 512 (common reimpl)heavyresearch
ArcFace ResNet-50/10099.8%166–249MB512heavyresearch
MobileFaceNet~99.5%~4MB512excellentresearch
SFace99.40%~37MB (INT8 ~19MB)128greatApache-2.0 (commercial-ok)

Notes:

  • MobileFaceNet is not 1MB. Its 512-d model has under 1M params and is about 4MB in FP32 (smaller after INT8); the “1.1MB” figure online doesn’t match the param count. LFW ~99.5%, a common edge recognizer. Its global depthwise convolution (GDConv) buys accuracy, not param reduction — “GDConv cuts 78% of params” is unsourced.
  • For commercial use, look at SFace. ArcFace/MobileFaceNet are mostly research-licensed and risky to ship. SFace is explicitly Apache-2.0 in OpenCV Zoo — LFW 99.40%, ~19MB INT8 — the realistic “legally shippable” edge choice.

Threshold tuning is scene-dependent: high-security/surveillance 0.6–0.7 (accept slightly higher reject rate), access/attendance 0.5–0.6, general recognition 0.4–0.5; for ID photos issued years ago, lower by another 0.03–0.05 to absorb aging. At registration, store 3–5 embeddings per person across lighting/angles — cheaper and more privacy-compliant than storing images.

Inference runtimes: picking the model isn’t enough

The same model performs very differently across runtimes.

One caveat: MNN’s “1.8×/3.5× faster than TFLite” is Alibaba’s own benchmark — the direction holds (community reproductions agree MNN is fast on ARM), but the exact multipliers are vendor figures, not independent measurements. The same goes for NCNN’s “28ms for YOLOv5s on Snapdragon 888” — a community-tuned typical value, not an official guarantee.

Runtime quick-pick: iOS → Core ML; Android (Qualcomm/MediaTek) → MNN or NCNN; with an NPU (HiSilicon/Rockchip) → TFLite (broadest NNAPI); Intel edge box → OpenVINO; zero-dependency → load ONNX straight through OpenCV DNN.

Model compression: INT8 first; don’t buy “67% combined usage”

When an off-the-shelf lightweight model still exceeds budget, compression carves out another tier.

ConversionSize cutSpeedupAccuracy lossNotes
FP32 → FP1650%1.5–2×negligibleFP16-capable GPUs
FP32 → INT875% (4×)2–4×usually <1%ARM/NPU default; PTQ ~<1%, <0.5% needs QAT
FP32 → INT487.5%4–8×1–3%extreme compression, needs calibration
  • Say “<1%” for INT8 loss to be safe. Post-training quantization (PTQ) keeps sub-1B-param models generally within 1%; the oft-quoted “<0.5%” usually needs quantization-aware training (QAT) — not free.
  • Pruning: channel pruning removes another 30–40% (<0.5% loss); structured pruning needs retraining.
  • Knowledge distillation: train the small model under a large one to recover post-compression accuracy.

One circulating claim — “67% of practitioners combine knowledge distillation + quantization + pruning” — has no survey or industry-report source and is most likely a fabricated statistic; don’t cite it. The three are genuinely often combined (distillation for accuracy + pruning for size + INT8 for speed), but attaching a fake percentage to it isn’t on.

Face-recognition trade-off is simpler: commercial use → SFace (Apache-2.0); extreme resource constraints → MobileFaceNet INT8; top accuracy → ArcFace.

At the floor: MCU cameras like the ESP32-CAM

Push the budget down one more tier and you reach the bottom of the “camera + MCU” category — the ESP32-CAM (ESP32, dual-core 240MHz, 520KB SRAM plus external PSRAM, no NPU). This is the real hardware behind the “＜0.5 TOPS” branch of the decision tree above, and it’s worth spelling out what it can and can’t do.

Motion detection: pixel-level frame differencing in firmware (two-frame grayscale diff → threshold → morphological denoise → multi-frame confirmation) runs comfortably, real-time at QQVGA/QVGA. This is the ESP32-CAM’s sweet spot — the MCU can’t run a background model, so “trigger on motion” is its natural use. ViBe is theoretically possible, but N samples × every pixel is unfriendly to PSRAM bandwidth and latency, so in practice people fall back to frame differencing.

Face detection: the ESP32 has no AI accelerator, but Espressif’s ESP-WHO library ships MTMN, a lightweight MTCNN replacement quantized for the ESP32, which does low-framerate face detection at QVGA. The ESP32-S3 has vector instructions and, with ESP-DL, can run a slightly larger quantized model (e.g. SCRFD-tiny); boards with a KPU (like the K210) have more headroom.

Face recognition: doing the full pipeline (detect + align + 512-d embedding + match) on an ESP32-CAM is a stretch — it usually only does “detect + track”. Espressif’s own face_id can do recognition against a small gallery, but gallery size and accuracy are limited, so treat attendance/access-control grade results with caution.

In practice, the dominant pattern for these floor-tier cameras is “trigger on the edge, recognize at the gateway”: the ESP32-CAM handles motion/face triggering and capture, then pushes the ROI or JPEG over Wi-Fi/MQTT to an NPU-equipped edge box (or server) that runs MobileFaceNet/SFace for recognition. This is just the hybrid pipeline from earlier, split across two devices — the first stage lives in a few KB of MCU RAM, the second on a box with real compute.

Reference stack

Putting the pieces together, a typical edge stack is: ViBe (floating-point-free) for motion → foreground ROI only → YuNet for face detection → MobileFaceNet (or commercial SFace) for embeddings → cosine similarity match. Total model size in the single-digit MBs, memory in the tens of MB, end-to-end per-frame in the tens of milliseconds — right inside a constrained device’s budget.
