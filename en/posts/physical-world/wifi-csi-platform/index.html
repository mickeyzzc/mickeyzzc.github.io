From Signals to Systems: How Platforms Build on WiFi Sensing

September 14, 2026 September 14, 2026 Physical World Development WiFi Sensing, CSI, Home Assistant, Matter, Smart Home, Edge Computing Signals & Image Processing Series 3018 words 15 min read
🔊

The previous three posts covered the physics, the detection algorithms, and two open-source projects — but between all that and “the lights come on by themselves” sits one more layer: the platform. Raw CSI and detector scores are for machines; a smart home consumes states like “bathroom occupied” or “no activity from grandma for an hour” that automations can be written against. This post takes the platform layer apart: how scores become events, events become semantics, semantics reach Home Assistant and Matter, how commercial platforms split edge and cloud, and how 802.11bf writes those roles into the protocol itself. Every mechanism comes with a living example — ESPectre and RuView on the open-source side, Cognitive Systems and ADT on the commercial side.

The platform stack: where features are born

Slice the platform side horizontally and you get four layers, each with its own duty and output:

    E --> P["Platform layer<br/>entities + API + rules<br/>output: entities, automations"]

The layers are not boxes for drawing’s sake — each layer absorbs one class of errors for the one above: the device layer uses scale-invariant features to absorb gain steps, the edge layer uses debounce and rules to absorb single misreads, the platform layer uses entity models and permissions to absorb accidental triggers, and the ecosystem layer uses external conditions like time and away-status to absorb actions that are technically correct but contextually wrong. Layer by layer:

From score to event: debounce, hysteresis, cooldown

The detector emits a probability dozens of times per second, but automations cannot consume it directly — the variance of the radio environment is what it is, and even a 97%-accurate detector leaves dozens of chances per hour to be wrong, while smart homes tolerate false alarms at nearly zero. One false trigger at 3 a.m. and the user disables the whole integration.

Eventization is therefore a three-piece toolkit. Debounce: require N consecutive agreeing evaluations before flipping — ESPectre exposes it as motion_on_hits: 4 / motion_off_hits: 3. Note that the in and out counts differ: stricter to enter, looser to leave. That asymmetry is hysteresis: once flipped, the entity does not jump back on a single “clear” evaluation, so a borderline signal cannot make the entity chatter. Cooldown: trigger-class events (a fall, say) go silent for a window after firing — RuView’s fall detection debounces 3 frames and then cools down 5 seconds, because a person does not fall three times in one second but oscillating signals absolutely alert three times in one second. All three are state machines of a dozen lines, and all three are the entire distance between “works in the demo” and “trusted in the home”.

From event to semantics: what platforms actually consume

“Motion” is too low-level to write “elderly inactivity” against, so platforms add a semantic state layer above events: rules with time windows and compound conditions.

RuView’s take is the most mature: each node exposes 21 entities — 11 raw signals plus 10 semantic states (someone-sleeping, possible-distress, bathroom-occupied, elderly-inactivity-anomaly, and so on), and every semantic state ships with its own precision/recall report. That design deserves a pause: the honest description of a sensing system is not “it can detect falls” but “the possible-distress state measures X% precision and Y% recall on the test set”. Decomposing fuzzy perception into quantifiable events is what makes a platform operable — when a false positive lands, you locate the exact rule, read its metrics, tune its parameters, instead of concluding “the AI acted up again”.

The semantic rules are plain combinations: bathroom-occupied = motion at the bathroom node and not cleared within N minutes; elderly-inactivity = no activity anywhere during daytime hours while activity history says someone is home; someone-sleeping = long stillness in the bedroom combined with a breathing signature. None of them is a black box — which is exactly why each rule can carry its own metrics.

The animation plays the whole path from signal to light: raw CSI amplitude crosses the threshold (red dot), the detector’s vote lights one slot of the debounce counter, four filled slots flip the binary sensor from clear to motion, the semantic layer composes “bathroom-occupied” on top of entities, and the automation adds a time gate before turning the light on. The way back is hysteretic — three consecutive “clear” evaluations to reset. A single misread never reaches the light.

Room-level occupancy: aggregating nodes into a view of the home

One node answers “is anyone near this link”; the platform must answer “which room is occupied, is anyone home”. The common recipe is node-to-room binding plus aggregation, but aggregation is its own state machine, and its two directions are wildly asymmetric in difficulty:

    O -->|"all silent<br/>past vacancy timeout"| V

    style O fill:#4CAF50,color:#fff,stroke:none

Occupied is easy: any node in the room passing debounce sets the state. Vacant is hard — the principles post established that WiFi sensing detects change, not existence, and cannot prove a room is empty. So “unoccupied” is never measured; it is inferred by timeout: when every node stays silent past the vacancy timeout (minutes for a bathroom, a dozen-plus for a living room), the state decays. This is why every occupancy sensor on the market carries a hold-time / vacancy-timeout setting — it is the only safe way to read “not detected” as “nobody there”, and it is the direct root of the classic complaint “I’m typing in the study and the living-room lights went off”: the timeout was set too short. There is no free lunch here — you trade between lights-off-too-early and lights-on-forever per room’s purpose.

Node density and overlap: one node per 50-70 m² (the placement geometry from the RuView post) with overlapping coverage, so one node’s miss is covered by its neighbor. Cross-room tracking (living room → hallway → bedroom trajectories) looks great in academic demos and rarely ships — one calculation shows why: a hallway crossing is two to three meters, walking speed 1 m/s, so a node gets two to three seconds of sampling window, and after debouncing there is not enough left for even one state flip; broken trajectories are the norm. Practical platforms step back to room-level occupancy and leave tracking to purpose-built security deployments. RuView’s room fingerprint is a middle path: no binding, the encoder recognizes “which room is this”, so remapping survives moving a node — at the cost of having collected data in that room first.

Reaching the ecosystem: Home Assistant and Matter

Three interfaces are the de facto standards. MQTT: the device publishes state to topics and HA discovers entities via MQTT Discovery — ESPectre’s browser-flash path takes this route, zero configuration. The ESPHome native API: the device connects straight to HA with entities, diagnostics, and online tuning passed through. Matter: sensing maps to a standard occupancy-sensor cluster that Apple Home, Google Home, Alexa, and SmartThings all recognize — RuView bridges via Matter, while ESPectre’s Matter path is still going through ecosystem validation.

The entity model is deliberate: motion is a binary_sensor (choosing device_class motion vs occupancy matters — motion means “moving this instant” and chatters, occupancy means “someone in the area” and carries hold time), the movement score is a sensor (for graded conditions in automations: “only escalate the notification when the score exceeds X”), and the decision threshold is exposed as a number entity for online adjustment — it is really “how sensitive is this room”, a user preference that deserves the same status as brightness. A set of diagnostic entities rides along, and their value is cashed at troubleshooting time. ESPectre’s diagnostics are four link metrics, each mapping to one failure class:

MetricWhen it drops, it meansWhere to look
Callback ratedriver receives no packets, or the callback is blockedtraffic mode, task blocking
Receive ratepackets in the air but droppedchannel congestion, promiscuous config
Admitted ratereceived but not entering the time gridarrivals too jittery, change traffic source
Slot occupancygrid saturated long-termtarget pps set too high, lower it

The four levels prune bottom-up: first confirm packets reach the driver, then the time grid, and only then suspect the detector — TROUBLESHOOTING.md’s order follows exactly this logic. This “localize the deployment problem to a layer” pattern is worth copying for every sensor-class integration.

One automation written down is only the start; scaling means templating “room + time window + notification target” into parameters — HA Blueprints exist exactly for this, and RuView ships 3 starter Blueprints. Semantic states plus Blueprints is the standard posture for wiring sensing into a platform: adding a room becomes filling in parameters, not copy-pasting YAML.

Commercial platforms: the edge-cloud division of labor

Commercial systems share the physics; they differ in the operations layer. First, the bandwidth calculation that decides the architecture: one CSI node at 100 pps × 56 subcarriers × 4 bytes (int16 I and Q) ≈ 22 KB/s; a four-node mesh runs around 90 KB/s — over 200 GB of raw data per month. Pushing raw CSI to the cloud is uneconomical, latency-hostile, and privacy-indefensible. So the industry converges on funnel-shaped upload: raw signals are digested at the edge, the edge emits only events and semantics (a few per minute, tens of bytes each), and the cloud sees “what happened” without ever seeing “what the signal looked like”.

Cognitive Systems’ WiFi Motion is exactly this shape: sensing software embedded in routers and gateways — it runs on any 802.11n-or-newer platform, deploys over the air, works even with a single access point, and in a mesh every node (IoT devices included) joins the array; real-time motion detection stays on the gateway at the edge, while behavior patterns, history, and model updates run in the cloud (infrastructure built on AWS EKS). The cloud portion solves fleet-scale operations that no edge can replace: false-alarm telemetry across hundreds of households, detection parameters grouped by hardware model, staged rollout of new detection models, threshold baselines compared across home types — only a cloud can aggregate these. Plume’s Motion Aware is this technology inside an ISP’s mesh platform, shipped to homes by the million, invisible to users — sensing is just another gateway service.

ADT, having acquired Origin AI, runs a different loop: sensing plugs into the arming state machine. While armed away, a motion event escalates to an alarm and reaches the professional monitoring center; while home, the identical event is merely an automation input. The security context adds confirmation logic — a single sensor’s motion does not dispatch; multi-source correlation like a door contact followed by motion does, because every professional dispatch costs real money and false alarms eat the margin directly. The same “motion” takes completely different reporting paths in the security context and the home context — that is what the platform layer is for. Huawei builds motion detection into its enterprise APs, selling “reuse the existing network as a perimeter” with no extra sensors — routing undisturbed, security on the side.

802.11bf: writing the roles into the protocol

Open-source projects each invent their own answers to “who transmits, who measures, how do we align” (RuView’s TDM slots, espargos’s shared clock). 802.11bf-2025 standardizes those roles into one interoperable procedure, in three steps.

Step one, Measurement Setup (negotiation): the Sensing Initiator (the side opening the sensing session) and the Sensing Responder (the side performing measurements) exchange and agree on parameters — bandwidth, sounding period, report contents. This turns “subscribing to sensing” into a standard action: the application states what it needs (type, rate, latency) and the network answers what it can provide; the application never parses CSI itself.

Step two, Measurement Instance (measurement): the typical flow has the initiator (commonly the AP) send an NDPA frame announcing, then an NDP sounding frame, with each responder measuring the channel from the received NDP and returning results in feedback frames. The NDP carries no data, only training fields — a frame type born purely for measurement; the responders’ replies ride the existing beamforming-report channel, so the protocol raised no separate stack for sensing.

Step three, Reporting: results return in a unified format. The value of format unification only shows against the status quo: today Intel 5300 and ESP32 CSI formats share nothing, and every dataset, every toolchain writes its own parser; once formats interoperate, the same detection algorithm can finally run across vendors.

Three boundaries are worth knowing. First, bf standardizes MAC-layer procedures — once you have the CSI, filtering, features, and detection live outside the standard, so ESPectre-style algorithm space is not narrowed. Second, whether the AP or a STA initiates maps to different measurement-instance flows; check your role against the table before implementing. Third, RuView’s ADR carries a blunt mapping — TDM transmit slot = Initiator, receive slot = Responder, one TDMA round = Measurement Instance, probe frame = Sensing NDP — open source grew the same architecture by itself and converged with the standard, which confirms these roles are the natural shape of such systems. The endgame: once negotiation and report formats interoperate, cross-vendor sensing (“your router triggers, my gateway measures”) gains a protocol foundation, and sensing graduates from vendor-private feature to basic network service.

Privacy engineering: the platform layer’s required course

A WiFi sensing device is by nature a sensor that can see your patterns of life, and on the platform side privacy is an architecture problem, not a disclaimer. Rank the data by sensitivity and the design principle falls out: raw CSI (KB/s, theoretically invertible to pose and routine) → features (a few numbers per second) → events (a few per minute) → semantic states (a few per hour). Each rung up drops sensitivity and invertibility by an order, while giving up nothing automations need. The bottom line is therefore layered: expose semantics instead of events where possible, events instead of features, and raw signals never leave the node by default.

RuView’s BFLD layer turns the principle into engineering: raw beamforming feedback never leaves the node, identity embeddings live in RAM only, cross-site correlation is made cryptographically infeasible with per-day-rotated BLAKE3 keyed hashes — daily rotation means even a leaked correlation key burns only that day — and an Ed25519 witness chain gives measurement records tamper-evident attestation. ESPectre processing locally with raw CSI upload off is the same principle in its single-node edition.

Commercial platforms carry more pressure: whose gateway holds the data, what reaches the cloud, how long it is retained, who can access — questions ISPs genuinely ask at procurement. The edge/cloud split is accidentally the right privacy answer too: the cloud holds only events and statistics, so the leak surface is small by construction. For the integrating engineer, one self-check suffices: does any field your platform exposes let a third party infer “what time this household goes to bed”? If yes, cut one layer down.

From signal to notification: the latency budget

Lay each function’s “signal to notification” time in a table and another layer of platform design shows itself — each function’s time constant is set by the physical quantity it measures, not by compute:

FunctionSignal → notificationWhere the time goes
Motion state flipabout 4-5 s1 s detection window × 4 debounces
Fall alertseconds3-frame debounce, fires immediately; cooldown only suppresses repeats
Breathing BPM10-30 sa 0.1-0.5 Hz signal needs one or two full cycles before BPM is credible
Room vacancyminutesthe vacancy timeout is the safe reading of “not detected” — slow beats wrong
Elderly inactivityhoursthe semantic definition itself is “prolonged no activity”

Motion must be fast — falls and intrusions are second-scale events. Vitals must be slow — a jittering BPM is unreadable; stability is what makes it consumable. Vacancy must be slower still — misjudging “someone home” as away (heating off at night, away-mode triggered) costs far more than a few minutes of lag. Latency is not a performance defect; it is each function’s own physical constant, and the platform layer’s job is to hold those constants separately instead of flattening them into one global refresh rate.

That closes the loop across four posts: how radio waves write the room into the signal (principles), how signals become “is anyone moving” (ESPectre), how they become “where and in what pose” (RuView), and finally how they become “the home knows what happened and reacts” (this post). The physics is shared; each step upward looks more like a product — and the reliability of every layer is the reason the next layer dares to trust it.
