Building Dual-Spectrum Thermal Cameras on Embedded: From Core Module to NVR

October 13, 2026 October 24, 2026 Physical World Dev Thermal Imaging, Dual-Spectrum, Embedded, NVR, Image Processing Signals & Image Processing Series 2764 words 14 min read
🔊

The companion post surveyed the technology, the NVR ecosystem, and the market. This one builds: say you have a thermal core on your desk — a Raytron 640×512 module, or a FLIR Lepton — and the goal is a dual-spectrum camera a stock NVR can manage. The hurdles, in order: the imaging pipeline, the radiometry chain, dual-channel registration, multi-stream encoding, platform protocols, analytics, and acceptance testing.

The thermal core and the visible sensor each image into the same IPC SoC (HiSilicon Hi3516DV500, SigmaStar, Rockchip — dual MIPI input is the hard requirement). The SoC runs thermal image processing, registration/fusion, dual-stream encoding, radiometry, and event reporting, then hands RTSP/ONVIF or GB28181 streams to the NVR. Worth knowing when picking the SoC: platforms like the Rockchip RV1126B ship native thermal support — beyond dual MIPI-CSI, the ISP includes IRFPA (infrared focal-plane array) processing: pixel-offset calibration (OCC), automatic NUC, de-striping, and dynamic-range compression all live in the hardware pipeline, effectively pushing part of the step-two image chain into silicon, with a 3 TOPS NPU alongside for person/vehicle classification. Three budgets to watch the whole way: memory (per-pixel temperature matrices and registration tables are not small), compute (640×512@25fps means 8M pixels/second through the pipeline), and bandwidth (two to three streams).

Embedded thermal starts with “which core.” The main options, by resolution and integration:

The first fork is the output format: modules with a built-in ISP output YUV (easy; image quality is bound to the module’s algorithms, and radiometry usually travels on a separate channel), or you take RAW14 and build the pipeline yourself (controllable; you can optimize radiometry and fusion, at the price of owning NUC/AGC). Consumer modules (Tiny1-C class) lead with YUV; industrial cores offer both.

Selection runs into price quickly. 2026 project-level bands: 256×192 / 384×288 cores run ¥1,000–8,000; 640×512 (12/17 µm) ¥8,000–40,000, with Chinese white-label export pricing down to USD 550–680; 1024/1280-class starts at ¥50,000; cooled MWIR spans ¥80,000–300,000. A few public anchors: TypCor-S640 (640×512/12 µm/≤35 mK/50 Hz) from USD 679, USD 638 at 10+ units; Yantai Botu BT-SE series (same spec) from USD 550, MOQ 2; Lecosi FTM640 (USB/CVBS, lens included, <25 g) at ¥4,999. Two modules both labeled “640×512” can be several times apart in quote — the difference is factory radiometric calibration (multi-point blackbody calibration costs money), the lens, and the completeness of the on-module algorithm chain. Align those three before comparing prices.

The second fork is the physical interface:

  • I2C (MLX90640): an MCU reads the temperature array directly at 8 Hz — no “image” to speak of
  • VoSPI (Lepton): a proprietary SPI timing protocol, unpacked line by line; bit-banging it on an SoC SPI peripheral is miserable. The easy road is GroupGets’ PureThermal board, whose firmware turns the Lepton into a standard UVC camera — plain /dev/video0 on Linux
  • MIPI CSI: the standard for real cores, into the SoC’s second MIPI lane — note many cores are not standard sensor timing, so you write a sensor driver shim (on HiSilicon SDKs, a sensor extension)
  • Ethernet: heavy cores speak RTSP or private protocols directly; your SoC demotes to a relay/fusion role

For early validation, take the UVC road: Lepton + PureThermal into Linux, grab RAW14 frames with v4l2-ctl, get the pipeline working, then swap to a MIPI core on real hardware.

Step Two: The Imaging Pipeline — an ISP Without 3A

The thermal pipeline shares almost nothing with a visible ISP: no Bayer pattern means no demosaic, and none of the auto-exposure/white-balance machinery that depends on scene illumination. What it gains are NUC and AGC — thermal-specific and both load-bearing. The animation runs top to bottom in data order.

NUC (non-uniformity correction) is the first hurdle. Every microbolometer pixel has its own response curve and offset; uncorrected, the frame is full of fixed stripes (fixed pattern noise, FPN). The engineering recipe has three parts:

  1. Factory calibration against a uniform source, producing per-pixel gain/offset coefficients stored in flash
  2. At runtime, a mechanical shutter periodically blocks the lens and the uniform reference updates the coefficients — that’s the “click” every thermal camera makes every minute or so
  3. Shutterless designs estimate drift from scene motion frame by frame — no moving parts, much harder algorithms; Raytron’s shutterless cores take this road

The frame freezes while the shutter fires. Handle it in the encoder: hold the previous frame or skip encoding during the shutter window, or recordings will carry a rhythmic stutter.

AGC (automatic gain control) answers “how does 14-bit data become an 8-bit display.” The raw dynamic range far exceeds 8 bits, and the mapping decides what you see:

  • Linear stretch: trivial, but wide-temperature-range scenes go all-white or all-black
  • Histogram equalization: maximum contrast, and maximum amplified noise
  • Plateau histogram equalization (PHE): caps the per-bin amplification — the mainstream engineering compromise in thermal

One practical trap: keep the measurement mapping separate from the viewing mapping. AGC is dynamic; gray levels jump. Overlaying temperature readings on post-AGC gray produces the uncanny “value unchanged, color changed” display bug. Draw measurement overlays from a locked mapping, or straight on a temperature-domain pseudo-color layer.

Pseudo-color is a 256-entry LUT (WhiteHot, ironbow, rainbow). Cost: negligible.

Step Three: The Radiometry Chain

From “the pixel looks bright” to “the number you can report” runs a separate calibration chain.

One sentence of theory: pixel response is proportional to target radiance, and radiance is a monotonic function of temperature — so calibration means building the response-to-temperature inversion curve. Vendors do multi-point blackbody calibration at the factory (record responses against a set of known temperatures) and fit calibration parameters (FLIR’s radiometry model ships as a set of R/S/B/C/O coefficients; domestic cores usually ship a temperature table), then invert at runtime.

A calibrated camera still has to clear the parameter gate in the field. Every link in the radiative transfer chain moves the reading; the eight parameters in the ONVIF spec are the complete field bill:

  • Emissivity: the dominant error source. Polished metal 0.1–0.3, oxidized/rough surfaces 0.9+. Measure a stainless tank at 0.95 without a blackbody tape patch and you’re off by tens of degrees
  • Reflected ambient temperature: the surrounding radiation the target’s surface reflects — huge weight on low-emissivity surfaces
  • Distance to object: atmospheric absorption and spot size both scale with it
  • Atmospheric temperature and humidity: the 8–14 µm transmittance correction

Practical expectations: against a lab blackbody (emissivity 1.0, filling the field of view, controlled reflections) you can honor ±2 °C/±2%; on shiny metal, small targets, or at distance, field error runs several times the spec. The correct mental model for a radiometric camera is trends and relative change (this joint is 15 °C hotter than yesterday), not an absolute thermometer.

When you need the per-pixel temperature field (isotherms, trend analysis), fetch it as a “snapshot with matrix” (Hikvision’s JPEG append data — a 640×512 float temperature frame is 1.25 MB) or a radiometric JPEG (FLIR). Do not try to reconstruct it from the video stream — the stream is post-AGC 8-bit gray, and the temperature information has been irreversibly discarded.

Step Four: Dual-Channel Sync and Registration

Two streams must align — in time and in space — before fusion or joint analytics.

Time sync: both sensors into one SoC is the easy structure; share a clock (or let one lane send frame-sync pulses to the other) and frame-level alignment is free. Split devices (core and visible camera in separate housings) fall back to timestamps: one clock source (PTP or master/slave NTP), error under one frame, or playback and joint analytics drift apart.

Spatial registration: the companion post covered parallax — baseline B between lenses, target distance d, misalignment Δ = B·f/d, inversely proportional to distance. The engineering flow:

  1. With a calibration target (dot grid or crosshair) at a known distance, collect a set of correspondences in both the visible and thermal frames
  2. Fit a homography (3×3, thermal coordinates → visible coordinates; different resolutions are fine, the matrix absorbs the scaling)
  3. Generate remap lookup tables (two 640×512 coordinate tables) and resample by table lookup at runtime

One real constraint: a homography is accurate near the calibration distance (parallax is depth-dependent; a homography assumes a plane). Vendors quote a “best registration distance” (say 50 m or 100 m) — security targets mostly sit beyond it, and the residual misalignment is acceptable. For applications with large depth variation (close-range robots), you need depth-adaptive registration, or you skip fusion and run picture-in-picture.

Step Five: Fusion and Multi-Stream Encoding

The fusion compute spectrum, light to heavy:

  • Alpha blending: fused = α·thermal + (1-α)·visible — one multiply-add per pixel; NEON-optimized, 640×512@25fps uses a fraction of a core; on the SoC’s hardware overlay (semi-transparent OSD layers) it costs no CPU at all
  • Pyramid fusion (Laplacian): visibly better edge preservation; the multi-scale decomposition and reconstruction need careful budgeting on embedded — 25 fps wants an NPU or heavy NEON
  • AI fusion (GAN/diffusion families): best looking, furthest from embedded real-time — papers and flagship demos for now

Encoding arithmetic: 4MP visible H.265 ≈ 4 Mbps; 640×512 thermal H.265 ≈ 1–2 Mbps; the fusion stream costs another thermal-sized chunk. Three streams together: 6–10 Mbps per camera — budget NVR storage on that. One thermal-specific behavior: industrial scenes are largely static so the average bitrate is tiny, but shutter events and AGC jumps cause momentary spikes — set the CBR ceiling with headroom.

Step Six: Platform Integration

At this point your device produces images. Last step: make it look like a proper camera.

Channel registration: follow the industry convention — channel 1 visible, channel 2 thermal. On ONVIF, one Media profile per channel; on GB28181, tag the thermal channel PhotoelectricImagingType=2 in the device catalog so platforms can identify the channel type.

Event reporting: three roads for temperature/fire events — ONVIF RadiometryAlarm (through Profile T’s event framework), vendor SDK alarm subscriptions (HCNetSDK’s COMM_FIREDETECTION_ALARM), and GB28181 alarms (no fire enumeration — private AlarmType extensions only, not portable across vendors). Attach rule name, temperature, coordinates, and a thermal snapshot so the NVR can link recordings and raise wall displays.

Compatibility reality: third-party NVRs (Synology, UniFi, open-source Frigate/ZoneMinder) will treat your device as two ordinary cameras — no measurement-rule UI, no thermal event search. To honor the radiometry features, pair with the same vendor’s NVR, or push events out-of-band into your business system (MQTT/HTTP webhook is the common pattern).

Step Seven: Analytics

The companion post stated the division of labor: detection at the edge, aggregation on the platform. The edge owes two things:

Fire detection. Not one sentence of “alarm when hot” — a bare threshold misfires dozens of times a day (pedestrians, headlights, reflections all qualify). The engineering chain is a gauntlet:

Each gate has a job: the threshold drops the background (typically 60 °C and up, tuned per scene); morphological erosion/dilation kills single-pixel noise and solidifies blobs; the same region must persist N frames (3–5 in practice) — transient interference dies here; finally the temperature rise rate separates “actually igniting” (steep dT/dt) from “warm thing that was always there” (heaters, exhaust pipes). The whole chain runs on the thermal stream; per frame it’s a few threshold sweeps plus one connected-components pass — 640×512@25fps is easy with NEON, or move it into the SoC’s smart-acceleration engine. Keep a false-alarm checklist (sun-heated walls, headlights just switched on, cigarette-sized hot spots) and walk it during acceptance.

Perimeter intrusion. Classic background subtraction (ViBe, MOG) performs poorly on thermal — low texture, low contrast; a pedestrian is a temperature blob. Two viable approaches: thermal-side temperature-anomaly detection (person-shaped warm blobs + size filtering) combined with ordinary person/vehicle recognition on the visible stream, with a joint verdict — thermal fired AND visible identified a person before linkage. That combination is the entire point of the dual-spectrum architecture: identification on thermal alone is unreliable; visible alone is blind at night.

Common Pitfalls

  • Mounting a thermal camera behind glass: 8–14 µm does not pass ordinary glass; outdoor housings need a germanium window. A germanium pane that looks like glass costs thousands — often more than the rest of the housing
  • Reflected metal posing as a hot spot: sunlit stainless reflects ambient radiation and reads 70–80 °C — reflection, not heat. Threshold gates need shape and temporal-persistence checks as backstop
  • AGC jumps spiking the bitrate: when the scene temperature distribution shifts (a door opens, a person walks in), AGC remaps and the whole frame changes — H.265 bitrate doubles momentarily; leave CBR headroom
  • Shutter frames leaking into analytics: the whole-frame change looks like “everything moved” to a motion detector; bypass the shutter window in the analytics pipeline
  • Dual-stream timestamp drift: two independent timestamps drift hundreds of milliseconds over a day and joint playback misaligns — one clock source, periodic re-alignment
  • Open-source NVRs can’t consume thermal events: Frigate/ZoneMinder see “two ordinary video streams”; push events out-of-band to your business system, don’t expect a measurement-rule UI
  • Germanium lens cleaning: no ordinary cloth, no pressure — coating and substrate are soft; follow the vendor’s procedure. Scratches are permanent
  • 9 Hz export units used for tracking: the ≤9 Hz export-friendly cores are cheap and easy to buy — but 9 Hz means one frame every 110 ms, so a drone cruising at 15 m/s moves 1.67 m between frames. Low-altitude flight and PTZ tracking are unusable. For overseas products, either accept 9 Hz for static radiometry only, or solve compliance for the 25/50 Hz standard version
  • Long-focus germanium lenses costing more than the core: border, maritime, and airport projects need long-focus or continuous-zoom infrared lenses (germanium, zinc selenide) that can cost as much as the core itself. Quote “core + lens + mechanics + calibration” as a package; don’t budget from the module price alone
