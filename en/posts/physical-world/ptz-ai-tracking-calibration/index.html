PTZ AI Tracking & Calibration: Gain Scheduling, Dead Time Compensation and Master-Detail

August 2, 2026 Physical World Dev PTZ, AI Tracking, Visual Servoing, Calibration, Kalman Filter Signals & Image Processing Series 3629 words 18 min read
🔊

The site called. AI tracking worked fine at the wide end — the target stayed centered and the gimbal followed it smoothly. Pull to telephoto and the gimbal starts swinging left and right, the swing growing until it finally throws the target out of frame. The first instinct is to turn down the PID’s P — but then the wide end stops keeping up and the target drifts out of frame. Same closed loop, two parameter sets, and neither works.

This isn’t a fluke — it’s two known problems stacking in the same loop. The first post (180) covered the two devils themselves, the second (190) covered four-layer stability defense, the third (200) covered latency breakdown. This one puts both in the same loop: gain scheduling fixes telephoto gain amplification, forward prediction/cascade fixes dead time. The two devils appear together for the first time, so the solution has to come as a set.

Why PTZ Tracking Is Especially Hard

Fixed-camera background modeling (MOG2/ViBe) fails completely on PTZ: gimbal motion causes global pixel displacement, breaking all background models.

Let me explain those two terms first. MOG2 and ViBe are two classic motion-detection algorithms. Both work by first “remembering” the static background — MOG2 records each pixel’s color distribution, ViBe records a sample set per pixel — then comparing each frame against that background; pixels that differ are foreground, i.e. moving objects. On a fixed camera this is fast and cheap, and surveillance has used it for over a decade. But its premise is that the background doesn’t change: when a PTZ turns, the whole frame shifts, every “static pixel” in the background model becomes a “moving pixel,” and the detector outputs wall-to-wall foreground noise.

So PTZ tracking needs a different approach — three engineering routes:

RouteApproachTrade-off
Pure detector-drivenYOLO/RT-DETR full-frame detection per frame + ByteTrack/DeepSORT association, no background dependencyHigh compute; distant small targets missed
Ego-motion compensated foregroundEstimate frame homography H from gimbal feedback or image registration, warp previous frame then differenceFails under motion blur, rolling shutter, or when pure-rotation assumption breaks
Hierarchical: panoramic + detailGun-ball coordination / master-detailRequires calibration

A counterintuitive pitfall: don’t trust pan/tilt feedback during motion. Academic research (SCITEPRESS 2014) explicitly states “internal camera information may not be correlated to real position when it moves” — pan/tilt feedback during motion may not sync with true position.

Two practical approaches: use gimbal feedback for coarse compensation, image registration (ORB/optical flow homography) for residual correction; or position servo + motion trigger condition — issue AbsoluteMove, wait for arrival before sampling, trading smoothness for stability.

Visual Servoing Control Law

First, “servo.” Servo means to drive an actuator with feedback — you set a target, the system measures the gap between current state and target, and drives the motor with that gap until it reaches zero. Visual servoing swaps the feedback signal for an image: the camera watches the target, computes the target’s offset from the frame center, and drives the gimbal with that offset to keep the target centered. It’s the same thing as an operator watching a monitor and pushing a joystick, except “human eye + hand” is replaced by “target detection + control law.”

Visual servoing splits into two schools by where the error is defined:

IBVS vs PBVS:

DimensionIBVS (Image Space)PBVS (Pose Space)
Error definitionPixel space (bbox center offset from frame center)3D pose space (back-projected to ground plane)
Requires depth/calibrationNo, robust to calibration errorsYes, geometric calibration required
Multi-camera coordinationCoordinates inconsistentCoordinates unified, easy coordination
PTZ mainstream✅Used for multi-camera coordination

IBVS (Image-Based Visual Servoing) works directly in the pixel domain: the error is “how many pixels the target center is from the frame center.” PBVS (Position-Based Visual Servoing) back-projects pixels into 3D space and defines the error on the ground plane. Single-PTZ mainstream is IBVS — it doesn’t need to know how far the target is, doesn’t need geometric calibration, and converts pixel offset directly to angular offset, naturally robust to calibration error. PBVS’s advantage is a unified coordinate system, which is what multi-camera coordination (like gun-ball linkage) uses.

Gans/Hu/Dixon (ACC 2009) formalized a 3-DoF image Jacobian with zoom actuation for PTZ, treating zoom as the third control degree-of-freedom, using “feature point mean/variance + motion perceptibility” task function to keep multiple targets in-frame. This is one of the few rigorous formalizations for PTZ IBVS.

Core control law (engineering first-order approximation):

Strict form is θ = arctan((u-c_x)/f_px), but telephoto end is already small-angle regime, linear approximation is sufficient.

Gain Scheduling: The #1 Cause of Telephoto Oscillation

First, “gain.” In a control loop, gain measures how strongly the controller reacts to an error: with a 1° error, a controller of gain 10 outputs ten times what a gain-1 controller does. High gain responds fast but overshoots — too much and you get back-and-forth oscillation; low gain is steady but slow, so the target gets away. The P in PID is the proportional gain; when engineers say “tuning,” they mostly mean this.

Gain scheduling: switch gains by operating point. Aircraft use different control parameters at different altitudes and speeds; a gimbal uses different gains at different focal lengths — same idea, and it comes up again below.

Problem quantification:

ModelRatioHFOV(W) → HFOV(T)Equivalent Gain Diff
Panasonic AW-UE16020×75.1° → 4.0°18.8×
Bolin EXU230H30×58.1° → 2.3°25.3×
Hikvision DS-2DF6A436XG136×56.6° → 1.7°33×

If Kp is fixed at the wide-end tuned value, telephoto equivalent loop gain is amplified 20–33× — limit cycle oscillation is inevitable. This is exactly why “works fine at wide end but oscillates at telephoto.”

“Limit cycle” deserves a sentence. A control system’s output neither converges nor diverges — it orbits forever at a fixed amplitude. For a gimbal that’s the left-right swing: the amplitude is set by system parameters, not external noise, so waiting for it to stop won’t work. It’s the inevitable result of a system with too much gain.

Solution: schedule gain with FOV

1

Kp(z) = Kp0 * HFOV(z) / HFOV_wide

Equivalent to Kp ∝ 1/f_px(z). Update via zoom position lookup.

Deadband must also be scheduled — keep angular deadband constant, not pixel deadband:

1

deadband_px = deadband_deg / HFOV(z) * W

Otherwise telephoto end exhibits “jitter-stop-jitter” sticky behavior.

Dead Time Compensation: What PID Can’t Fix

AXIS 233D/Q6034 measured delay breakdown:

SegmentSymbolMeasured
Capture + network transportτ₁Much smaller than others
Detection/tracking inferenceτ₂Algorithm-dependent
Command issue to gimbal start (dead time)τ₃≈300 ms, independent of movement amplitude
Actual gimbal motionτ₄Amplitude-dependent
τ₃+τ₄ (20° pan-tilt)—400–550 ms

300 ms pure dead time is lethal for IBVS: closed-loop phase margin is consumed, overshoot—undershoot reciprocating oscillation. PID tuning can’t fix this — dead time only eats phase margin without changing magnitude response.

“Phase margin” is control-theory jargon; intuitively it’s the closed loop’s tolerance to delay. Every segment of delay in the feedback path makes the output lag the input by a beat; when the accumulated delay crosses a threshold, the system flips from “correcting error” to “amplifying error” — it swings. The nasty thing about dead time is that it only consumes phase margin and doesn’t change the magnitude response, so raising PID gain just makes the swing worse and lowering it loses the target. That’s the literal meaning of “PID can’t fix it.”

Five compensation methods:

1. Kalman/α-β filter forward prediction (most common)

Use τ₁+τ₂+τ̂₃+τ̂₄ as prediction horizon, extrapolate target position to “when gimbal actually arrives,” not current frame position.

1
2

tau_total = tau1 + tau2 + tau3_hat + tau4_hat(move_amplitude)
target_predicted = kf.predict(dt=tau_total)

One sentence: the position the detector sees is from several hundred milliseconds ago, so driving the gimbal straight at it is driving toward the past. The Kalman filter maintains the target’s velocity/acceleration estimate and uses it to push the position forward to the moment the gimbal actually arrives — the prediction horizon is the sum of those delays.

2. IMM-KF (Interacting Multiple Model, most elegant)

CV (constant velocity)/CA (constant acceleration)/turn multi-model parallel, robust to sudden direction changes.

First, what IMM itself is. The target may be moving at constant velocity, constant acceleration, or turning — any single model only guesses right part of the time. IMM runs three or four models in parallel, each predicting the target’s next position under its own assumption; after the measurement update, whichever predicted best gets its posterior probability raised and the others decay — equivalent to adaptively switching between models.

The elegance: use the model posterior probabilities to drive the zoom strategy. When the three models compete and none dominates, the target’s motion mode is changing violently — extrapolating at current velocity is about to lose it. So zoom out proactively, widen the FOV, and give the target room to be re-captured; once the models re-converge and confidence recovers, push back to telephoto. The traditional approach is “zoom out after losing” — the target has already drifted out of center, and zooming out is a re-search. IMM sees “I’m about to lose it” at the model level, eases off a step first, then tightens — the target stays in frame. This maps controller confidence directly to an optical decision: the error hasn’t happened yet, but the probability is rising, so the optics adjust first. Doing a preventive action before the control loop errors is an idea worth copying into many scenarios.

3. Smith predictor

Model τ₃ as pure lag e^(-τs), then parallel a delay-free gimbal model inside the controller, making feedback act on the predicted output, effectively moving dead time outside the loop.

An intuition for this structure. In a traditional closed loop, every controller action has to wait for the real gimbal to turn and the frame to come back before it sees the result, with 300 ms dead time in between — the controller is driving blind. The Smith predictor plants a mathematical model of the gimbal inside the controller: every command is fed to both the real gimbal and the model gimbal. While the real gimbal is still on its way, the model has already computed “where this command should be in 300 ms,” and the feedback compares against the model output. As long as the model is accurate enough, the 300 ms delay is moved outside the loop and it feels like there’s no dead time.

Prerequisite: the model must be accurate. The gimbal’s accel/decel curve and arrival time are model inputs; a model mismatch introduces new instability — the controller uses “guessed” results as “real” results, and a wrong guess is worse than no guess. In practice, calibrate the accel/decel curve first, then add the Smith predictor — don’t bolt it on cold.

4. Position servo replacing velocity servo

Because angle feedback during motion is unreliable, switch to AbsoluteMove + motion trigger condition. Trade smoothness for stability.

5. Inertial inner loop + visual outer loop cascade (vehicle/mounted essential)

IMU inner loop stabilization (high bandwidth, no visual delay), visual servoing outer loop (low bandwidth). IEEE dual-cascade structure validated on a six-DOF pneumatic hexapod simulating ship motion.

This is the key architectural insight: high-frequency disturbances go to the low-latency inertial loop, low-frequency target tracking goes to the high-latency visual loop. Use bandwidth separation to bypass delay.

Cascade uses bandwidth separation to bypass visual delay: IMU inner loop (purple, high BW) quickly responds to wind/vibration high-frequency disturbances, visual outer loop (blue, low BW) tracks target low-frequency motion. The two loop bandwidths differ by about one order of magnitude — high-frequency disturbances don’t pass through the visual delay chain.

Why “cascade” rather than two loops doing their own thing? Because the outer loop’s output is the inner loop’s input — the angular velocity reference computed by the visual outer loop is handed to the IMU inner loop to execute. The inner loop handles “stabilize,” the outer loop handles “track,” the two don’t interfere, and each only processes signals in its own bandwidth. On vehicle/pole mounts, wind load and vehicle vibration far exceed the gimbal motor’s anti-disturbance ability; without the inner stabilization loop, the visual outer loop sees nothing but shake and detection can’t hold.

Master-Detail (Gun-Ball Coordination)

First, “homography.” It’s the mathematical form of a coordinate translation table: it maps pixel coordinates in one image to the corresponding position in another coordinate system. In gun-ball linkage, the panoramic gun camera sees the target at (x, y) and must tell the dome “which pan/tilt angle to turn to” — that’s the mapping needed. Because targets are near the ground and roughly constant height, both cameras see the same planar scene, and a plane-to-plane mapping happens to be describable by a 3×3 matrix — that’s the homography. In engineering you don’t derive the matrix; you sample N points, calibrate, and build a lookup table of “gun coordinates → dome angles,” interpolating in between.

Three calibration mapping methods:

MethodApproachCharacteristics
Homography/LUTDirect mapping from panoramic image coords → PTZ (pan,tilt), sample N points for interpolationMost common. Dahua government procurement spec explicitly requires “support auto-calibration, calibration points ≥6” — 6 points is industry de facto minimum
Learning-based mappingRBF neural network learns (x_A,y_A,x_B,y_B) → (θ_p,θ_t), then Mean Shift + SOM active vision online feedback refines weightsAnalytic calibration-free, absorbs mechanical errors
Coplanar constraint self-calibrationDual PTZ uses target motion info for coplanar constraint self-calibrationNo calibration target needed, calibration results don’t degrade with pan/tilt/zoom changes

Product form (Hikvision): panoramic perimeter → detect target → link detail PTZ tracking + alert; also supports “click panoramic frame to link detail PTZ” manual tracking.

Note this uses AbsoluteMove (absolute position mode) rather than velocity mode: panoramic-to-detail is “point and shoot,” one step to the target, and the intermediate process doesn’t depend on feedback — which neatly avoids the “angle feedback during motion is unreliable” pitfall from earlier.

PTZ Calibration

Standard model is weak perspective:

1

x = κ · K(z) · R(p,t) · X

Where

1
2
3

K(z) = ⎡ f(z)   s    c_x ⎤
       ⎢  0   α·f(z) c_y ⎥        Typically α=1, s=0
       ⎣  0     0     1  ⎦

R(p,t) is standard pan-tilt rotation composition.

Let me explain what each block does. X is a point in world coordinates (say a target on the ground); R(p,t) is the gimbal rotation matrix, turning world coordinates into “as seen looking in the current pan/tilt direction”; K(z) is the camera intrinsic matrix that projects 3D coordinates into image pixels, where f(z) is the focal length that changes with zoom; κ is a scale factor. Together: target’s world position → gimbal rotation + lens projection → lands on an image pixel. Calibration’s job is to measure the unknowns in this chain — the focal-length-vs-zoom function, principal point position, distortion coefficients.

Three engineering points:

(1) f(z) strongly nonlinear

Vendor zoom position is motor steps, relationship with focal length is nonlinear with hysteresis.

Practical approach:

  • Calibrate at discrete zoom positions, build zoom step → f_px lookup table, interpolate;
  • Or fit exponential/polynomial model;
  • Must record zoom motion direction during calibration — hysteresis causes same step to map to different focal length in opposite directions.

(2) Distortion varies with zoom

Leite/Bernardino/Gaspar method parameterizes (c_x^z, c_y^z, k1^z, k2^z) separately for each zoom position z, identifying an important pattern: radial distortion decreases with focal length — wide end must model it, telephoto end can ignore.

Calibration method: minimize reprojection error of feature points across images with different poses/zoom, no physical camera contact needed (very practical for already-installed high-pole equipment).

(3) Rotation center ≠ optical center (parallax) ⚠️

PTZ-Calib (arXiv 2502.09075) assumes “camera projection center and rotation center have no offset” for efficiency. This assumption doesn’t hold on large-lens PTZs — near-field targets show pure-rotation stitching residuals. Selection/algorithm design must account for this. Correct solution (Sanchez-Riera et al., VISAPP'09): first find gimbal rotation center in world frame; establish “arbitrary rotation ↔ principal point shift” equation, get arbitrary pan/tilt extrinsics; then compute intrinsics as function of zoom; verify stability with inlier/outlier reprojection error.

Segmented Localization Method (debugging essential)

Because jitter exists, must measure distribution (min/avg/max/histogram) not single point — latency tests reporting only one average are not trustworthy.

Why measure distribution rather than a single point? Latency is a random process of several stacked segments, each with its own jitter: sensor exposure phase is uniformly distributed within a frame period (0–33 ms at 30 fps), network jitter and encoder queueing each have variance. A single measurement catches only “one instant’s luck” — two measurements 100 ms apart can differ not because the device changed but because the sampling phase differed. In practice measure 30+ times and look at min/avg/max and histogram shape: if P95 and mean differ a lot, jitter comes from unstable segments like network or buffering; if P95 and mean are close, the latency is stable protocol overhead. Looking only at the average collapses a random process into a point and loses all diagnostic information.

“Short-circuit” each segment sequentially, use difference to back-calculate each segment’s contribution:

StepOperationMeasured segment
(a)Camera HDMI/SDI direct to monitorCapture + ISP + Display
(b)Local ffmpeg -f null encode-decode loopEncode + Decode
(c)Player buffer set 0 vs default comparisonJitter buffer share
(d)Wired LAN vs Wi-Fi/4G comparisonNetwork portion

Auto-Tracking Debugging Key Points

  • Only telephoto end jitters → Gain not scheduled with FOV (most common)
  • All ratios oscillate → Dead time not compensated → Add KF forward prediction / Smith predictor
  • Confused by other moving targets → EIS conflicts with tracking → Use gyroscope prior decoupling

Gain scheduling solves telephoto loop gain amplification, forward prediction or cascade architecture bypasses dead time. Two stability posts + one latency post defenses finally work together in the auto-tracking closed loop.
