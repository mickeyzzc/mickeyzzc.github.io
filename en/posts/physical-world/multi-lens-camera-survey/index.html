Multi-Lens Camera Survey: From Imaging Principles to Liveness Module Integration

October 29, 2026 October 30, 2026 Physical World Development Computer Vision, Stereo Vision, Depth Camera, Structured Light, ToF, Face Recognition, Liveness Detection, Serial Protocols, Selection Signals & Image Processing Series 5469 words 26 min read
🔊

Close one eye and the world doesn’t disappear, but your sense of distance immediately gets unreliable — alternate eyes and objects seem to “jump.” That jump is disparity: brains use it to judge distance, and stereo cameras use it to compute it. A single photograph has no depth, so everything a camera does about “measuring distance, telling live faces from fakes” depends on getting depth back through extra means — and multiple lenses are the most direct route. Phone face unlock, robot-vacuum obstacle avoidance, automotive surround view, door-access face modules: all the same set of principles underneath.

This post walks the whole chain from zero to a working integration. A roadmap first, jump as needed:

Why a photograph has no depth

Imaging is a projection: a 3D point (X, Y, Z) passes through the lens and lands on the sensor as (fX/Z, fY/Z) — the depth Z is divided away at the moment of projection. Two points at different distances but the same direction land on the same pixel. Looking at one photo, you cannot tell whether a point is 1 meter or 10 meters away: one equation, three unknowns, no solution.

So a camera can only recover depth three ways, and every scheme fits one of them:

  • Take another picture: shoot from a second viewpoint and solve for depth from the geometry (stereo, multi-camera);
  • Cast light: project known light into the scene and observe how it deforms or returns (structured light, ToF);
  • Guess from priors: use trained knowledge to estimate depth from a single image (monocular depth estimation).

Start with the classic first route.

Monocular: guess by recognition, or estimate with networks

A single camera has no second viewpoint, but two routes still sense distance.

The first is recognition + prior size: detect “that’s a car,” then use the fact that cars are about 4.5 m long together with how many pixels it occupies to back out the distance via similar triangles. The problem: it only knows categories from the training set, fails on anything new, and degrades with distance. For surveillance tasks — detection, license plates, behavior analysis — the recognition part works fine, but don’t expect precise distances from it.

The second is monocular depth estimation: a neural network predicts per-pixel depth from a single image. Two concepts first:

  • Relative depth: only the near/far ordering is guaranteed; absolute scale is unknown;
  • Metric depth: output carries real meter scale and directly answers “3 meters or 30 meters?”

Anything that makes decisions in meters — robot planning, driving — needs metric depth (or relative depth converted via camera intrinsics), otherwise 3 m and 30 m look the same. AR cutouts and photo 3D effects get by with relative depth. On a related note, the monocular models that surveillance actually runs daily — detection, face recognition — are covered tier-by-tier in my motion detection & face recognition post; I won’t repeat it here.

Stereo: measure disparity, and distance falls out

Place two cameras side by side with a fixed horizontal gap between their optical centers — the baseline B. The same 3D point lands at slightly different horizontal pixel positions in the left and right images; that gap is the disparity d. Similar triangles give the distance directly:

text
1

Z = f × B / d

f is the focal length in pixels (from calibration) and Z is the distance to the object. Example: f = 700 px, baseline B = 6 cm, disparity d = 35 px gives Z = 700 × 60 / 35 = 1200 mm — 1.2 meters.

You can demonstrate disparity on yourself: hold up a finger against a distant background and alternately close each eye — the finger jumps against the background. Bring it close to your nose and repeat: the jump gets much larger. Near means large disparity; far means small. A stereo camera measures that jump pixel by pixel.

In the diagram, the two dots at the top are object points, the two boxes at the bottom are the left and right cameras, and the horizontal line in the middle is the image plane. The same point projects to different positions on the two image planes; that offset is the disparity. The near object’s disparity (orange) is clearly larger than the far object’s (purple). With disparity in hand, Z = f × B / d gives the distance in one step.

Accuracy decays with distance squared

Stereo has an inescapable physical law. Rearranging the formula: at ten times the distance, disparity shrinks to one tenth — and the distance error caused by the same 1-pixel disparity measurement error grows as Z² / (f·B). Distance ×10 means error ×100.

With the same rig (f = 700 px, 6 cm baseline): at 1 meter, a 1-pixel disparity error maps to about 2.4 cm of depth error — fine for obstacle avoidance. At 10 meters, the same pixel maps to about 2.4 meters — the result is unusable. Consumer stereo rigs with 10–15 cm baselines follow the same law. To measure far, either widen the baseline or switch to an active method. Geometry decides this; no code optimization gets around it.

One sentence per stage: calibration uses a checkerboard to recover each camera’s intrinsics and distortion plus the rotation and translation between them — a reprojection RMS under 0.5 px is usable, under 0.2 is clean; rectification reprojects both images onto a common plane so corresponding points land on the same pixel row, turning 2D matching into 1D and cutting compute drastically; matching searches the other image for each pixel’s correspondence and outputs a disparity map; depth computation applies Z = f × B / d to get a depth map or point cloud.

The two most common matching algorithms: BM (block matching) is fast but ragged at edges and fails in low texture; SGBM (semi-global matching) costs about 5× BM’s compute but handles low texture far better and keeps edges clean — it is the usual compromise. Deep-learning matchers (RAFT-Stereo, CREStereo, IGEV-Stereo, FoundationStereo) are more accurate and generalize better, but need a GPU. One OpenCV trap worth knowing: StereoSGBM outputs fixed-point disparity in 16 bits; divide by 16 to get the real value.

Where stereo fails

Disparity is fundamentally “find the same point in the other image,” so depth breaks wherever the point cannot be found:

  • Textureless surfaces: white walls, sky — both images look identical, nothing to match;
  • Repeated texture: blinds, railings, stripes — the matcher locks onto the wrong repetition;
  • Occlusion, reflections, transparency: one side is hidden, or glass produces no stable image;
  • Disparity near zero: far away, d is only a pixel or two, and 1 pixel of error sends the depth flying — a raw disparity map must never go downstream unfiltered; apply threshold filtering and smoothing (median, morphological closing) first.

Product forms: passive and active stereo

  • Passive stereo: natural light only, simple and cheap, but dependent on texture and lighting. A consumer example, the ZED 2i: 0.3–20 m with the default 2.1 mm lens, up to 35 m with a 4 mm lens, at roughly 1% depth error up close.
  • Active stereo: adds IR illumination on top of stereo, fixing the dark and low-texture pain points; the trade-off is that outdoor sunlight drowns the projector and limits range. Examples: the RealSense D400 series, OAK-D series.

Triple and quad cameras: two different things called “multi-lens”

Seeing “triple camera,” don’t jump to stereo vision — it means two different things.

In automotive context, a triple camera is three monoculars with different focal lengths: wide-angle covers the near blind zone, main camera the middle distance, telephoto far-field recognition — the windshield triple found in autonomous-driving sensor suites. They shoot independently and divide the distance range: “multi-focal monocular,” not stereo. Multi-view measurement means Multi-View Stereo (MVS): three or more viewpoints constrain each other geometrically, reducing the mismatches stereo suffers, with bundle adjustment improving accuracy — at rising compute cost. The classic open-source implementation is COLMAP (SfM + MVS), which also serves as the pose-estimation front end for neural reconstruction methods like NeRF and 3D Gaussian Splatting; feeding “a set of overlapping photos” for novel-view synthesis walks this pipeline.

In NVR land there’s a humble form of multi-camera: multiple network cameras are simply “multiple monocular positions,” covering the scene through time sync and complementary angles; tracking a person or vehicle across cameras requires extrinsic calibration per camera plus multi-object tracking — MiBeeNvr-side practice lives in MiBeeNvr + YOLO detection. Also note: multi-camera cooperation only works when cameras are synchronized to the microsecond level, which software timestamps cannot deliver — hardware triggering is required.

Active depth: structured light and ToF

Structured light projects a known coded IR pattern (dot grid, fringes) into the scene; the pattern deforms on the object’s surface, and decoding that deformation yields depth. Each projected point carries its own “identity label,” so no per-pixel matching like stereo. Near-range accuracy reaches millimeters — some flagship phones’ face unlock and offline payment terminals use structured light. Weaknesses: short working range (within meters) and sunlight drowning the projector outdoors.

ToF (Time of Flight) measures the round-trip time of light directly: iToF reads the phase shift of modulated light, dToF times light pulses. Fast response, works at medium-to-far range — common in robot vacuums and tablet autofocus. Weaknesses: unstable on transparent and highly reflective surfaces, plus multipath interference (light bouncing off several surfaces before returning).

Choosing between them comes down to three variables: how accurate, how far, how bad the lighting. Millimeter precision up close (faces, payments) — structured light. Medium-far and fast — ToF. General-purpose on a budget — stereo; add IR and it becomes active stereo for dark scenes.

Hardware: shutters, dynamic range, interfaces, compute

The depth algorithms are covered, but these parameters decide success just as much when picking a camera module.

Shutter. Rolling shutters expose row by row; fast motion or camera motion produces the “jello effect” — verticals lean sideways. Rolling is cheap and performs better in low light, the right choice for static or slow scenes. Global shutters expose every pixel simultaneously, giving consistent timing in dynamic scenes at the cost of higher price and slightly worse sensitivity. Rule of thumb: robots, automotive front view, vibrating platforms — anything where camera or target moves — pick global; fixed cameras watching static scenes, rolling is fine. With controllable lighting, strobed illumination (flash during exposure) lets even a rolling shutter freeze motion.

Dynamic range (WDR). Fixes backlit faces going black against blown-out backgrounds. Face-recognition access control wants 90dB+, automotive 120dB+.

FOV and frame rate. Narrow under 60° for long-range capture, 60–90° for access control, 140–180° for vehicle surround; 30fps for regular monitoring, 60fps+ for motion capture. Day color and night IR don’t mix — an IR-CUT filter switches between them: blocking IR by day for color, keeping it for night vision modules.

Face recognition modules: what they store isn’t your photo

The biggest consumer application of multi-lens cameras today is the stereo liveness face-recognition module inside door locks, access terminals, and attendance machines. The reader recognizes you and unlocks the door — what did it memorize? Intuition says photos. In reality, the module throws the image away the moment you enroll, keeping only a 128- to 512-dimensional numeric vector. The design isn’t corner-cutting — it’s forced from three directions at once: security, storage, and privacy. Once this is clear, the rest of the spec sheet falls into place.

At enrollment, the module feeds the face image into a feature-extraction network, gets back a high-dimensional vector (embedding), and drops the image immediately; all matching afterwards happens on that vector. What’s stored is not a compressed photo, nor an encrypted photo, but a one-way mathematical description extracted from the original facial information — even someone holding the storage contents cannot reverse it back into a face image.

Why must it be irreversible? Because a face and a password are fundamentally different: a leaked password can be changed; a leaked face cannot. Biometrics is a one-shot asset, so the industry runs on three rules:

  • Data minimization: don’t capture if you don’t have to; discard right after feature extraction if you did;
  • Local matching: templates are compared only inside the device; the only output is pass/fail — the system side never sees raw features;
  • Encrypted storage: templates are encrypted before being written to Flash; matching happens in an isolated region.

Trust the official spec page, not secondhand capacity numbers.

One sentence per step:

  • Capture: stereo modules grab visible-light and IR channels simultaneously; mono gets visible light only;
  • Face detection: find the face, output a bounding box and landmarks (eye corners, nose tip);
  • Alignment: affine-transform by landmarks to an upright, scaled 112×112 canonical face, removing pose and distance effects;
  • Liveness check: decide whether the subject is a live person or a photo/screen/mask. Note it sits before feature extraction — a failed liveness check rejects outright, never reaching matching. This ordering is the key design in access-control and payment products;
  • Feature extraction: the aligned face goes through the feature network, becoming a 128–512-dim vector;
  • Matching: 1:1 (are you who you claim) or 1:N (which enrolled person is this) via cosine similarity or Euclidean distance, pass the threshold and the door opens. Where the threshold sits sets the balance between FAR (misidentifying strangers as you) and FRR (rejecting you) — payment-grade strict, access-grade looser; the “instant unlock” experience is traded against security exactly here.

Which detection and feature models to use at each stage, and how they tier by compute, is covered in the motion detection & face recognition post; I won’t repeat it here.

The real dividing line: recognizing a face is easy, recognizing a live person is hard

Four of those six steps — detection, alignment, features, matching — a plain mono RGB camera handles completely: a K210, or a single-board computer with a USB camera plus MobileFaceNet, runs offline 1:1 recognition. Mono can “recognize faces”; there’s no dispute about that.

The problem is liveness. Photos, phones, and tablet replays are all flat 2D images — to a mono lens they look “just as flat” as a real face. This is not a theoretical risk: in the April 2026 comparative test by the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei consumer councils, 3 of 30 smart locks were unlocked by a printed photo and 12 by cloned IC cards; the 2019 round was worse — 32 of 38 samples fell to fake fingerprints. Vendors lowering recognition thresholds for instant-unlock feel, leaving algorithms unable to tell a live face from a flat one, is the direct cause of these incidents.

Mono liveness exists but is limited: silent liveness (texture, moiré, screen-reflection analysis) or challenge-response (“please blink”). They stop cheap attacks, not high-resolution screen replays or 3D masks.

Why stereo is a physical barrier

A stereo liveness module (one visible-light camera, one IR camera, plus IR LED fill) closes the attack paths with two signals:

  1. Depth. The two lenses have physical separation, so the module computes the relief of nose bridge, eye sockets, cheekbones. A real face is 3D; photos and screens are flat — a flat surface has the same depth everywhere, and it’s exposed in milliseconds.
  2. IR skin texture. IR light scattering off real skin carries a specific signature (pores, fine hair); paper and LCD/OLED screens have none. A face played on a phone screen can pass in visible light and immediately fails in the IR channel.
  3. Works in darkness. The IR channel doesn’t depend on ambient light — night and backlight scenes keep working, which a mono visible-light solution cannot offer.

Vendor figures put photo-attack interception near 100% for this class of stereo liveness, and video/mask interception above 99.5%; the FM223’s official rating is no more than 1% on both liveness attack acceptance and false rejection. Treat these as marketing-grade magnitudes — credible at the order-of-magnitude level, but test before buying.

Structured light and ToF take the other active routes: structured light rebuilds a 3D model from tens of thousands of IR dots — the strongest against masks; ToF builds depth from light round-trip time. What they share with stereo IR: dedicated hardware is required. Depth and IR are physically invisible to a plain RGB camera, so any “software-only liveness” is stuck shuffling 2D information — its defense ceiling is low.

Stereo liveness modules: product forms and selection

This class of modules comes in two product forms; sort that out before discussing models:

  • Algorithm-in-a-box: recognition algorithms and a closed protocol live inside the module; the host gets results over serial. Fast to integrate, algorithm not changeable — nearly all door-lock and access vendors use this form.
  • Bare stereo module + SDK: color + IR dual-channel UVC cameras only (roughly 100–300 RMB), with a separate ISP and development kit; detection, liveness, and matching are all on you. Flexible, but you maintain the algorithms.

Integration: serial protocols, frame formats, and code

These modules are slaves on the wire: the host (your MCU or gateway) sends commands, the module executes and replies. The physical layer is highly uniform — UART 115200, 8 data bits, 1 stop bit, no parity (8N1); video goes separately over USB (UVC) to a display or peephole screen. The protocol layer is anything but uniform: the seven models above run three incompatible protocol families:

A. FM22X / AI-xxFM223/225/226/888, AI-10Binary: Sync + MsgID + Size(2B) + DataXOR
B. TX510TX510Binary: Sync + MsgID + Size(4B) + DataAdditive sum
C. FR1002FR1002Plain-text AT commandsNone

Families A and B both start frames with 0xEF 0xAA, but the Size field width and checksum algorithm differ — frames are not interchangeable. Family C is plain text, debuggable with any serial terminal. Breaking each down:

Family A (FM22X / AI-xx): XOR checksum

Frame structure (host commands and module replies share it):

text
1

0xEF 0xAA | MsgID(1B) | Size(2B, little-endian) | Data(N) | XOR(1B)

The checksum byte is the XOR of everything from MsgID through Data. The actual reset frame (no data) reads EF AA 10 00 00 10 — 0x10 ^ 0x00 ^ 0x00 = 0x10, verifiable by hand.

The module sends back two kinds of messages, and parsers must tell them apart: NOTE is the real-time status stream (power-on-ready, and during enrollment the face’s position and pose — the “move left, come closer” UI hints come from here), while REPLY is the final result of a command. Three practical traps, all from vendor FAQs:

  1. Wait for ready after power-on: the module takes about 400ms to initialize and answers nothing before that; when ready it sends a READY-type notification — send commands after receiving it.
  2. Don’t cut power right after photo enrollment: for a photo-enrolled user, the module completes the IR template at the first successful recognition; the manual requires a 5-second delay before power-off, or the template lands incomplete.
  3. Don’t trust secondhand frame formats: a wrong “0xA5 header + CRC16” description has circulated online, contradicting both the official manual and third-party implementations. Capture a real frame with a serial terminal before integrating; the manual is the authority.

Family B (TX510): additive checksum

The cleanest frame of the three; learn this one first:

text
1

0xEF 0xAA | MsgID(1B) | Size(4B, big-endian) | Data(N) | Checksum(1B)

The checksum is the low 8 bits of the sum of all bytes except the sync words. A real enroll frame reads EF AA 13 00 00 00 00 13 (Size=0, sum 0x13), and delete-user-1 reads EF AA 20 00 00 00 02 00 01 23 (Size=2, data 00 01, sum 0x23) — both verifiable by hand. Recognition (0x12), delete user (0x20), and wipe all (0x21) all have known MsgIDs; unknown commands get no reply at all, so when debugging “no response,” check frame format and checksum first.

Reply frames carry a result code: 0x00 success, 0x01 no face detected, 0x03 pose angle too large, 0x06 2D liveness failed, 0x07 3D liveness failed, 0x08 match failed. Splitting “no one there” from “liveness failed” from “no match” makes debugging nicer than family A.

Family C (FR1002): AT commands

Plain-text AT commands, three steps to enroll: AT+REGFACE starts capture and the module returns a binary feature package, AT+WRITEFACE=<uid> writes the features to a user, AT+READFACE=<uid> reads them back (format: user ID + length + feature stream). Features are 128/256-dim vectors, binary, encrypted, irreversible — no raw photos are stored by default. On success the module can assert a switch-level output to drive a relay and open the lock.

Wiring and power: three things that go wrong

  • Level: the module’s UART is 3.3V TTL; add level shifting before connecting a 5V MCU — direct connection is a risk.
  • Power: modules like the FM225 explicitly want no less than 1A; powering from a devboard’s 3.3V/5V pins usually can’t deliver, and unstable voltage causes recognition glitches or reboot loops. FR1002 accepts a wide 5–14V but still wants honest current per the manual.
  • Video and commands are separate roads: commands and recognition results go over UART; the color video stream goes over USB (UVC). Don’t wait for video on the serial port — it isn’t there.

import serial

SYNC = b'\xEF\xAA'

# ---- Family A (FM22X): Sync + MsgID(1) + Size(2, little-endian) + Data + XOR ----
def pack_a(msg_id: int, data: bytes = b'') -> bytes:
    size = len(data)
    frame = SYNC + bytes([msg_id, size & 0xFF, size >> 8]) + data
    xor = 0
    for b in frame[2:]:
        xor ^= b
    return frame + bytes([xor])

# ---- Family B (TX510): Sync + MsgID(1) + Size(4, big-endian) + Data + additive sum ----
def pack_b(msg_id: int, data: bytes = b'') -> bytes:
    body = bytes([msg_id]) + len(data).to_bytes(4, 'big') + data
    return SYNC + body + bytes([sum(body) & 0xFF])

ser = serial.Serial('/dev/ttyUSB0', 115200, timeout=1)
ser.write(pack_a(0x11))                  # Family A: GET STATUS
ser.write(pack_a(0x12, bytes([0, 10])))  # Family A: VERIFY, 10s timeout
ser.write(pack_b(0x20, bytes([0, 1])))   # Family B: delete user 1

# ---- Family C (FR1002): plain-text AT commands ----
ser.write(b'AT+REGFACE\r\n')             # start enrollment, module returns binary features
# write/read features: AT+WRITEFACE=<uid> / AT+READFACE=<uid>

When parsing replies: family A needs NOTE (real-time) separated from REPLY (final); family B collects a full frame by Size and verifies the checksum before trusting it; family C reads line by line, with feature packages read by length. On every family, wait for the ready notification before sending commands.

References and further reading

For the full stereo workflow from calibration to matching, the Stereo section of the OpenCV docs is the standard starting point: docs.opencv.org; weights for the monocular depth models are all on Hugging Face, ready to run. Module-side byte-level protocol formats are not published on the web — requesting the development manual PDF from the supplier or distributor is the only reliable path. Secondhand articles get frames wrong (the “0xA5 + CRC16” case mentioned above is one), so capture real frames against the manual before writing any code.
