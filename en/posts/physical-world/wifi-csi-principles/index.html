From Radio Waves to CSI: The Physics of WiFi Channel Measurement

September 7, 2026 September 7, 2026 Physical World Development WiFi Sensing, CSI, OFDM, Channel Estimation, Signal Processing Signals & Image Processing Series 4219 words 20 min read
🔊

The two WiFi-sensing posts in this series — ESPectre and RuView — both treat CSI as a given: the chip hands it out, the algorithm consumes it, everyone is happy. But where does CSI itself come from? Why does a physical layer designed to move data incidentally measure the whole room? This post digs out the foundation, written for two kinds of readers: if you are new to WiFi sensing, read from the top — every section starts from intuition and closes with the formula; if you have already played with ESPectre, jump straight to the second half — traffic sources, range, and capability boundaries, the shared physical ledger behind all three posts.

Start with a number you look at every day: RSSI

The WiFi bars in your phone’s status bar are one number: RSSI (received signal strength indication), in dBm, usually between -30 and -90 — the closer to zero, the stronger. It records “how much of the signal arrived” — 56 subcarriers and all multipath paths compressed into that one scalar.

For communication that is enough (fast enough? about to drop?). For sensing it is nowhere near enough. A party next door: RSSI tells you “it’s loud over there” and nothing else — when someone walks to the door, how many people are talking, how fast the music is, all erased by that average. CSI keeps exactly what RSSI throws away: one complex number per subcarrier — recording both “how strong” (magnitude) and “how late” (phase).

“Complex number” sounds intimidating; in this post it is just an arrow with a direction — how long is the magnitude, which way is the phase. Every animation below draws that arrow.

Three facts about radio waves in a room

First: the wavelength is the size of furniture. The wavelength is about 12.5 cm at 2.4 GHz and 6 cm at 5 GHz — the same order as most indoor objects (people, chairs, doors, pets). This coincidence decides that WiFi signals indoors neither simply “pass through” nor simply “go around” objects: objects reflect, scatter, and absorb simultaneously, with behavior set by the size-to-wavelength ratio. Humans are strong scatterers at both bands — the body is about 70% water, water’s relative permittivity at 2.4 GHz is near 80, and its reflection loss is low.

Second: there is never just one path. Shout in a valley and you hear several echoes, because copies bounced off different cliff faces arrive at different times — that is multipath, acoustics edition. Radio waves do exactly the same, just faster: light covers 30 cm per nanosecond, so on room scales the path-delay differences are nanoseconds to tens of nanoseconds. Between router and ESP32, part of the signal travels directly, part reflects off walls, part diffracts around the ceiling, and countless fragments scatter. Each path brings back its own arrow (length = strength after attenuation, direction = phase set by path length), and the receiver adds them tip to tail — aligned directions grow the sum, opposed directions shrink it.

Third: motion changes path lengths. Write each path’s contribution as a number: gain β_l times a phase factor set by its delay τ_l. The whole link’s channel is the sum over paths:

text
1

H(f, t) = Σ_l  β_l(t) · e^(−j·2π·f·τ_l(t))

When a person moves, the τ_l of the paths belonging to them move too: lengthen a path by half a wavelength and its phase rotates by π, flipping the superposition from constructive to destructive. Everything that follows — the structure of CSI, the principle of detection, the uses of phase — is a corollary of this sum.

The animation stages facts two and three together: the blue direct vector stands still; the orange body-reflection vector rotates with the person’s position — every half-wavelength of path change rotates it half a turn; the green resultant’s length is the CSI amplitude, tracked by the bar on the right, longest in phase, shortest out of phase. Plug in numbers: a person walking 3 cm changes the two-way reflection path by 6 cm — almost exactly half a wavelength at 2.4 GHz (λ = 12.5 cm) — and the amplitude swings from peak to trough. “A person moves, the signal changes violently” is that one picture, and there is no more math to it.

OFDM: one bad channel becomes 56 good ones

Multipath handed early single-carrier WiFi two hard problems. One is frequency-selective fading: multipath copies interfere differently at different frequencies — this frequency lands constructive, that one destructive — leaving a dozen-MHz band full of hills and pits that the equalizer must fight as a complex high-order filter. The other is intersymbol interference: multipath delivers copies of the same data at different times, and at high single-carrier rates the “echo” of one symbol steps into the next.

The answer since 802.11a (1999) is to slice the band into many narrow subcarriers transmitted in parallel — OFDM:

ConfigurationFFT sizeSubcarriers usedData among them
802.11a/g, 20 MHz645248 (plus 4 pilots)
802.11n, 20 MHz645652
802.11n, 40 MHz128114108
802.11ac, 80 MHz256242234

The subcarrier spacing is fixed at 312.5 kHz — exactly the reciprocal of a 3.2 µs OFDM symbol. That is the entire meaning of “orthogonal”: neighboring subcarriers integrate to zero over one symbol, so an FFT separates them cleanly no matter how closely they sit. The empty FFT slots are not waste: guard subcarriers at both band edges suppress adjacent-channel leakage, the center DC subcarrier stays null because of carrier leakage and LO interference, and 4-8 pilot subcarriers carry known sequences for the receiver to track residual phase drift through the data portion.

One more piece of key engineering: the cyclic prefix. Each symbol copies its last 0.8 µs to the front as a guard interval; as long as multipath copies arrive within the guard, the symbol’s linear convolution acts as circular convolution, and after the FFT the subcarriers remain orthogonal with no inter-carrier interference. Typical indoor RMS delay spread is only tens of nanoseconds — an order of magnitude below 0.8 µs — so the condition holds in daily life.

Then something crucial for sensing happens: on each 312.5 kHz-wide subcarrier, the channel is approximately flat. However gnarly the fading, once sliced narrow enough, each piece is described by one complex number — one complex multiplication. Without OFDM, describing the same channel takes an entire impulse response; with it, one complex number per each of the 56 subcarriers suffices.

The two panels are the same channel from two views: across the whole 20 MHz (top) the response is violently bumpy, with deep fades where several paths land out of phase at that frequency; inside the single 312.5 kHz subcarrier framed by the orange window (bottom) it is nearly a straight line, comfortably described by one complex number. CSI’s 56 values are the slope and height of 56 such “nearly straight lines”.

Channel estimation: all the engineering behind one division

Once the subcarriers are flat, demodulation reduces to one question: how does the receiver learn what complex number the channel applied to each subcarrier? The idea is plain — send a test letter with fixed content and see what the channel did to it. Every WiFi frame’s preamble carries training fields (LTF, Long Training Field): the L-LTF spans 8 µs and contains two 3.2 µs long training symbols whose values are specified subcarrier-by-subcarrier in the 802.11 standard (BPSK ±1 constellation points), memorized by both ends. From 802.11n on, HT-LTFs (one, two, or four) stack per spatial stream; 802.11ac uses VHT-LTFs to the same end.

The receiver does one division per subcarrier after the FFT:

text
1
2
3
4
5

H(k) = Y(k) / X(k)

e.g. X(k) = 1         (standard-defined training symbol)
     Y(k) = 0.2+0.2j  (what actually arrived)
     H(k) = 0.28∠45°  (subcarrier attenuated to 0.28, phase rotated 45°)

Since X is ±1, the implementation does not even divide — multiplying by the conjugate suffices. This is least-squares estimation; single-subcarrier and single-antenna leaves no room for fanciness: unglamorous, but mapping one-to-one onto the physics.

The three panels play the whole story: the blue X is the standard-fixed training symbol, always the same shape; the orange Y is what arrives after the channel — barely wobbling in a quiet room, rippling per subcarrier when a person walks; the green H is Y divided by X, keeping exactly that ripple. Divide out the known content and every remaining change belongs to the channel — CSI is not an extra measurement; it is the channel itself, an intermediate the chip already computes to demodulate every packet and normally throws away.

Stacked up, CSI is a time-frequency grid: one frame yields 56 complex numbers along frequency, roughly 100 frames per second yield the evolution along time, and MIMO multiplies on antenna pairs — the Intel 5300 at 3×3 gives nine groups. One clarification worth making here: extra antennas buy angle resolution and diversity gain, not sensing distance — the classic way to push range out is canceling common noise (FarSense’s ratio method below), not stacking antenna gain. ESPectre consumes the amplitude layer of this grid and RuView mostly the phase layer.

How multipath writes the room into these complex numbers

Frequency axis: coherence bandwidth sets information density

Read the sum H(f) = Σ β_l·e^(−j2πfτ_l) backwards: it is a Fourier series in frequency whose “frequencies” are the path delays τ_l. An inverse FFT of H yields the time-domain impulse response — one peak per family of paths at similar delay, squared into the power delay profile. In other words, the ripple rate of CSI across frequency encodes the spread of path lengths: faster ripple, more spread-out delays.

How similar are neighboring subcarriers’ views? Intuitively: adjacent subcarriers differ by 312.5 kHz — 0.01% of 2.4 GHz — so of course they see the same face. Quantitatively it is the coherence bandwidth, on the order of B_c ≈ 1/(5·σ_τ) with σ_τ the RMS delay spread; indoor σ_τ is typically tens of nanoseconds, putting B_c at a few MHz — in subcarrier counts, a dozen or so adjacent subcarriers all see “the same change of the same channel”.

Same-colored dots bob in sync (their frequencies are close, perturbed by the same stretch of multipath); different-colored groups keep their own rhythms. That is the physics behind the ESPectre post’s “12 spread-out subcarriers”: rather than sampling redundantly inside a coherence band, jump across bands and buy independent observations — detection wants many independent witnesses, not one loud witness.

Time axis: Doppler calibrates motion

When a person moves, τ_l moves and CSI ripples at a rate set by the Doppler shift. For reflection-style sensing, f_d = 2v/λ (two-way): walking at 1 m/s is about 16 Hz at 2.4 GHz and 33 Hz at 5 GHz; breathing at 0.2 Hz and heartbeat at 1.2 Hz sit two orders slower. These numbers set the sampling budget — 100 frames per second leaves better than 3× Nyquist headroom for walking Doppler and far more for vitals. WiFi sensing is never bottlenecked by sampling rate, only by SNR and by how much traffic fills the air.

The same CSI stacks into a time series and splits into two consumption paths: amplitude is sensitive to paths appearing, disappearing, and being occluded — right for “is there large motion”; phase is sensitive to path length — a 1 mm path change rotates phase about 2.9° one-way (5.8° two-way), so ±2 mm of chest movement is a periodic swing of about 11.6° peak-to-peak (the RuView post ran this arithmetic). Amplitude reads “how many paths”, phase reads “how long the paths are” — the essential division of labor between the two layers.

Why phase is dirty by birth

If phase is this useful, why does ESPectre use amplitude only? First, an arithmetic cold shower. Two oscillators each off by 10 ppm in opposite directions make the 2.4 GHz carriers differ by:

text
1
2

2.4 GHz × 20 ppm = 48 kHz of carrier frequency offset (CFO)
phase rotating at 2π × 48000 rad/s → 480 full turns every 10 ms (one frame)

The receiver performs frequency-offset correction before demodulation, but the residual CFO still runs tens to hundreds of hertz — phase keeps turning between frames. And that is only one of four impurity families:

ImpurityPhysical originAppearance in dataStandard fix
Carrier frequency offset CFOTX/RX oscillators disagreeall subcarriers’ phase rotates linearly in timeinter-frame differencing, conjugate multiplication
Sampling frequency offset SFOsampling clock mismatchphase tilts linearly across subcarrier indexlinear fit and de-ramp over index
PLL initial phaserandom at every lockwholesale phase jumps between framesphase reference or differential methods
AGC gain stepsautomatic gain switchingamplitude stepsscale-invariant features, normalization

The first two are systematic and modelable; the last two are random. The academic standard is a combo: unwrap the phase, linearly fit against subcarrier index to remove the SFO component, then pair inter-frame conjugate multiplication or two-antenna ratios to cancel CFO — FarSense (IMWUT 2019) made common noise cancel in the ratio of two antennas’ CSI and pushed breathing monitoring to 8 meters; it is the flagship of this family. ESPectre’s choice is more radical: these impurities barely stain the amplitude layer, so trust amplitude only, design every feature scale-invariant, and leave AGC untouched. Neither route is wrong — one purifies richer-but-dirtier data, the other runs statistics on adequate-but-cleaner data.

Passive and active: where the sensing node gets its traffic

CSI rides on every packet’s preamble — no packets, no measurement. Where do the packets come from? Three playbooks:

PlaybookWho transmitsRepresentative
Purely passiverouter beacons (~1 per 100 ms) and other people’s data framesespargos passive localization
Self-generatedthe sensing node sends small multicast packetsESPectre’s internal mode
Active probingmesh nodes send probe frames in time slotsRuView’s TDM mesh

A passive node puts its WiFi interface into monitor (promiscuous) mode: no association, no joining, no transmitting — it grabs frames already in the air and computes CSI from them. Deploying it changes the room’s RF environment not at all; that is what “zero added radiation” means. Router beacons arrive about every hundred milliseconds — the most stable free signal source there is, so a passive node almost always has something to hear; the price is an uncontrolled packet rate and arrival timing. Self-generated traffic is ESPectre’s choice (internal mode’s hundred small multicast packets per second, covered in the previous post). Active probing is the heaviest and buys a controlled packet rate plus known link geometry — exactly what RuView’s multistatic fusion feeds on. The standards are moving the same way: 802.11bf-2025 writes WLAN Sensing into the protocol, making active sensing a built-in capability — sensing frames at the same power level as ordinary data, well under 1% of airtime.

Four routes to actually get CSI

With the principles done, back to reality: how to get this string of complex numbers. Vendors do not expose CSI by default — it is a demodulation intermediate, computed and discarded — so each of the four routes represents a different way of prying it loose:

RouteRepresentativeWhat you getCost
Official APIESP32 (the esp_wifi_set_csi trio)20/40 MHz, L-LTF/HT-LTF selectable, all chipslow bandwidth ceiling
Research NIC toolIntel 5300 (Linux CSI Tool)30 subcarrier groups (adjacent pairs averaged), 3×3 MIMOa decade-old NIC plus pinned old kernels
Research NIC toolAtheros AR9580 (Atheros CSI Tool)all 56/114 subcarriersbound to specific kernel versions
Firmware patchnexmon_csi (Broadcom)up to 80 MHz, UDP broadcast outputfirmware reverse-engineering, specific old chips only

The first three intercept at the driver layer; the fourth patches the firmware itself: nexmon_csi reverse-engineers Broadcom’s closed firmware, writes C patches that hijack the execution flow into its hooks, and intercepts CSI from the PHY’s channel-estimation table — it runs on a Raspberry Pi 3B+/4B and is the academic standard tool for high-bandwidth CSI. The ESP32 is the only mainstream platform with CSI in its official SDK, which is precisely why ESPectre and RuView chose it: tens of yuan, not a single GPIO needed, CSI flowing out of a callback. One discipline worth learning early: the CSI callback executes in the WiFi driver’s task context, and slow work inside it drags the whole stack down — push the data onto a queue and return immediately; process it in your own task.

How far it can sense: the physics of range

First, a common misconception corrected: WiFi sensing has no single “operating distance”. In the same room, “is anyone moving” reaches a dozen-plus meters while heart rate keeps only under three — range is set by the physical quantity each task chases:

TaskTypical rangeDisplacement being measured
Motion / presence10-15 m open space, ~5 m through a wallwalking, meter-scale
Breathing1-5 m (two-antenna ratio reaches 8 m)chest wall, ~5 mm
Pose estimation3-6 m, multi-nodelimbs, ten-centimeter scale
Heart rateunder 3 mbody-surface vibration, ~0.1 mm

(Typical figures from the literature and product docs; they float with the environment.)

Device count scales with the task the same way: motion detection needs one board; breathing needs one TX-RX pair; 2D localization wants at least three receiving points to triangulate; pose estimation wants 4-6 nodes for multiple viewpoints — exactly RuView’s default mesh size.

Why the spread? Communication distance answers to the link budget — free-space path loss FSPL ≈ 20·log10(d) + 40 dB at 2.4 GHz (d in meters) — but that is only the “can you hear it at all” floor. Sensing range answers to a different ledger: split CSI into a static component (direct path plus fixed reflections) and a dynamic component (the body’s reflection), and sensing range is the distance at which the dynamic component still towers over the noise. The body’s reflection while breathing is already orders of magnitude weaker than while walking (5 mm versus 1 m of displacement), and every extra meter of distance shaves it further — the collapse of range with task granularity is physics, not sloppy engineering.

There is also a resolution ledger: the classical radar range resolution Δr ≈ c/(2B).

text
1
2
3

20 MHz (one channel) → 7.5 m
60-70 MHz (three non-overlapping channels stitched) → ~2 m
4 GHz (mmWave FMCW radar) → 3.75 cm

A 20 MHz channel resolves range only to 7.5 meters — larger than a room — which is why WiFi sensing “feels the motion but cannot say which desk it is behind”, and one reason RuView stitches the three non-overlapping channels together (168 virtual subcarriers). Subspace super-resolution methods (the SpotFi family) can cross this line when SNR is good, at the cost of sensitivity to signal quality. mmWave radar trades 4 GHz of bandwidth for 3.75 cm — two orders beyond WiFi; the two are not competitors, they merely both use radio.

What it can and cannot measure: the physics of boundaries

WiFi sensing detects change, not existence. Every algorithm compares CSI against a baseline: the couch, the cabinet, and the walls contribute constant reflections that get subtracted as background. The design cuts both ways — robust against clutter (furniture being there forever never disturbs the baseline) and blind to perfectly still targets. A normally “still” person holds one last lifeline: breathing, the ~5 mm periodic chest motion that keeps them measurable (reliably within 5 m); a person holding their breath and freezing is, at the signal level, indistinguishable from a chair.

Static objects cannot be drawn. Beyond background subtraction, there is the resolution ledger: 7.5 m of resolution dwarfs any piece of furniture. A large metal object can be flagged as “a strong reflector is over there” — that is all. The frontier is attacking exactly this: UCSB’s Wiffract traces the diffraction signatures WiFi waves leave on sharp edges and demonstrated reading letters through a wall in 2023 — with laboratory-grade dedicated arrays, not something an ESP32 can do.

The size floor is roughly “a cat”. Radar cross section (RCS) shrinks brutally with size: an adult is about 1 m², cats and dogs one-tenth to one-twentieth of that, a sparrow about one-thousandth (-30 dB); anything smaller drowns in noise. A fish in an aquarium suffers a double penalty: 2.4 GHz penetrates only one or two centimeters into water (the same physics as a microwave oven), so the tank is a wall; and a fish is far smaller than the wavelength, deep in the Rayleigh-scattering regime where reflected power scales roughly with the fourth power of size-over-wavelength. WiFi sensing can watch your cat; it cannot see your fish.

Seen end to end: radio waves build multipath in the room, OFDM slices the multipath channel into one complex number per subcarrier, the training-field division computes those numbers, and Doppler and path lengths make them breathe with human motion — CSI is a snapshot of the room refreshed a hundred times per second. Its capability radius is equally computable: it detects change, not stillness; its size floor is a cat; its range collapses from a dozen meters to a few as the task tightens. The next two posts turn this physics into engineering: ESPectre does motion detection on the amplitude layer, and RuView decodes pose and vital signs on the phase layer and multistatic geometry.
