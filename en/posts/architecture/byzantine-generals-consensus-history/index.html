The Ghost of Byzantium: From the Generals' Dilemma to Blockchain Consensus

August 9, 2026 Architecture & Design Distributed Systems, Byzantine Fault Tolerance, BFT, Consensus, Blockchain, Tech History 2425 words 12 min read
🔊

Byzantine fault tolerance (BFT) is one of the most-cited ideas in distributed systems, yet few can explain where the name comes from. It wasn’t a theorem invented one morning — it grew, step by step, out of an aviation fault-tolerance project, a paper nobody read, and a name chosen because it would travel. This essay follows that thread chronologically, from a 1978 flight-control computer to the voting layer of every modern blockchain.

Why three computers aren’t enough

In the 1970s NASA funded SRI (Stanford Research Institute) to build a fault-tolerant computer for aircraft flight control, project codename SIFT. The background was practical: new airliners, to save fuel, had ever-thinner wings and smaller control surfaces, making them aerodynamically unstable — without computer control they would crash. In that design, a single flight-control computer failure meant a crash.

Fault tolerance wasn’t optional. It was a matter of lives.

The conventional approach was straightforward: install three computers and take a majority vote. If one fails, the other two still vote the right answer. A three-machine system tolerates one fault — this was taken as industrial common sense. Until the SRI group proved that common sense wrong: if the failing machine isn’t “dead” but “crazy,” three isn’t enough — you need four.

The “traitor” at SRI

In 1978 SRI ran a project called SIFT (Software Implemented Fault Tolerance) to design a fault-tolerant flight-control computer for NASA. The group included several people who would deeply shape distributed systems: Leslie Lamport (later a Turing Award winner), Robert Shostak, Jack Goldberg, and the project lead John Wensley and L. J. M. Melliar-Smith. (The SIFT paper’s author list is Wensley, Lamport, Goldberg, Weinstock, Green, Levitt, Melliar-Smith, Shostak.)

The problem they hit was harder than “the computer crashed.” In SIFT’s design, multiple processors exchanged sensor data over a shared bus and voted. But Lamport noticed a possibility everyone had missed: what if a processor doesn’t “die” but “goes crazy”?

It doesn’t stop working — it sends different data to different processors, telling A “altitude 3000” and B “altitude 5000.” To the voting logic that processor is still “online,” still “participating,” yet every vote it casts sows division. That’s not a fault. It’s a defection.

Goldberg and the SRI colleagues first realized that this “defection-style fault” would punch through the fault-tolerance logic of a three-machine system. Then Pease, Shostak, and Lamport jointly produced the rigorous proof: three processors cannot tolerate one traitor — you need at least four; generalized, to tolerate f traitors you need at least 3f+1 processors.

In 1980 the three published the result in Journal of the ACM as Reaching Agreement in the Presence of Faults. The paper later won the 2005 Dijkstra Prize. But it had one problem: nobody remembered it.

Three can’t cast one vote

The core result is an impossibility proof: with only three generals, one of them a traitor, the loyal generals can never agree. The reasoning runs like this.

Suppose commander A sends orders to two lieutenants, B and C. A may be a traitor or may be loyal. Case 1: A is loyal, sends “attack” to B and C, but C is a traitor and lies to B that “A told me to retreat.” Case 2: A is a traitor, sends “attack” to B and “retreat” to C; C truthfully relays to B “A told me to retreat.”

The crux: the information B receives is identical in both cases. If B picks “attack” in Case 1, it will also pick “attack” in Case 2 — but C will pick “retreat,” so they disagree; if B picks “retreat,” then in Case 1 it has disobeyed a loyal commander. Every choice fails.

This isn’t a protocol being dumb — it’s indistinguishability in the information-theoretic sense: the traitor manufactures an information hole where the evidence seen by loyal nodes is identical under two utterly different realities, and no deterministic algorithm can break that symmetry.

Generalized: with n generals and f traitors, the problem is solvable if and only if n ≥ 3f+1. Whether messages are signed changes the bar a lot:

Message modelTolerate 1 traitorTolerate 2Tolerate 3
Oral (no signatures)n ≥ 4n ≥ 7n ≥ 10
Signed messagesn ≥ 3n ≥ 5n ≥ 7

The engineering takeaway: the old common sense is overturned — three isn’t enough, you need four.

From Albania to Byzantium

Lamport later admitted, in retrospect, to a kind of “jealousy.” He’d long felt that Dijkstra’s “dining philosophers problem” got far more attention than it deserved simply because it had a fun story — philosophers around a table grabbing forks to eat spaghetti. The lesson he drew: if you want people to pay attention to a problem, give it a story.

There was already a “Chinese generals problem” in distributed computing — two generals communicating via unreliable messengers who might be intercepted. Lamport borrowed the “generals” image but made the problem harder: it’s not the messenger who may be lost, it’s the general himself who may be a traitor. A traitor can send different orders to different generals, lie about what he received, and do everything possible to stop the loyal generals from agreeing.

Now he needed a nationality. Lamport first picked “Albanian generals” — for a practical reason: 1970s Albania was the most isolated country in Europe, nearly cut off from the world, so “it was unlikely any Albanian would read the paper and object.” His colleague Jack Goldberg was clearer-eyed: there are Albanians outside Albania, and Albania won’t be a black hole forever.

Lamport picked a new name: Byzantine. The Byzantine Empire had fallen in 1453, so there were no “Byzantines” to object; and “Byzantine” in English already connoted “complex, convoluted, intrigue-laden.” In 1982 Lamport, Shostak, and Pease published The Byzantine Generals Problem in ACM TOPLAS. The paper’s main purpose — in Lamport’s own words — was to give the problem a new name. “Byzantine” was now nailed into the distributed-systems lexicon for good.

The power of signatures

Lamport contributed an extra weapon to the problem: digital signatures. If the generals’ orders carry unforgeable signatures — traitors can’t forge the commander’s signature or alter a signed order — the problem gets much easier: only 2f+1 processors are needed to tolerate f traitors.

It’s worth noting how Lamport knew about digital signatures at all: he was friends with Whitfield Diffie (co-inventor of the Diffie-Hellman key exchange). By Lamport’s own account, around 1975 in a Berkeley coffee house, Diffie mentioned they were working on an open problem of “digital signatures”; Lamport thought “that doesn’t look hard” and quickly worked out a way to build signatures from a one-way function (published in 1979 as the SRI technical report Constructing Digital Signatures from a One Way Function — the first digital-signature scheme; the popular “scribbled on a napkin” version is later embellishment; Lamport himself only describes a conversation in a coffee house). The scheme was impractical — signing one bit meant publishing about 64 bits of key material — but conceptually complete. He later introduced signatures into the Byzantine problem and proved they drop the threshold from 3f+1 to 2f+1.

FLP: a deeper despair

The 1982 paper gave the problem a name. But in 1985 another paper plunged the field into a deeper despair. Michael Fischer, Nancy Lynch, and Michael Paterson published Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process in Journal of the ACM — later called the FLP impossibility theorem.

They proved: in a fully asynchronous network (no bound on message delay), even with only one crashed node, no deterministic consensus protocol can guarantee termination. This is more lethal than the Byzantine problem. Byzantine assumes “traitors” — actively malicious nodes; FLP targets the milder “crash” — a node simply halts. And FLP only requires asynchrony, not a single traitor. The meaning: if you can’t assume messages will eventually arrive within some known time, then no deterministic algorithm can guarantee the system reaches consensus — you can’t tolerate even one dead node.

FLP split consensus research into two paths: settle for partial synchrony — assume some unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST) after which the network behaves (Raft, PBFT, Tendermint all take this); or embrace randomization so the algorithm terminates “almost surely” (HoneyBadger, Algorand).

PBFT: from theory to engineering

In 1999 MIT’s Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov published Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance at OSDI. The paper’s significance isn’t a theoretical breakthrough — the framework is still 1982’s — but one word: Practical. For the seventeen years before it, Byzantine fault tolerance had stayed theoretical; everyone knew the 3f+1 bound, but nobody knew how to make it fast enough to run in a real system.

Castro and Liskov did three things. First, a three-phase protocol (Pre-Prepare → Prepare → Commit): the primary broadcasts a proposal, replicas exchange Prepare to confirm they all got the same proposal, then exchange Commit to lock the decision. Second, view change: if the primary fails, replicas time out and run view-change to replace it (new primary = view mod n), with strict correctness proofs that swapping primaries doesn’t fork. Third, a real implementation: they built a PBFT prototype that hit about 2,700 ops/s on a four-replica LAN with single-digit-millisecond latency — an order of magnitude faster than the prior generation of Byzantine protocols (like Rampart, tens of milliseconds). That was enough to prove BFT wasn’t just theory.

PBFT set the template for the next twenty years of BFT protocols: three phases, QC (quorum certificate), view-change. Tendermint, HotStuff, IBFT all optimize on this skeleton — fewer communication rounds, linear message complexity, aggregated signatures.

Satoshi’s fork

In 2008 someone (or some group) named Satoshi Nakamoto sent an email with a nine-page paper attached. Bitcoin’s consensus took a completely different road: no known participants, no 3f+1 identity verification, not even eventual synchrony — instead it assumed compute is scarce, so cheating doesn’t pay. PoW’s genius isn’t the cryptography (SHA-256 was off-the-shelf) but the incentive design: it reframes “who is the traitor” as “who is willing to spend the most to be one.” A 51% attack is theoretically possible, but controlling 51% of global Bitcoin hash power costs billions — not a technical barrier but an economic one.

The price is that Bitcoin gives up deterministic finality: once a block is mined it’s only “increasingly likely” to be final, never “certain.” In PBFT a decision is irreversible the moment it’s made; in PoW you can only wait for more blocks to pile on, driving the probability of rollback toward zero. Two philosophies diverge:

DimensionBFT campNakamoto camp
Admissionknown participants, verified identityno admission, anonymous
Basis of tolerance3f+1 votingcompute / stake (economic cost)
Finalitydeterministic, one vote settlesprobabilistic, deeper = safer
Trade-offSafety first (stall over fork)Liveness first (always produce)

Convergence

In the late 2010s the two roads began to cross. On one side, blockchains needed BFT’s deterministic finality — financial settlement can’t tolerate blocks that “might be rolled back”; Tendermint (2014) ported PBFT’s idea into a public chain, using PoS to elect a validator set and then running BFT voting to finalize each block on the spot. On the other, BFT needed blockchains’ throughput: traditional BFT’s O(n²) communication drags at 100+ nodes, so HotStuff (PODC 2019) used threshold signatures to compress each round to O(n) and pipelined the votes of multiple blocks; Aptos’s DiemBFT and Sui’s Mysticeti followed this road, pressing latency from seconds to sub-second. And the digital signatures Lamport devised back then live on, as BLS signatures and threshold signatures, in the voting of every modern BFT chain.

Back to Byzantium

On May 29, 1453, the Ottoman Empire breached Constantinople and the Byzantine Empire fell. 529 years later, the name “Byzantine” that Lamport lent to distributed systems has shown up in tens of thousands of papers, thousands of blockchain projects, and the flight-control redundancy of every modern airliner.

The name endured not because of the empire itself, but because Lamport made a correct judgment in 1982: a good metaphor spreads a truth further than a hundred formulas. Even he probably didn’t imagine that the paper he wrote “to give the problem a name” would end up defining the most important fault model in all of distributed computing.
