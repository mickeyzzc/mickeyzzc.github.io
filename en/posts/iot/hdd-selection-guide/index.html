HDD Selection — The CMR vs SMR Core Distinction, and NAS, Surveillance, and Enterprise Tiers

July 27, 2026 IoT HDD, Mechanical Hard Drive, CMR, SMR, NAS, Hardware Selection Tech & Engineering Selection Series 1609 words 8 min read
🔊

The Cost Advantage of HDDs for High-Capacity Storage

A 1TB NVMe SSD costs around 400 yuan today, with a small price gap against a same-capacity HDD. But above 16TB, HDD per-GB cost is about 1/5 to 1/10 of SSD. Scenarios such as NAS, cold backup, surveillance recording, and video archiving — high-capacity, low-frequency access — remain the most economical choice for HDDs.

The key distinction in HDD selection is CMR vs SMR: choosing wrong causes large drops in NAS sync performance and abnormally long RAID rebuild times. This post covers that distinction first, then moves on to RPM, helium filling, and product tiers.

CMR vs SMR: The Core Distinction in Selection

An HDD reads and writes data via a magnetic head over spinning platters. Conventional CMR (Conventional Magnetic Recording) keeps tracks independent and non-interfering. SMR (Shingled Magnetic Recording) overlaps tracks like roof tiles — higher density, larger per-platter capacity — at the cost of much more complex writes.

When SMR writes a track, it overwrites the data of neighboring tracks. To write, the controller must first read the neighboring track’s data, merge new and old, then rewrite both — one write becomes a three-step read-modify-write. Sequential writes (surveillance recording, file appends) have limited impact, because the controller can batch; but random writes (NAS ZFS, databases, RAID rebuilds) trigger dense read-modify-write operations, dropping performance significantly to single-digit MB/s or even stalling.

The 2020 WD SMR Incident

In April 2020, the community discovered that some WD Red models (EFAX) had silently switched from CMR to SMR, with no marking on the product page. These drives were positioned as NAS-specific, but failed during ZFS rebuilds, freezing entire RAID pools. WD initially claimed “SMR is suitable for NAS workloads,” was later refuted by Arstechnica and ServeTheHome benchmarks, and eventually admitted it and published a CMR/SMR model list.

After this, WD, Seagate, and Toshiba all started marking CMR/SMR on their product pages. But entry-level desktop drives still default to SMR, due to higher per-platter capacity and lower cost. When buying NAS drives, you must confirm CMR.

How to Tell CMR from SMR

ChannelMethod
Official product pageWD / Seagate / Toshiba all have CMR/SMR list PDFs
Model lookupSee the CMR/SMR markings in the tier section below
Price signalNoticeably cheap for the capacity is basically SMR
Real-world testPoor sustained random write performance, jagged HD Tune write curve — that’s SMR

Rule of thumb: Desktop drives at 4TB and below are mostly SMR; NAS, surveillance, and enterprise drives are basically all CMR; above 8TB, almost all are CMR (SMR’s gains diminish at high capacity).

RPM: The Speed vs Noise Trade-off

RPM (revolutions per minute) determines how fast the head sweeps, directly affecting sustained transfer rate and seek time:

RPMSustained R/WSeek timeNoiseTypical use
5400 / 5900 rpm100–150 MB/sSlowerLowNAS, surveillance, archive
7200 rpm150–200 MB/sMediumMediumDesktop, game library
10000 rpm200–250 MB/sFastHighEnterprise, database (rare)

NAS and surveillance drives mostly use 5400/5900 — the speed meets requirements, and noise, power, and heat are low, making them suitable for 24/7 operation. Desktop library drives use 7200 to balance speed and noise. 10000rpm (like WD VelociRaptor) has essentially been replaced by SSDs now.

Helium-Filled vs Air-Filled

Platters spin at high speed inside the chamber; air resistance slows the rotation and generates heat. Helium-filled drives fill the chamber with helium (1/7 the density of air), reducing drag and allowing more platters to stack — pushing single-drive capacity above 10TB:

TypeCapacity rangeCharacteristicsRepresentative products
Air-filled≤ 8TBCheap, fewer plattersDesktop drives, entry NAS
Helium-filled10TB–24TB+More platters, low power, low noiseWD UltraStar, Seagate Exos, enterprise

Above 10TB, almost everything is helium-filled. Helium slowly leaks; helium drives are well-sealed within their design life (5-year warranty), but used helium drives past 5 years require caution — helium leakage increases platter drag, affecting both performance and lifespan.

Interface

InterfaceRateUse case
SATA III6 Gbps (~550 MB/s)Consumer, NAS
SAS (Serial Attached SCSI)12 Gbps or 24 GbpsServers, enterprise

SATA meets consumer needs — HDDs themselves only do 100–250 MB/s, far below the SATA ceiling. SAS is for enterprise servers: hot-swap, dual-port redundancy, higher MTBF, and more than twice the price. SAS is not recommended for home use; motherboards and backplanes typically don’t support it.

Product Tiers

HDDs are tiered by workload — choosing the wrong type accelerates aging:

TierWorkloadRepresentative productsRecording tech
Desktop8×5 intermittentWD Blue, Seagate BarracudaMostly SMR (≤4TB)
NAS24/7 medium loadWD Red Plus, Seagate IronWolf, Toshiba N300CMR
Surveillance24/7 sustained sequential writeWD Purple, Seagate SkyHawkCMR
Enterprise24/7 high loadWD Gold / UltraStar, Seagate Exos, Toshiba MGCMR + helium

Desktop Tier

WD Blue, Seagate Barracuda — suitable for PC library drives. 8×5 intermittent workload; using them for continuous NAS operation accelerates aging. Mostly SMR below 4TB — check the model before buying.

NAS Tier

WD Red Plus (note: Plus; regular Red has SMR models), Seagate IronWolf, Toshiba N300. Designed for 24/7 operation: CMR, anti-vibration (RV sensor, rotational vibration sensor), annual workload 180–300TB. NAS must use this tier.

WD distinction: regular “WD Red” has SMR models (EFAX); “WD Red Plus” is CMR. When buying WD, look for the Plus suffix.

Surveillance Tier

WD Purple, Seagate SkyHawk. Designed for surveillance recording: sustained sequential write optimization, ATA Streaming command support (allows dropping frames to keep the stream flowing), annual workload 180–360TB. Purpose-built for DVRs/NVRs — endurance is significantly higher than desktop drives.

Enterprise Tier

WD Gold and UltraStar (acquired from HGST), Seagate Exos, Toshiba MG series. SAS or SATA, helium-filled, 550–5500 TB/year workload, MTBF 2–2.5 million hours, 5-year warranty. Unit price 30–50% higher, but endurance and reliability are several times those of consumer drives. First choice for cold storage and backup archive.

Scenario Selection

NAS and RAID

Look for NAS tier + CMR + 5400/7200rpm. RAID rebuilds (especially ZFS resilver) are heavy random I/O; SMR drives make rebuilds run for days or fail outright.

WD Red Plus4–12TB5400–7200CMR¥600–2200
Seagate IronWolf4–18TB5900–7200CMR¥550–2800

For NAS with 4+ bays, pick models with RV sensor (rotational vibration compensation) — multi-drive resonance damages heads.

Surveillance Recorder

Choose surveillance tier + 24/7 sustained write.

Desktop drives should not be substituted for surveillance use — consumer drives fail in 1–2 years under 24/7 sustained write; surveillance drives last 3–5 years.

Choose enterprise tier + high capacity + helium. Cold storage access is infrequent, but data reliability requirements are high — enterprise drives have the most stable MTBF and warranty.

Choose 7200rpm + high capacity; SMR is acceptable (intermittent writes don’t trigger read-modify-write).

On a tight budget for a desktop library, used enterprise drives (like pulled Exos) offer better value, but require the ability to spot refurbs (see below).

Used Drives and Refurb Warnings

HDDs are mechanical wear parts; power-on hours directly reflect lifespan. Purchasing used drives (mining drives, pulled enterprise drives) requires checking S.M.A.R.T.:

MetricMeaningHealthy range
Power-On HoursPowered-on hoursLower is better; >30000 hours is risky
Start/Stop CountStart/stop cyclesReflects power cycle frequency
Reallocated Sector CountRemapped sectors>0 means bad sectors, don’t buy
Current Pending SectorPending sectors>0, don’t buy
Uncorrectable Sector CountUncorrectable sectors>0, don’t buy

Tools: CrystalDiskInfo on Windows, smartctl -a /dev/sdX on macOS/Linux.

Spotting Zeroed / Refurbished Drives

Some sellers use professional tools to zero out S.M.A.R.T. data, reflashing drives with tens of thousands of power-on hours as “brand new.” How to spot them:

  • Implausibly low price: 16TB enterprise drive at 800 yuan (new is 2200+) is almost certainly zeroed
  • Wear inconsistent with S.M.A.R.T.: screw scratches, yellowed labels, but S.M.A.R.T. shows 0 power-on hours
  • G-List abnormal growth: professional tools can reveal hidden bad sector records
  • Official warranty lookup: check the serial number on WD/Seagate websites — warranty period mismatch indicates a problem drive

For users unfamiliar with HDDs, stick to JD direct and official flagship stores; do not chase cheap used drives. Only consider used enterprise drives once you have the ability to read S.M.A.R.T. yourself.
