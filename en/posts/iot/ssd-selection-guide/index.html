SSD Selection — Form Factor, Protocol, and NAND, From SATA to PCIe 5.0 NVMe

July 27, 2026 IoT SSD, NVMe, PCIe, Hardware Selection, Flash Memory Tech & Engineering Selection Series 1512 words 8 min read
🔊

The Gap Between Sequential Peaks and Real Workloads

SSD advertised speeds are sequential read/write peaks, but common workloads like system disks and app launches are bottlenecked mainly by 4K random I/O. Upgrading from SATA to PCIe 3.0 NVMe is a qualitative change, but going from PCIe 4.0 to 5.0 produces little perceptible difference for boot times or daily app responsiveness; the benefit shows up mainly in large sequential transfers (VM images, video footage).

Before buying, identify the bottleneck of your workload, then decide whether PCIe 5.0, a dedicated DRAM cache, and similar specs are worth the premium.

Let’s unpack these four.

Form Factor: Slot and Size Constraints

Form factorSizeInterfaceTypical scenario
2.5" SATA100×70×7mmSATA data+powerOlder PCs, NAS bays
M.2 228022×80mmM key (NVMe) or B+M key (SATA/NVMe)Laptops, desktop system disks
M.2 223022×30mmM keySteam Deck, handhelds, mini PCs
M.2 2211022×110mmM keyEnterprise, workstations
U.2 / U.32.5" but different pinsSAS/SATA/NVMeServer hot-swap
EDSFF (E1/E3)Long stripPCIeData centers

The most common consumer form factor is M.2 2280. Before buying, confirm two things:

  1. Whether the motherboard has an M.2 slot, and whether it supports SATA or NVMe (old Z97 boards’ M.2 slots only run SATA).
  2. Whether the M.2 keying is M key or B+M key. B+M key has two notches — better compatibility but only PCIe ×2; M key has one notch — PCIe ×4, the full-speed NVMe form factor.

The 2230 size is now popular thanks to Steam Deck and handhelds, but capacity ceilings are low (usually 512GB–2TB).

Protocol and Bus: Speed Ceiling

Protocol / busTheoretical bandwidthReal sequential readRepresentative drives
SATA III6 Gbps~550 MB/sSamsung 870 EVO, Crucial MX
NVMe / PCIe 3.0 ×4~4 GB/s~3500 MB/sSamsung 970 EVO Plus, WD SN770
NVMe / PCIe 4.0 ×4~8 GB/s~7000 MB/sSamsung 990 PRO, WD SN850X, Zhitai TiPlus 7100
NVMe / PCIe 5.0 ×4~16 GB/s~14000 MB/sZhitai TiPro7000, Crucial T700

Note the gap from SATA to NVMe (550 → 3500) is far larger than the marginal gain from PCIe 3.0 to 5.0 (3500 → 14000). Upgrading from SATA to PCIe 3.0 NVMe is a qualitative change; upgrading from PCIe 4.0 to 5.0 produces little perceptible difference for most workloads — unless it involves heavy sequential large-file reads/writes or video editing.

PCIe 5.0 drives run hot — almost all require a heatsink, and some motherboards even mandate active fans. Mini cases and laptops essentially cannot use PCIe 5.0.

DRAM Cache: Affects Sustained Performance

The SSD’s FTL mapping table (logical address → physical block) needs to live somewhere. Three approaches:

ApproachPrincipleProsConsTypical drives
Dedicated DRAMHas its own DDR cache chipStable performance, long lifeExpensive, takes spaceSamsung 990 PRO, WD SN850X
DRAM-less + HMBBorrows 64MB of host memory for mappingCheap, compactMay slow down under loadWD SN770, Zhitai TiPlus 7100
Dedicated SLC cacheCarves out NAND as pseudo-SLCFast burst writesDrops hard after cache exhaustsMost mid-low drives

Key constraint: HMB (Host Memory Buffer) depends on host memory. PS5 and some older motherboards don’t support HMB — drives on them slow down severely. When selecting a DRAM-less drive for PS5, check the official compatibility list.

High-end drives use hybrid strategies — the Samsung 990 PRO has both dedicated DRAM and SLC cache, combining stability and performance, and is priced accordingly.

NAND and Slowdown

Die types (SLC/MLC/TLC/QLC) and their P/E endurance were covered in the SD/TF card and USB drive posts — same applies to SSDs:

NANDP/E cyclesCurrent mainstream use
SLC~100,000Industrial, enterprise cache
MLC3,000–10,000Exited the consumer market
TLC1,000–3,000Consumer mainstream
QLC100–1,000High-capacity low-cost drives

The SLC cache slowdown mechanism is the main shortcoming of QLC drives. The controller carves out part of the TLC/QLC die as pseudo-SLC cache; writes hit the cache first (high speed), then the controller moves data to the TLC/QLC region in the background. Once the cache fills, direct QLC writes drop from 2000MB/s to 80–160MB/s.

QLC drives (Samsung 870 QVO, Crucial P3 Plus) offer high capacity and low per-GB cost, suitable as warehouse drives and cold storage. They are not recommended as system disks or working drives — frequent random writes drain the SLC cache quickly and trigger sustained slowdown.

Endurance: TBW and DWPD

MetricMeaningTypical value
TBW (Terabytes Written)Total lifetime write volume1TB TLC drive ~600 TBW
DWPD (Drive Writes Per Day)Full-drive writes per day (within warranty)Consumer 0.3–1, enterprise 1–10

A 1TB Samsung 990 PRO is rated 600 TBW; at 100GB/day writes, that works out to about 16 years. Most consumer SSDs are not written to death — they are replaced when the device is upgraded. Therefore, outside of NAS/server 24/7 write scenarios, there is no need to pay a premium for high TBW.

The Maxio MAP1602 (PCIe 4.0 DRAM-less) underpins the cost-effectiveness of Chinese SSDs — the Zhitai TiPlus 7100 uses it to hit 7000MB/s reads at half the price of the Samsung 990 PRO.

Scenario Selection

System Disk

Look for DRAM + TLC + PCIe 4.0. System disk workload is heavy on small-file random writes; DRAM cache keeps FTL mapping stable, and PCIe 4.0 is the current sweet spot.

ProductCapacityProtocolNAND / cacheReference price
Samsung 990 PRO1TBPCIe 4.0TLC + DRAM~¥700
WD Black SN850X1TBPCIe 4.0TLC + DRAM~¥650
Zhitai TiPlus 71001TBPCIe 4.0TLC + HMB~¥450
Samsung 870 EVO1TBSATATLC + DRAM~¥550

If your old PC only has SATA, pick the 870 EVO — don’t force NVMe.

Gaming Disk

Look for PCIe 4.0 + DirectStorage support. DirectStorage lets the GPU read the SSD directly, bypassing the CPU — load times improve noticeably (same tech as PS5 and Xbox Series). At least 2TB.

WD Black SN850X2TBPCIe 4.0Gaming-optimized firmware~¥1200
Samsung 990 PRO2TBPCIe 4.0Top-tier performance~¥1300
Crucial T5002TBPCIe 4.0Value pick~¥900

Laptop

Look for M.2 2280 + low power + single-sided NAND. Laptop space is tight — double-sided drives won’t fit; high-power drives hurt battery life.

ProductCapacityPowerNotesReference price
WD Blue SN5801TBLowDRAM-less, power-efficient~¥400
Zhitai TiPlus 71001TBLowHMB doesn’t need dedicated power~¥450

NAS and Server

Look for enterprise-grade + PLP (Power Loss Protection) + high DWPD. NAS runs 24/7 writes — you must pick enterprise drives; consumer drives die in six months. PLP uses capacitors to flush cached data into NAND on power loss.

ProductCapacityProtocolNotesReference price
Samsung 983 ZET1.92TBSATAEnterprise TLC + PLP~¥2500
WD Red SN7002TBSATANAS-specific~¥1500
Micron 7450 Pro3.84TBPCIe 4.0Enterprise NVMe + PLP~¥6000
Kioxia CD63.84TBPCIe 4.0U.2 enterprise~¥5500

NAS SSDs mostly serve as cache acceleration (read cache or SSD tier); the main storage is still HDDs — see the next post.

PS5 Expansion

Sony requires PCIe 4.0 NVMe + heatsink. Capacity 250GB–8TB; DRAM-less drives may be incompatible (PS5 doesn’t support HMB).

ProductCapacityHeatsink includedReference price
WD Black SN850X1TBOptional with/without~¥700

Zhitai TiPro 70001TBWith heatsink~¥600

PS5 drives must have a heatsink — bare sticks will throttle or trigger thermal protection.

Universal rule: check your motherboard’s supported protocol and slot form factor before buying — don’t end up with a drive you can’t plug in. PCIe 5.0 is the current performance ceiling, but it runs hot and costs a premium; skip it unless you need it.
