SD/TF Card Selection Analysis — Flash Technology, Endurance Ratings, and Speed Classes: Choose by Scenario

July 26, 2026 IoT MicroSD, SD Card, TF Card, Flash Technology, Endurance, Hardware Selection Tech & Engineering Selection Series 2083 words 10 min read
🔊

About This Handbook

This handbook is organized by flash technology and endurance standards, not by brand and model. The underlying dimensions of an SD/TF card — NAND flash type (SLC/MLC/TLC/QLC/pSLC), P/E cycle lifespan, endurance rating convention (hours vs TBW), speed class systems (V/U/A), temperature range — have been stable for years (NAND type tiers since the 2000s, speed class standards since the 2010s), making them a longer-lived reference than specific models.

The same model of TF card may die within two weeks inside a surveillance camera yet run problem-free for six months in an action camera. The difference is not the brand — it is the write workload. Surveillance is 24/7 continuous sequential writes; action cameras do intermittent burst writes; a Raspberry Pi system disk does random small-file writes. Three workloads with completely different demands on the flash.

The workload dictates endurance requirements, endurance requirements dictate the card tier, and the speed class dictates whether the card can meet the scenario’s bandwidth.

NAND Flash Type: The Hard Limit on Lifespan

Every flash memory cell has a hard limit on the number of times it can be erased and rewritten, called a P/E cycle (Programming/Erasing). Beyond that limit it becomes a bad block. The NAND type sets the fundamental difference in P/E lifespan.

NAND TypeBits per CellP/E Cycle LifespanTypical Use
SLC1 bit~100,000 cyclesIndustrial, automotive
MLC2 bit3,000–10,000 cyclesEnterprise, high-end consumer
TLC3 bit1,000–3,000 cyclesMainstream consumer cards, high-endurance cards
QLC4 bit100–1,000 cyclesHigh-capacity budget cards

Sources: Axis security storage white paper; WD surveillance endurance white paper; JEDEC solid-state storage standards.

Most consumer cards use TLC, with a theoretical 1,000–3,000 P/E cycles. But WD’s testing found that budget cards may deliver only 10–200 cycles — lower-grade dies are one factor, but the larger problem is cut-rate controller firmware.

pSLC (Pseudo-SLC)

There is also pSLC (pseudo-SLC): running TLC/MLC as if it were SLC, storing only 1 bit per cell. P/E lifespan jumps from roughly 3,000 cycles to 30,000–40,000 cycles, at the cost of usable capacity shrinking to 1/3 of the original. Industrial cards commonly use this approach.

The Firmware Value of High-Endurance Cards

Tear down a high-endurance card and the TLC dies inside are no different from those in consumer cards. The premium is in the firmware:

  • Wear leveling: distributes writes evenly across all blocks, preventing hotspots from wearing out early
  • ECC error correction: uses stronger correction algorithms to extend the effective lifespan of the dies
  • Power-loss protection: recovers data consistency on unexpected power loss, reducing filesystem corruption
  • Auto read refresh: detects blocks with rising error rates and proactively migrates data to healthy blocks

SanDisk MAX Endurance goes further — it runs TLC in pMLC mode (2 bits per cell instead of 3), trading 1/3 of capacity for roughly 6× the P/E lifespan.

Endurance Ratings: Hours vs TBW

High-endurance cards use two endurance rating conventions with different test conditions — they cannot be directly converted or compared.

Hours Convention (used by Samsung, SanDisk)

How long the card can continuously record at a specified bitrate.

Card TypeCapacityEndurance HoursWarranty
Samsung PRO Endurance256GB140,160 hours (~16 years)5 years
SanDisk MAX Endurance256GB120,000 hours (~13 years)15 years
SanDisk High Endurance128GB~10,000 hours2 years
Kingston High Endurance32–256GB26,900 hours3 years

TBW Convention (used by WD, Kingston, Dahua)

Total write volume over the card’s lifetime.

Card TypeCapacityTBWP/E Cycles
WD Purple SC QD312256GB256 TBW3,000
SanDisk High Endurance128GB~117 TBW~1,000
Kingston High Endurance256GB~157 TBW3,000
Dahua TF-S100128GB240 TBW—

⚠️ Hours and TBW cannot be directly converted. Samsung and SanDisk official datasheets publish only hours, not TBW. “Equivalent TBW” figures calculated by third-party media are not official values. When comparing cards, stick to one convention.

Speed Class: V, U, A — Each Covers a Different Concern

Manufacturers market “read speed 200 MB/s” — but video recording does not care about read speed; it cares about sustained write speed. The SD Association defines three rating systems, each covering a different concern:

RatingWhat It MeasuresTiers and Minimums
V (Video)Sustained video writeV10=10, V30=30, V60=60, V90=90 MB/s
U (UHS Speed)Minimum sustained writeU1=10, U3=30 MB/s
A (Application)Random IOPSA1=R1500/W500, A2=R4000/W2000

Source: SD Association official standards (sdassociation.org).

A 1080p H.265 surveillance stream typically runs only 2Mbps, so Class 10 is enough; for 4K, look at V30. A1/A2 measure random IOPS, which is meaningless for continuous recording — that metric matters for phones running apps and Raspberry Pi system disks.

The A2 Command Queuing Trap

⚠️ The A2 rating depends on the host supporting SD 6.0 Command Queuing. Raspberry Pi 4 and earlier models do not support CQ, so an A2 card may actually perform worse than an A1 on them. Only the Pi 5 onward has an SD interface that supports command queuing, where A2’s value is finally unlocked. For Pi 4 and below, A1 is the pragmatic choice.

Temperature Range

Card TypeOperating TempTypical Scenario
Consumer0°C ~ 70°CIndoor phones, tablets
High Endurance / Surveillance-25°C ~ 85°COutdoor cameras, dashcams
Industrial-40°C ~ 85°CIndustrial logging, automotive, outdoor gateways

A car dashboard in summer sun can exceed 80°C; a consumer card (0–70°C) placed behind the windshield in a dashcam exceeds its operating range and will simply drop offline.

Scenario Chapters

Surveillance and Dashcams

Surveillance is the harshest workload for a microSD card: 24/7 continuous sequential writes with loop-overwrite once full. Consumer cards typically fail within 2–8 weeks.

Four parameters define the selection: endurance (hours or TBW), temperature (-25–85°C for outdoor/automotive), speed (V30 is enough), and capacity.

Capacity estimation formula:

1

Daily storage (GB) = bitrate (Mbps) ÷ 8 × 3600 × 24 ÷ 1024
Resolution / CodecTypical BitrateDaily StorageDays recorded on 256GB
1080p H.2652 Mbps~21 GB~12 days

Surveillance cards should be proactively replaced every 1–2 years — do not wait for failure. Silent corruption means critical footage may already be lost. Hikvision/Dahua OEM cards read S.M.A.R.T. status through their own cameras, and the SanDisk Video/Industrial series expose health registers — check remaining lifespan regularly.

Raspberry Pi and Single-Board Computer System Disks

When a Raspberry Pi uses a microSD card as a system disk, the workload is the exact opposite of surveillance: system logs, package manager caches, temp files, swap — all random small-file writes. The bottleneck is 4K random IOPS, not sequential write bandwidth.

The key to extending system disk lifespan is not buying the most expensive card — it is reducing writes:

Start at 32–64GB: large enough to leave free blocks for write amplification (favorable for wear leveling), not so small that block scarcity accelerates aging. Stick to Samsung EVO+ and SanDisk Extreme; avoid white-label cards.

For heavy workloads, use a USB 3.0 SSD instead of SD — random I/O, endurance, and cost per GB all crush SD.

Action Cameras, Drones, DSLRs

The bottleneck in these scenarios is peak sequential write speed, not endurance. GoPro requires at least V30 + A2, DJI recommends U3/V30 or above, and DSLRs shooting 4K/8K need V60/V90.

A2 is meaningless for cameras — cameras do not run apps on the card, so random IOPS is not the bottleneck. Do not be misled by “read speed 200 MB/s”; the V class (sustained write speed) is what actually matters.

Capacity first — start at 128GB. High-bitrate video consumes space quickly; V90 + large capacity is more practical than chasing endurance.

Phones and Tablets

Phone internal storage is eMMC/UFS; microSD is typically used for expansion. For storing photos and videos, V10/V30 is enough — the more capacity the better.

If you install apps onto the card (Android adopted storage), A2 noticeably improves launch and runtime speed — the A class was designed specifically for “running apps on the card.” Note that some phones have dropped the card slot entirely; confirm the model supports it before buying.

Industrial and Automotive

Industrial logging, automotive, and medical equipment demand the most extreme specs: wide temp (-40–85°C, or automotive-grade -40–105°C), vibration resistance, power-loss protection, and long lifespan. These use industrial-grade pSLC cards.

For automotive certification, look for AEC-Q100. Prices run 5–10× consumer cards, but unattended scenarios have no alternative — one consumer card failure in an industrial environment costs far more than the card itself.

Fake Card Detection: Mandatory Before Buying

Tom’s Hardware used 8 machines and 70 card readers to write 18 PB of data across 200+ cards. Result: genuine brand cards sold by Amazon Direct averaged 3,000–5,000 P/E cycles, while “same-name” cards from AliExpress channels and fake flash averaged only a few hundred — some failed before reaching 100 cycles.

The most common form of fake card is the expanded-capacity card: a 32GB die with modified firmware reporting as 128GB. Once data written exceeds the real capacity, everything is destroyed. Detection methods:

Windows — H2testw:

Download H2testw, insert the card, select the drive letter, click “Write + Verify.” It writes and reads back the full capacity; fake cards report errors at the real capacity boundary.

macOS / Linux — F3:
