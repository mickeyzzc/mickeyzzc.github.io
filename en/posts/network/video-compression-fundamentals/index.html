Why Video Compresses 100×: Redundancy, YUV, I/P/B Frames and GOP

August 24, 2026 August 27, 2026 Network Video Coding, H.264, GOP, Motion Estimation, YUV, PTS, DTS, Streaming Network Development Practice 1882 words 9 min read
🔊

The previous post on browser codec selection kept mentioning H.264, I-frames and GOP without explaining them, and every latency figure in the protocol panorama ultimately hinges on these concepts too. This post fills in the foundation: what actually happens when a stream of pixels turns into a few Mbps. No math derivations — mechanisms, each tied to its engineering consequence (latency, seeking, corruption, storage).

Start with some arithmetic.

How big is uncompressed video

One 1080p (1920×1080), 30fps, 8bit RGB stream:

text
1
2
3
4

pixels per frame  = 1920 × 1080 = 2,073,600
bytes per frame   = 2,073,600 × 3 (RGB) ≈ 5.93 MB
per second        = 5.93 MB × 30 ≈ 178 MB/s
bitrate           ≈ 1.49 Gbps

That is 10.7 GB per minute. 4K (3840×2160) multiplies by another 4: ~42.7 GB/min. Sixteen surveillance cameras recording for a month at these rates is beyond any storage system. In practice 1080p surveillance streams run 2–8 Mbps — a compression ratio on the order of several hundred to one. Compression is not an optimization; it is the precondition for video existing at all.

Four redundancies, four tools

Video data carries four kinds of redundancy, each matched to a compression tool:

SpatialNeighboring pixels within a frame are highly correlatedIntra prediction + transformLarge runs of identical blue in a sky

CodingSymbol probabilities are skewedEntropy coding (CABAC/CAVLC)MV (0,0) far more common than large displacements

Of the four, inter prediction contributes the most — which is where the I/P/B story begins.

YUV: subsample the color first

Video coding works almost exclusively in YUV rather than RGB. Y is luma, U/V are chroma. Two reasons:

  1. The eye is far more sensitive to brightness than color — roughly 120 million rods handle luma while only ~7 million cones handle color. Halving chroma resolution is barely visible.
  2. Historical compatibility: black-and-white TVs just read the Y channel; YUV was designed for the color-TV transition.

RGB→YUV per BT.601 (the consumer-video default):

text
1
2
3

Y  =  0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 B
Cb = -0.169 R - 0.331 G + 0.500 B
Cr =  0.500 R - 0.419 G - 0.081 B

Since chroma is the insensitive part, the U/V planes can be subsampled. The three common formats:

FormatHorizontalVerticalBits/pixel (8bit)Data vs 4:4:4Typical use
4:4:4fullfull24 bit100%Professional production, archiving
4:2:21/2full16 bit67%Broadcast production chains
4:2:01/21/212 bit50%Streaming, surveillance, consumer video

With 4:2:0, the Y plane of 1920×1080 stays full-resolution while U and V are 960×540 each — every four luma pixels share one chroma pair, halving the data outright. Blu-ray, YouTube and camera outputs are essentially all 4:2:0.

One exception worth remembering: screen recording. Slides and code contain fine lines and colored text where chroma subsampling visibly blurs edges. That is why screen recorders use 4:4:4 or dedicated screen-content tools (AV1 SCC, VVC both have them).

I, P, and B frames

The core idea in one sentence: encode the first frame fully, then every later frame only carries its difference from the past. Frames therefore fall into three roles:

FrameFull nameReferencesSizeStandalone decode
IIntra-codednothinglargestyes
PPredictiveearlier decoded I/P frames~1/3–1/2 of Ino
BBi-directionalboth directions~half of Pno

(Size ratios are typical for surveillance/live streams and vary a lot with motion.)

The I frame: intra compression

An I frame is compressed like a still image — intra prediction (guess the current block from already-coded neighbors) removes spatial redundancy, followed by transform, quantization and entropy coding. It depends on nothing, so a decoder can start from any I frame. The price is size.

The P frame: motion estimation + compensation

P frames carry most of the compression gain. The reasoning: 90% of a surveillance picture is identical to the previous frame or merely shifted — so don’t send pixels, send “where it moved from” plus a small correction.

Concretely, motion estimation: split the current frame into blocks; for each block search a window in the reference frame for the best match, usually measured by SAD (sum of absolute differences). Record the displacement as a motion vector (MV); what actually gets encoded is the MV plus the residual (current block − matched block), and the residual is mostly near zero, which is extremely cheap to compress.

Three things to watch in the animation: the search block steps through candidate positions in the reference frame, computing an SAD at each, and finally lands on the object’s previous position (match found, SAD near zero); the MV costs a few bytes; the residual is the only real payload. H.264 refines motion estimation to 1/4-pixel precision (interpolation manufactures “nonexistent” subpixel positions), and motion compensation is the encoder’s most expensive module — search range and algorithm (three-step, diamond search, …) determine encoding speed.

The B frame: bi-directional prediction

A B frame references frames on both sides, taking a weighted average of the two matches as its prediction. More reference information, best compression. But the cost lands directly on latency: to decode a B frame you need the P frame that comes after it — which splits decode order from display order. More on that below.

Real-time systems (conferencing, camera push, co-streaming) usually disable B frames outright: the bandwidth saved is not worth the buffering delay and CPU cost.

IDR and GOP

First, a common confusion: an I frame ≠ a random access point.

PropertyIDR framePlain I frame
DefinitionInstantaneous Decoding Refresh; starts a new sequenceIntra-coded, not necessarily a sequence start
Reference bufferDecoder flushes it immediatelyNot flushed
Later framesMust not reference anything before the IDRMay still reference frames before this I
Seek / channel changePlayers prefer landing hereNo guarantee playback can start here

If a plain I frame is followed by frames still referencing content before it, starting there produces corruption. The IDR cuts history cleanly — which is exactly why “keyframe requests” fix a corrupted stream (on packet loss, the NVR/player asks the source for an IDR and restarts).

A GOP (Group of Pictures) is everything from one IDR up to the next — the basic access unit of encoding and decoding:

The animation first shows the reference structure: P frames hang on the forward chain from the previous I/P, and each B frame references both neighbors. Then the seek demo: a viewer landing on a B frame cannot decode it and must rewind to the GOP’s IDR and decode forward — GOP length directly bounds seek response and loss-recovery speed.

ParameterShort GOP (e.g. 15–30)Long GOP (e.g. 250)
CompressionLower (more I frames)Higher (bits saved go to P/B quality)
Seek responseFastSlow (may rewind seconds)
Packet-loss resilienceStrong (short error propagation)Weak (a corrupted P pollutes until the next IDR)
Typical useLive, real-time surveillanceVOD, distribution

Worked example: GOP=250 at 25fps puts IDR intervals at 250/25 = 10 seconds — a common VOD transcoding default. A surveillance recorder’s typical “IDR interval 5s” setting is the same storage-vs-seek-speed trade-off.

GOPs are also open or closed: a ClosedGOP is self-contained with no cross-GOP references; an OpenGOP lets boundary B frames reference the previous GOP for extra compression at the cost of dropped frames on mid-join. HLS/DASH bitrate switching requires ClosedGOP (a new rendition’s segment must decode standalone). H.265 formalizes this as CRA/RASL syntax — post three animates it.

PTS and DTS: two timelines

B frames trigger one bitstream-level consequence: the order frames appear in the stream (decode order, DTS) no longer matches the playback order (presentation order, PTS).

For display order I B B P, the P frame references the two B frames and must decode first, so it sits ahead of them in the stream:

FrameDisplay (PTS)Stream/decode (DTS)
I00
P31
B12
B23

In the animation the P frame decodes into the buffer first but waits until both B frames have displayed. The decoder keeps a reorder buffer for this. Three engineering consequences:

  • Frame-by-frame consumers (e.g. an NVR snapshotting frames) must pull by PTS, not arrival order, or the picture “jumps”.
  • A/V sync aligns on PTS; dropping or miscomputing timestamps during remuxing is the classic cause of lip-sync drift.
  • B frame count equals added decode-to-display delay. With B frames disabled, DTS equals PTS and the pipeline gets a segment simpler.

ffprobe shows both timelines (pkt_dts is decode order, pts_time display order):

bash
1
2
3

ffprobe -v error -select_streams v:0 \
  -show_entries frame=pict_type,pts_time,pkt_dts_time \
  -of csv input.mp4 | head -8

The pict_type column reveals the actual I/P/B layout — try it on any MP4 and the B frames’ pts/dts offset is immediately visible.

That covers the frame-level vocabulary: why compression works (redundancy), in what space (YUV), how frames are organized (I/P/B, GOP), and how timestamps are laid out (PTS/DTS).
