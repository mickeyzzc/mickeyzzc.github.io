Third-Party Free P2P Nodes: Public STUN, Free TURN Quotas, and Community Relays

October 19, 2026 October 20, 2026 Network P2P, NAT Traversal, STUN, TURN, ICE, Relay Server Network Development Practice 3149 words 15 min read
🔊

When it comes time to provision NAT traversal for a P2P application, “just use someone else’s free nodes” is the most commonly suggested route. In practice, most third-party P2P networks cannot be connected to at all: node discovery, authentication, traversal coordination, and the encrypted handshake are each governed by the network designer’s own private protocol. Without implementing that protocol stack, your first packet never goes out. Only three categories of nodes are open to any application and free of charge: public STUN, free TURN quotas, and community relay networks.

This post walks through all three: what each one does, how reliable it actually is, and who pays for the “free”.

Which “third-party nodes” you cannot connect to at all

A P2P network is not a public utility you can plug into; it is a complete proprietary protocol stack. Vendor P2P SDKs (TUTK Kalay and its kind — baked into devices at the factory, with proprietary cloud protocols) are the productized extreme of this pattern; the earlier post “Comprehensive Survey: NVR P2P Remote Access Technologies” already covers them in depth. WebRTC cloud platforms are the SaaS flavor of the same lock-in: their network is open to you only if your signaling and media both go through their SDK. Open-source P2P networks (libp2p, iroh) publish both the protocol specs and the code, but “joining” means implementing the whole protocol stack — not pasting an address into a config file.

The only interfaces universal to every application are the three RFC-standardized ones: STUN (RFC 8489) for address discovery, TURN (RFC 8656) for relaying, and ICE (RFC 8445) to orchestrate both. Any client that supports ICE can consume these three kinds of public nodes directly — that is the technical premise on which “third-party free nodes” exists at all.

In one sentence: the only thing you can freeload unconditionally is STUN, and STUN carries no media; relays that do carry media are never free without conditions — quota, throttling, or protocol lock-in, at least one of the three.

Public STUN: the closest thing to unconditional free

Who provides it

The best-known is Google’s stun.l.google.com:19302 (with stun1 through stun4 on the same port). It is the default in Chrome’s official WebRTC samples — the de facto industry default. The service has been running since at least 2013, but Google has never attached an SLA or official documentation to it: a best-effort free service that can be withdrawn without notice. Addresses are handed out through DNS rotation, and the IPs do change (one round of changes landed in September 2021, instantly breaking a batch of projects that had hard-coded the IPs) — always reference the hostname.

Twilio’s global.stun.twilio.com:3478 is another common choice: the STUN side is free and unmetered, and only TURN (Network Traversal Service, billed per GB) costs money.

On the community side, the de facto standard list today is always-online-stun: it re-verifies every entry hourly, and each record must answer an RFC 5389 Binding request correctly to stay on the list. The older gist lists (mondain’s, zziuni’s) have both conceded to it. The very fact that a list needs hourly re-verification is the best commentary on the category: public STUN servers do disappear, and not just a few.

Nextcloud’s stun.nextcloud.com:443 is a useful middle case: still running as the factory default for Nextcloud Talk, but Nextcloud’s own AIO deployment deletes it in favor of its own TURN, and the community reports intermittent timeouts. It works — it is not infrastructure.

Why STUN can be free and TURN cannot

A STUN Binding transaction is comically small: the request is just a 20-byte header, the response adds the XOR-MAPPED-ADDRESS attribute for roughly 32 bytes total, and a session needs a handful of packets at most. The operator’s real cost is log storage, not bandwidth. TURN is the opposite: it carries the entire media stream — 1 Mbps of video is about 450 MB per hour, per direction, and a relayed call is settled by the gigabyte.

Six orders of magnitude separate the two, and the business models diverge accordingly: STUN can be a public good; TURN can only be a business or a subsidy.

Reliability and limits

  • No availability guarantee. The big-provider nodes behave like infrastructure, but there is no SLA to invoke; single-server entries on community lists come and go — which is exactly why always-online-stun re-verifies hourly.
  • Open STUN is a reflection-amplification vector. Binding requests with spoofed source addresses can be abused for DDoS reflection (roughly 4x amplification on IPv4, ~6x on IPv6); NETSCOUT has documented rising STUN reflection attacks, and Shadowserver notifies operators of exposed STUN services. Rate-limiting and tightening by operators is a real response.
  • Restricted networks block UDP 3478. Corporate firewalls routinely drop UDP 3478; commercial TURN offers TLS-on-443 variants to survive exactly this, and free public STUN offers no equivalent guarantee.
  • Privacy is part of the protocol. STUN works precisely by telling you the address it observed for you; the operator necessarily sees your public mapping and request timing. That is not misbehavior — it is how the protocol functions.

How much of the problem STUN actually covers

Measured NAT behavior data (Richter et al., IMC 2016): fewer than 2% of residential router NATs are symmetric; but cellular CGNAT is bimodal — about 40% symmetric — and over 90% of cellular autonomous systems deploy CGNAT. STUN-only hole punching between two home broadband lines mostly works; against a peer on cellular it mostly fails.

Some quotable magnitudes: UDP hole punching works for roughly 82% of NAT combinations (Ford/Srisuresh/Kegel, USENIX 2005 — the basis of RFC 5128). Production measurements: callstats found 22% of conferences needed TURN relaying, and another P2P-call study put the relayed share at 17.7% (both are 2016-2017 data; the order of magnitude is still cited today, but treat them as magnitudes, not precise figures). For UPnP/NAT-PMP, which can fetch an external mapping directly, the Netalyzr dataset shows availability in about 40% of non-cellular sessions — the reason UPnP sits ahead of STUN in the traversal ladder is data-backed, not folklore.

One often-missed engineering number: about 74% of NATs reclaim an idle UDP mapping within 60 seconds (median ~65 s). Keep keepalive intervals below that line, or the mapping dies first.

Free TURN quotas: real relay, quota as funnel

TURN is a real relay, and it is the only public facility that can pull a device behind a symmetric NAT into a session. The business logic is simple: the quota gets your demo running; growth converts to paid plans.

The 500 MB at Xirsys and Metered is development-and-debugging territory. Free quotas also shrink — Metered once advertised 20-50 GB/month free; its pricing page now says 500 MB.

Interoperability: the line between standard TURN and SDK lock-in

Everything in the table above speaks standard TURN with short-lived credentials — Twilio’s REST credentials are an HMAC-signed username/password pair, and Cloudflare’s credential endpoint follows the shape of the turn-rest draft — so generic stacks like coturn, libwebrtc, and pion consume them directly. Buying quota is not buying their SDK. That separates them from “cloud RTC platforms” that bundle signaling, SFU, and TURN into one package: the former can be used purely as a relay, the latter means migrating your whole stack or not using it at all.

The reliability ledger

In production, quota-based free relay does not degrade — it cuts off: quota exhausted, trial expired, card charge failed, and the relay stops on the spot, stranding every session that depended on it. Balance monitoring around month boundaries and renewal dates belongs in routine operations. There is also an earlier signal worth watching: the ICE connection-failure rate — a public STUN server going dark or a TURN tier throttling shows up there first, well before users complain. Regional pricing matters too: Twilio charges USD 0.40/GB in the US/EU, USD 0.60/GB in Singapore, USD 0.80/GB in Sydney and São Paulo — the same app can carry twice the bill in a different region. Cloudflare bills egress only, hands out IPv4-only relay addresses, and applies backpressure packet drops past 50-100 Mbps per client — details spelled out in its FAQ, worth a pass before committing.

Community relay networks: someone else pays the bandwidth

Relays with no bill at all do exist — always as a combination of protocol lock-in and subsidy:

  • Syncthing relay pool: a volunteer runs one strelaysrv (Syncthing’s official relay-server program) and automatically joins the public pool; clients pick relays automatically. Traffic is end-to-end encrypted, so relays see only ciphertext; rate limiting is off by default (global/per-session rates default to 0). The pool’s port distribution is telling: fetching the public directory (https://relays.syncthing.net/endpoint) returns roughly a hundred nodes, the vast majority listening on Syncthing’s default port 22067, with only about one in ten on 443 — and restricted networks typically allow only 80/443, so the genuinely usable relays are the minority. The pool speaks only the Syncthing protocol.
  • Tor Snowflake: volunteers lend a browser tab as a WebRTC ephemeral proxy — low bandwidth, gone without notice; the design goal is bootstrapping access, not stable transport.
  • Yggdrasil public peers: a community-maintained list on GitHub whose README says outright, “not all peers are guaranteed”.
  • iroh’s n0 public relays: run by the project itself, free, rate-limited, no SLA, and speaking only the iroh protocol. The “iroh Deep Dive” post covers the deployment shape.
  • Tailscale DERP: the official free fallback relay when direct connection fails, self-hostable, carrying tailnet traffic only.

Three shared traits:

  1. Protocol lock-in. None of them is a generic TURN — only clients speaking the house protocol enter the pool. To use one, your application must first become part of that ecosystem.
  2. The bandwidth belongs to someone else. Volunteer or project subsidy means a resource you cannot put under contract: no SLA, and peak-hour congestion or nodes going offline is the norm, not an outage.
  3. Survival requires that relays see no plaintext. Syncthing, iroh, and DERP all apply end-to-end encryption; relays carry ciphertext only. Volunteers are willing to run them and users willing to use them on exactly this condition.

Wiring it in: one ICE list is all it takes

For a standards-compliant ICE client (WebRTC, pion, even a coturn probe), there is exactly one way to consume these nodes: the iceServers list. Put STUN and TURN in the same list and do not worry about ordering — ICE tries candidates by its own priority (host → srflx → relay) and falls back to the relay only when direct connection fails:

    { urls: [
        "stun:stun.l.google.com:19302",

    ]},
    { urls: "turn:global.relay.metered.ca:80",
      username: "<short-lived username>", credential: "<short-lived credential>" },
    { urls: "turns:global.relay.metered.ca:443",  // TLS on 443 for restricted networks
      username: "<short-lived username>", credential: "<short-lived credential>" },
  ],
});

Two frequent mistakes:

  1. Configuring STUN but no TURN. Behind a symmetric NAT the STUN-learned candidate is guaranteed to be useless; without a working TURN in the list, the connection simply fails — the most common cause of “I configured the free nodes and it still doesn’t connect” (NAT behavior and failure surface in the data above).
  2. Hard-coding a long-lived TURN username/password. Public TURN servers get scanned, and a leaked static credential turns your quota into someone else’s relay. The correct approach is TURN’s REST credential mechanism (RFC 7635): your backend signs a temporary username/password valid for minutes to hours with a shared secret, and the server verifies the timestamp with an HMAC. Twilio’s REST credentials and Cloudflare’s credential endpoint both follow this pattern.

What “free” really costs: whoever pays the bandwidth sets the rules

The figure shows the traffic ledger of one session: candidate exchange over signaling is kilobytes, a STUN reflection is hundreds of bytes, and once direct connection succeeds, media never touches a third party. Only when traversal fails does TURN carry the stream by the gigabyte. Put all tiers in one table and the cost structure is plain:

NodePer-session magnitudeWhere the free comes from
SignalingKBCheap enough for anyone to self-host (eight takeaways in the earlier post)
STUNHundreds of bytesNear-zero cost; sustainable as a public good

Sustained bandwidth is a sustained cash flow. An “unlimited, unconditional, free” third-party relay cannot exist under this economics — every free supply chain carries at least one of: quota, throttling, protocol lock-in. STUN is the sole exception, because its cost is near zero.

Treat third-party nodes as untrusted components

Once you use someone else’s nodes, the security model assumes the operator sees and logs everything it can see:

  • STUN: your public mapping and request timing land in the operator’s logs — that is the protocol working as designed. For single-box STUN outside the big providers, treat it as a recorder, not neutral infrastructure.
  • TURN: use short-lived credentials — Twilio’s REST credentials default to 24 hours, Cloudflare’s cap at 48. A leaked static long-term credential turns your quota into someone else’s traffic. And the endpoint that issues credentials needs its own authentication, or anyone can relay through your quota — the most common failure of quota-based setups.
  • The media itself: DTLS-SRTP establishes encryption between the two endpoints, so the relay sees ciphertext only. This is the same trust condition the community relays live by: distrust the relay, put encryption in place, and it is just a porter.
