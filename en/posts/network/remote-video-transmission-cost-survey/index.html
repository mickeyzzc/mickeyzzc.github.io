The Resource Bill of Remote Video Transmission: Bitrate, Latency, Loss, and Three Amplifiers

November 1, 2026 November 2, 2026 Network Video Transmission, Bandwidth, Latency, Cost, Survey, NVR, FPV, Satellite Network Development Practice 2659 words 13 min read
🔊

The previous post, rescuing picture quality on a weak network, asked what receiving-side compute can salvage after the network wrecks the picture. This one steps back a stage and asks about transmission itself: what does it cost in bandwidth, compute, power, storage, and money to move a video from A to B. Protocol mechanics stay out of scope; where the groundwork matters, I point to the earlier streaming protocols survey and video compression fundamentals in this series.

Put the official specs, vendor documents, and third-party measurements side by side, and two facts deserve to land first:

Remote video is not one scenario but a family spanning six orders of magnitude of latency. Analog image links run at millisecond level; traditional HLS delivery takes 15–30 seconds — both would call themselves “real-time video.” Before comparing costs, pin down which order of magnitude you need; the bills between two “real-time” claims are not comparable.

Every scenario has an amplifier, and whose bill explodes depends on who carries it. The same 4 Mbps stream aggregates into a 16-channel uplink in surveillance, becomes a 99-stream downlink through an SFU in conferencing, and fans out to ten thousand viewers through a CDN in live streaming — utterly different magnitudes.

Three Variables and Three Amplifiers

Every remote video scheme balances three variables:

VariableMeaningWhat it decides
BitrateSteady-state / peak bandwidth per streamBandwidth bills and storage volume
Latency budgetAcceptable end-to-end delay“Live control” vs. “after-the-fact review”
Loss toleranceHow much loss is acceptable, and what covers itTCP retransmission vs. UDP+FEC/NACK

Bitrate and latency naturally fight each other: cutting latency means cutting buffering, GOP length, and retransmission opportunities, which raises bitrate at the same quality. Loss tolerance is the third corner — FEC alone eats 5–30% redundant bandwidth.

The amplifier decides where the “save” lever sits. Three topologies, three different amplification points:

One stream in, three topologies amplify it in entirely different directions: in surveillance each camera pushes its own uplink and N channels aggregate into N× uplink bandwidth at the access side; in conferencing each participant sends one stream but receives N−1 from the server, amplifying the downlink; in live streaming the host sends one stream and the CDN copies it once per viewer, paid as traffic fees. Every cost calculation below comes from these three amplifiers.

These are end-to-end experience latencies (encoding + network + decoding + necessary buffering), not single-hop figures. The transport-protocol rows align with the matrix in the streaming protocols survey (HLS ~20 s, LL-HLS ~2–3 s, RTMP ingest ~3 s is that post’s unified baseline); the image-link, conferencing, and satellite rows come from each scenario’s official specs. Each order of magnitude corresponds to a different business: milliseconds sell control, seconds sell reach — don’t cross-compare quotes from different magnitudes.

Screen Content and Camera Footage Are Two Different Things

Remote desktop and cloud gaming are both “remote,” but what they carry is fundamentally different. Screen content (UI, text, documents) compresses to very low bitrate with SCC (Screen Content Coding — the H.264/HEVC extension built on intra block copy and palette modes); camera footage and game renders have no such structure and must ride natural video coding, an order of magnitude hungrier.

ScenarioCodingTypical bitrateLatencySource

Two numbers worth keeping. In Microsoft’s bandwidth table, AVC444 mode drops web browsing from 6.5 Mbps (default) to 0.9 Mbps — a real video codec meeting screen content pays off immediately. Tencent’s self-developed screen codec TSE (built on HEVC-SCC block copy and palette) is officially quoted at roughly 55% compression improvement over x265. One widespread claim needs correcting: “SCC saves 10–100× over ordinary coding” has no fair source — it’s a hundredfold against uncompressed raw frames, but a few-fold against natural H.264/H.265 coding. Ratios against different baselines must not be mixed.

Watch the direction too: cloud gaming encodes in the cloud and you only receive (GFN officially recommends 25 Mbps downlink for 1080p); but when “streaming your own machine out” (Parsec/Moonlight/PS Remote Play), the host’s uplink is the ceiling — PS5 remote play officially requires 15 Mbps uplink. A gigabit home plan fixes your downlink, not your stream quality.

Surveillance: The Amplifier Is Aggregation, the Bill Is Storage

Surveillance is familiar territory in this series — for protocol roles see the surveillance protocol landscape; here we only count the bill.

Smart coding is the one big lever on this side: Hikvision’s H.265+ is officially quoted at an average bitrate of 32.4% of standard H.265, and the 2026 Guanlan codec (AI perceptual coding) officially saves 30–50% storage. Dahua has never published a figure for Smart H.265+. These proprietary codecs only work inside their own ecosystems — generality traded for compression, degrading to the standard tier on cross-vendor playback.

The industry’s back-of-envelope for storage: 1 Mbps of constant bitrate is about 10.5 GB per day. Sixteen 1080p H.265 channels (4 Mbps) kept 30 days is 4 × 10.5 × 16 × 30 ≈ 20.3 TB, with a 64 Mbps constant uplink at the access side. Where that storage lands makes an order-of-magnitude difference:

ItemEdge NVRCloud storage

Cloud costs about 31× edge. That money is only worth spending when centralized management and tamper resistance are hard requirements; for home setups with bandwidth and power to spare, edge storage has no competition.

Image Links: Power Is the Hard Constraint

FPV transmission is the most demanding on both latency and power: the picture is used for live control, and the battery is on the aircraft. The competitive dimension here is milliseconds and watts, not bitrate.

The numbers are cross-checked against official specs and public tests: O4 Pro’s 60 Mbps, 15 ms, and 15 km come from DJI’s spec page; the 6–12 W power figures come from Oscar Liang’s measurements (Lite draws ~6 W at 700 mW transmit; ~12 W peak with 4K recording). An earlier draft of this research claimed “HDZero latency 4 ms” — public measurements cluster at 10–30 ms and no source for 4 ms surfaced, so it’s gone.

Analog’s “zero latency” deserves unpacking: the FM link has no codec, so transmission delay is only the milliseconds of RF propagation; glass-to-glass with camera and goggles usually lands around ten milliseconds — still the lowest in the table. The cost is the degradation mode: SNR falls with distance and the picture goes from snow to tearing to black — gradual degradation, while digital links break hard after block artifacts. For control, snow beats a black screen, which is why analog refuses to die. One clarification: ELRS is a control link, not an image link — “seeing” and “controlling” are physically separated; don’t merge them into one budget.

Conferencing and Streaming: The Amplifier Decides Whose Bill

Both are “1 up, N down,” but the amplifiers differ in price by an order of magnitude.

On the conferencing side, topology is the bill:

TopologyPer-stream bitrateLatencyServer compute
P2P mesh1–2.5 Mbps/streamLowestClient self-destruct: (N−1) uplinks
SFU1–3 Mbps/stream150–300 msLow (forward only)
MCU1–2.5 Mbps compositeHigherHigh (full transcoding)
Zoom 1080p 1:1~3.8 Mbps150–400 msLow

SFU downlink amplification is the core of conferencing cost. The extreme case — 100 people all with camera and mic on: each subscribes to 99 streams; 22 workdays × 1 h/day is 100 × 99 × 60 × 22 ≈ 13 thousand kilo-minutes. At TRTC’s 720p tier of ¥28/kilo-minute, the fully-meshed subscription ceiling is about ¥366k/month. Real products crush this with “forward only visible/active streams + Simulcast”: subscribing to 16 active streams is 100 × 16 × 60 × 22 ≈ 2,112 kilo-minutes — about ¥59k/month at 720p, or about ¥30k/month on a 360p low layer. Any selection process must separate the theoretical amplifier from the product’s actual throttling; the vendor’s price list won’t do this step for you.

On the streaming side, CDN is the norm:

StageLatencyNotes
RTMP/SRT contribution uplink2–5 s / 0.5–1 sSRT leans on ARQ retransmission, steadier on weak networks
LL-HLS / LL-DASH delivery2–5 sFully cached on CDN
Traditional HLS15–30 sCheap, no practical ceiling on scale
WebRTC interactive layer<1 sExpensive; intercom seats only

The bill for a 1-hour 1080p (4 Mbps) stream to 10,000 viewers: over CDN traffic, 10,000 × 4 Mbps × 3600 s ÷ 8 = 18 TB, at the mainstream domestic ¥0.24/GB ≈ ¥4,320; the same viewership over SFU subscriptions, at TRTC’s official 1080p rate of ¥63/kilo-minute (billed per viewer’s subscription time), 600 kilo-minutes = ¥37,800 — about 9× more, buying sub-second latency. No suspense in the conclusion: viewing goes over CDN, WebRTC stays for interaction. Production architectures usually take SRT low-latency contribution into the origin and serve viewers through LL-HLS on CDN, keeping WebRTC only for the co-broadcasting seats.

When There Is No Terrestrial Network

Emergency, offshore, disaster zones — the per-GB price ordering is: satellite ≫ bonded cellular ≫ ordinary broadband.

TechnologyUplinkLatencyPowerCost
Bonded cellular backpack20–100 Mbps0.5–1.5 sBattery ~4.5 hHardware in the tens of thousands of ¥; ≈2.25 GB/h at 5 Mbps
Starlink Mini (LEO)10–40 Mbps25–50 msTerminal 20–40 W$50–165/mo + Ocean Mode $6/GB
Traditional GEO~3 Mbps500–600 msTens of W$70–200/mo
Iridium Certus (LEO narrowband)22 Kbps up / 88 Kbps downBelow GEO5–10 WMetered, expensive

Bonded cellular (LiveU/TVU-style multi-SIM backpacks) is the best all-around answer where terrestrial cellular exists: multi-carrier redundancy buys strong fault tolerance at ¥1–3 per GB. Starlink is the answer when there is no terrestrial network, but ocean data just took a price hike: Ocean Mode went from $2/GB to $6/GB effective September 27, 2026 (coverage); the $2/GB in the first research draft is stale. GEO’s 500–600 ms latency is set by orbital altitude — no terminal fixes it; Iridium Certus’s Kbps-class bandwidth carries telemetry and stills, not continuous video. The iron rule: use the ground when you can.

ScenarioKey constraintsBasis

One popular figure in tele-surgery coverage needs cleaning up: URLLC’s design targets are a 1 ms air interface and 99.999% reliability (BLER 10⁻⁵); “BLER 10⁻⁹” is not a 3GPP figure. The sensible human–machine loop budget is 100–200 ms, with well-built private networks reaching under 20 ms; claims of “5 ms” either refer to one air-interface segment or are simply wrong. AR/VR’s 20 ms is an experience red line — past it the problem isn’t “a bit laggy,” it’s physiological motion sickness, which is why cloud rendering must sink to edge nodes.

Where does the amplifier sit?
├─ Surveillance aggregation → edge NVR + smart coding; keep traffic inside the LAN
├─ Conferencing downlink (N−1)× → beware fully-meshed subscription bills; Simulcast + active-stream whitelist
└─ Streaming fan-out N× → CDN delivery; WebRTC for the interaction seats only

Division of Labor with Receiver-Side Super-Resolution

Placing the previous post’s conclusions back into this post’s ledger clarifies the positions:

  • Loss and corruption (block-level damage) are transmission-layer problems, solved entirely here — SRT’s ARQ retransmission, WebRTC’s NACK, surveillance sub streams as the fallback. Super-resolution on corruption only amplifies artifacts as texture.
  • Whole-frame softness (layer-level damage) is the deliberate down-step of adaptive bitrate — that is the receiver-side SR battlefield. After Simulcast steps down to 360p, on-device compute restores the viewing experience; Tencent Cloud and Teams have both validated it (30% / 15.3% bandwidth saved).
  • Keep originals at the edge, enhance on demand is the only cost-sane combination: 16-channel edge storage is 3% of cloud. Keep originals at the edge and enhance per request at playback — far cheaper than cloud-wide super-resolution.

For anyone building an NVR, the two posts compose one map: the transmission layer earns “no corruption, no stalls” first — the first line of defense; super-resolution handles only the “complete but soft” tail, on the playback side, on demand. The two complement; neither replaces the other.
