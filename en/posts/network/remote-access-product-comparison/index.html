A Detailed Comparison of Remote Access Products and Open-Source Projects

October 9, 2026 October 9, 2026 Network Remote Desktop, RustDesk, TeamViewer, AnyDesk, ToDesk, Teleport, Tailscale, JumpServer, PiKVM, Comparison Network Development Practice 2669 words 13 min read
🔊

The two previous posts covered the technical landscape and the RDP protocol itself: A Comprehensive Survey of Remote Desktop and Remote Terminal Technologies gives the panorama, RDP Protocol Deep Dive goes down to the wire format. This post adds the selection perspective: it puts the industry’s well-known products and open-source projects against the same set of dimensions — connection architecture, codecs, encryption, self-hostability, license, activity, security incidents — table first, commentary after. Data time point is October 2026; GitHub stars and licenses were fetched via the GitHub API, product behavior comes from official documentation and security advisories, and no benchmark measurements are included.

The Product Spectrum

Remote access solutions spread along one axis: who controls the session data.

The further left, the more connection infrastructure the vendor carries (signaling, relay, NAT traversal), the lower the user’s setup cost, and the more session metadata passes through the vendor; the further right, the more of the network and hardware the user owns, the higher the setup cost, and the more autonomous the data path. The comparison’s value is showing where each solution sits on this axis — what it pays, what it gets.

TeamViewer: The Industry Template and Lessons on Both Ends

TeamViewer’s connection architecture is the industry template: devices register with master servers and receive a numeric ID; after the master-server handshake, roughly seventy percent of sessions establish a direct UDP/TCP path, the rest go through global relay infrastructure; encryption is RSA 4096-bit key exchange plus AES 256-bit session keys, with the official statement that the vendor cannot decrypt session content (teamviewer.com security statement). The business model tiers by use and device count; the personal edition is free but with strict commercial-use detection — false positives there are a recurring reputation complaint.

Its two security incidents hit two different links: the 2016 wave of accounts logged in by third parties, attributed by the community to password reuse and credential stuffing (user-side authentication); and the June 2024 breach of the internal network by APT29/Midnight Blizzard via a stolen employee credential, with the vendor stating that network segregation protected production (vendor-side infrastructure). The two ends of a remote control product’s trust chain — the user’s account system and the vendor’s own network — both demand seriousness; TeamViewer supplied the lessons for both.

AnyDesk: A Codec Company’s Supply-Chain Incident

AnyDesk was founded by ex-TeamViewer employees in Erlangen, Germany; its technical card is the proprietary DeskRT codec — intra-frame compression designed for screen content (repetitive patterns, text edges), officially claimed at sub-16ms encode+decode latency and usable 60fps at 100 kbit/s (anydesk.com). The divergence from general-purpose video codecs is deliberate: screen content has low temporal redundancy and high spatial redundancy, so intra-frame compression beats inter-frame prediction here.

In January 2024 its production systems were breached and source code plus code signing certificates were stolen; AnyDesk revoked all certificates and reset portal passwords, stating end-user devices were unaffected (quorumcyber.com, akamai.com). A stolen signing certificate means malicious installers can carry a “valid” signature — the user-side action is to fetch the latest signed builds from official channels. The incident also reminds: the industry’s attack surface extends beyond the protocol to the distribution chain.

ToDesk and Sunlogin: The Two Regional-Market Leaders

ToDesk emphasizes home-market node density and direct-connection rate, officially claiming over one hundred million users and two hundred-plus access nodes (vendor marketing and third-party reviews, 2023-2025); public technical detail is thinner than for international vendors — the confirmable shape is the generic ID addressing plus direct/relay hybrid. Sunlogin (BeiRui) has the longer history and a full ecosystem around remote control: power-on peripherals (boot sticks/smart plugs), an enterprise console, hardware boxes — chaining “remote power-on — remote control — NAT traversal” into a product line. On security: in early 2022 Sunlogin disclosed an RCE vulnerability (CNVD-2022-10270/03672) affecting the personal Windows edition ≤11.0.0.33162, fixed in 11.1.1.38222, and 360’s APT report shows real-world exploitation (antiy.cn) — a vulnerability in a remote control tool is host control; patching cadence must be top priority.

Chrome Remote Desktop: The Browser Is the Client

Google’s solution hands transport to the standard WebRTC stack: DTLS-encrypted data channels, ICE/STUN/TURN negotiation, falling back to Google TURN relays; authorization has two tiers — a one-time support code valid about five minutes (ad-hoc help), and persistent access bound to a Google account plus PIN. Its value is a form-factor demonstration: zero installation, cross-platform consistency, an entirely standard protocol stack; the cost is a narrow feature set (little beyond file transfer for enterprise use) and full dependence on Google infrastructure, with reachability subject to local link quality.

RustDesk: The Whole Commercial Logic, Open-Sourced

RustDesk (125k+ stars on GitHub, AGPL-3.0) is the only consumer remote control offering a complete self-hosting path: two server binaries in Rust — hbbs (ID/signaling/punch coordination) and hbbr (relay) — plus the 21115-21119 port range reproduce the commercial connection layer on any VPS; encryption is NaCl-based, end-to-end when self-hosted with your own Key, with the public server mode on the default trust model (rustdesk.com docs). Codecs span the VP8/VP9/AV1 software route and the H.264/H.265 hardware route, adapting to the link. The AGPL-3.0 license choice matters: companies redistributing services built on it carry open-source obligations — a contrast with xrdp’s Apache-2.0.

Its existence proves a point: the signaling + relay + codec core of commercial remote control is no longer a barrier for a mid-sized team — the barriers have moved to global node density, brand trust, and compliance certifications.

The division of labor is clean: for the “help someone fix their computer” product form, RustDesk; for folding Linux into an enterprise RDP regime, xrdp (Windows mstsc connects directly, no client install); for low-activity management graphics, TigerVNC suffices, and browser-side noVNC makes it the staple of cloud consoles; for workstation-grade feel (attached typing, high frame rates), Sunshine + Moonlight — its NVENC/AMF/QSV hardware encoding over UDP fits a sub-50ms budget; and NoMachine’s NX protocol does commercial-grade semantic compression on X11, free as a personal edition, usable as “RDP on Linux” — noting it is the only closed-source entry in this table.

xrdp deserves a paragraph of its own: it is the hub of the protocol-unification route — an RDP client comes in and gets bridged to an Xorg session or a VNC backend, meaning a Windows machine manages Linux desktops with zero new software. Together with the RDP services built into GNOME/KDE (gnome-remote-desktop, KRdp), it is converging on “Linux remote desktop is RDP” as the de facto standard.

In Detail

Teleport’s core abstraction is “certificates have a lifespan”: when nobody can say whose public keys sit on which machines, hours-valid certificates at least guarantee that currently valid authorizations were freshly issued. RBAC decides who reaches which machine, JIT elevation turns “request — approve — expire” into a process. Unifying heterogeneous protocols (SSH, databases, K8s, Windows RDP) under one certificate and recording regime is its position apart from pure SSH tooling (21k stars on GitHub).

Tailscale moves the “access layer” up into the “network layer”: once the WireGuard mesh is up, SSH authentication reuses the tailnet identity, SSH’s own key system is bypassed entirely, authorization follows ACLs, and the device-person relationship is managed by the IdP (tailscale.com). The client is BSD-3-Clause open source (37.2k stars), the control plane is SaaS; deployments that mind control-plane hosting can use the community Headscale to self-host it. The significance of this route: protocols without enterprise access capabilities of their own — RDP, VNC — gain an identity and audit foundation simply by riding the mesh.

Cloudflare Zero Trust trades inbound ports for outbound tunnels: an in-network cloudflared tunnels out to the edge, SSH/RDP/VNC services never expose public ports, and users render sessions in the browser after identity verification (developers.cloudflare.com). The attack surface shifts from “exposed services” to “outbound tunnel management”; scanning and brute force lose their targets; the cost is that all traffic rides the edge, and latency-sensitive graphics sessions depend on distance to the nearest point of presence.

Apache Guacamole is the lightest multi-protocol gateway: the browser is the client, the backend guacd daemon initiates RDP/VNC/SSH connections to targets and translates them into a drawing-instruction stream for the canvas (guacamole.apache.org). It does not solve identity (it fronts external authentication); its value is folding heterogeneous protocols into one clientless, auditable web entry — a staple for lightweight VDI substitutes and contractor access control.

JumpServer is the open-source representative of the bastion-host route (GPL-3.0, 31.7k stars, Python): centralized asset, account, authorization, and session-audit management under the 4A model, covering SSH/RDP/databases, aimed at stringent audit-compliance regimes. The philosophical difference from Teleport: JumpServer evolved along the “bastion host” as an operations-management form (asset grants, tickets, command control), Teleport along the “certificate infrastructure” (short-term certificates, native SSO) — the former fits organizations with strong process-compliance requirements, the latter cloud-native identity systems.

The trade-offs are legible: PiKVM is the most complete (IPMI/Redfish compatibility lets it blend into existing datacenter regimes), JetKVM priced the category at server-aftermarket level (about $5.9M crowdfunded in 2025, jetkvm.com), and NanoKVM uses RISC-V to push cost to a fifth of PiKVM’s, paying with one-to-two-hundred milliseconds of MJPEG latency — fine for BIOS and installs, not for daily graphics use. Server-built-in BMCs (iDRAC/iLO/IPMI) remain the datacenter answer, but remember their historical lesson: CVE-2013-4786 (IPMI 2.0 authentication packets returning password hashes) troubled the industry for a decade, and BMC network-segment isolation remains doctrine.

Beyond this table, closed and open source differ structurally: closed vendors build trust through compliance certifications (SOC 2/ISO 27001) and security statements, with implementations users cannot audit; open-source projects are auditable, but “auditable” does not mean “audited” — before adoption, checking whether the project has published an independent security audit and the security-response rhythm of its issue tracker is more substantive due diligence than counting stars. The license dimension deserves equal weight: AGPL-3.0 projects (RustDesk, Teleport) impose open-source obligations on services redistributed on top of them, while Apache-2.0 (xrdp, Guacamole) is far more permissive — when building enterprise services on open source, the license is a selection input on the same level as the encryption algorithm.
