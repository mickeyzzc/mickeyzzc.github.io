ONVIF vs GB/T 28181: How They Work Inside — Plug-and-Play Devices vs System-Level Networking

August 10, 2026 Network ONVIF, GB28181, SIP, SOAP, WS-Discovery, RTSP, Surveillance, NVR Network Development Practice 3804 words 18 min read
🔊

After MiBeeNvr v0.9 shipped ONVIF auto-discovery, manual camera configuration was mostly gone. The next question was whether to add GB/T 28181 uplink support, so I dug through the mechanics of both protocols. Most comparison articles online stop at “ONVIF is the international standard, GB28181 is the Chinese national standard”—which carries zero information for someone writing code. This post breaks both protocols down to the signaling-flow and message level: how ONVIF discovers devices and obtains stream URIs; how GB28181’s 20-digit codes address everything, how INVITE negotiates a stream, and what PS encapsulation actually is.

The conclusion first: these two protocols do not compete on the same plane. ONVIF solves “how an NVR and a camera become plug-and-play”; GB/T 28181 solves “how platforms and devices interconnect across networks and regions for supervised networking.” The typical industry architecture chains them together: cameras join the local NVR via ONVIF, and the NVR uplinks to the supervisory platform via GB28181.

You don’t need prior SIP or SOAP knowledge to read this—check the glossary below for unfamiliar terms; the text also explains each term on first use.

There is no reliable public benchmark comparing end-to-end latency of the two paths; only magnitudes: direct RTSP on a LAN sits in the hundreds of milliseconds. GB28181 chains typically run higher than direct RTSP because of PS encapsulation, the frequently-added TCP passive mode, and multi-level platform forwarding—exact numbers depend on transport mode and cascade depth.

Pull vs Push: Two Streaming Models

The most fundamental difference between these protocols isn’t message format but who initiates. Once this clicks, every later mechanical difference falls into place.

The pull model (ONVIF/RTSP): the NVR is the active party. It discovers the device, asks for its capability list, asks for a stream URI, then actively connects and “pulls” the stream. The upside: control rests entirely with the client, and the device doesn’t need to know who’s using it. The cost: the client must be able to find the device—which is why ONVIF needs a discovery mechanism.

The push model (GB28181): the device is the active party. The camera or subordinate platform proactively registers with its superior platform and reports “I’m online”; when the platform wants a stream, it sends an INVITE telling the device “push the stream to this address and port.” The upside: as long as the device can reach the platform, it works—naturally suited to cross-subnet, cross-ISP WAN scenarios, and the platform holds full dispatch authority. The cost: you first need a registration and catalog system—the platform must know “who has registered, and which channels they have.”

A mnemonic: ONVIF is “I go to the store and pick goods”; GB28181 is “the supplier registers into the system first, then I place an order and it delivers.”

Read top-down: the upper layer defines “what operations exist,” the middle solves “how to find each other / how to stay reachable,” the lower solves “how the stream travels.” One key insight: ONVIF arranges streams, it does not carry them. It translates camera capabilities into standard SOAP interfaces; GetStreamUri ultimately returns an RTSP URL, and actual streaming is done by RTSP/RTP itself. GB28181 instead runs signaling over SIP and media over RTP/PS, with devices registering actively and the platform scheduling retrieval—a model that naturally fits supervision and cascading.

ONVIF Mechanics

WS-Discovery: Four Steps to a Stream URI

ONVIF device discovery builds on WS-Discovery (SOAP-over-UDP). The idea is simple: a newly plugged-in device doesn’t know who will use it, and the client doesn’t know what’s on the network—so everyone agrees on a “bulletin board,” the multicast group 239.255.255.250:3702. From discovery to a playable stream takes five steps:

mermaid
flowchart TD
    A["Probe<br/>multicast 239.255.255.250:3702"] --> B["ProbeMatch<br/>device replies service address"]
    B --> C["GetCapabilities<br/>obtain service endpoints"]
    C --> D["GetProfiles<br/>obtain Profile Token"]
    D --> E["GetStreamUri<br/>returns RTSP URL"]
    style A fill:#FF9800,color:#fff
    style B fill:#2196F3,color:#fff
    style C fill:#2196F3,color:#fff
    style D fill:#2196F3,color:#fff
    style E fill:#4CAF50,color:#fff

Step by step:

  1. Probe: the client (NVR) sends a Probe to the multicast group, optionally carrying Types (which device class to find) and Scopes (which scope) as filters.
  2. ProbeMatch: every matching, online device replies with its own ProbeMatch carrying XAddrs (the ONVIV service address, e.g. http://192.168.1.100/onvif/device_service), Types, and Scopes. Note this is many-to-one—20 cameras on the network means 20 replies.
  3. GetCapabilities: the client picks one and sends a SOAP request to its Device service, receiving the capability list: endpoints of the Media/PTZ/Events services, supported Profile versions, and so on.
  4. GetProfiles: ask the Media service for the Profile list. A Profile is “one stream’s configuration” (codec, resolution, framerate); main and sub streams are separate Profiles.
  5. GetStreamUri: pick a Profile Token, exchange it for an RTSP URL, then do the standard RTSP handshake (DESCRIBE → SETUP → PLAY) and pull the stream.

Here is a pitfall no production deployment escapes: multicast does not cross subnets. VLAN isolation, a firewall blocking port 3702, or AP isolation—any one of these turns “device not found” into a mystery. Mature NVRs therefore run discovery plus scanning in parallel: WS-Discovery for the local subnet, subnet scanning (probing ONVIF services IP by IP) as fallback, and manual IP entry as the last resort.

The Profile System: Not All “ONVIF Support” Is Equal

When a device claims “ONVIF support,” ask which Profile—different Profiles are different capability sets, like “supports WiFi” should prompt “WiFi 4 or WiFi 6?”:

Profile S’s retirement was announced on 2025-10-09: it mandates username-token authentication, which no longer matches modern cybersecurity recommendations. After 2027-03-31 manufacturers can no longer submit new devices for Profile S certification, and new projects should be designed against Profile T. That date matters if you’re writing access code today—authentication written for S faces migration within three years. The fuller Profile dynamics (V, M evolution, and OSDP on the access-control side) are expanded in the landscape post.

The response is simply <Uri>rtsp://192.168.1.100:554/onvif/media/stream1</Uri>, after which an RTSP client takes over.

Three beginner FAQs:

  • Why so verbose? SOAP was the mainstream enterprise web-service choice of the 2000s: self-describing XML, cross-platform, mature tooling (WSDL can generate client code directly). A reasonable choice when ONVIF started in 2008; the cost is today’s developers processing piles of XML. Community practice generally avoids hand-writing SOAP, using WSDL generation or wrapper libraries instead (see the ecosystem post).
  • How is PasswordDigest computed? Not a plain password hash: SHA1(Nonce + Created timestamp + password) Base64-encoded. The server validates a time window—so the client must sync its clock with the device first (GetSystemDateAndTime), otherwise authentication fails at random. This ranks among the top two ONVIF pitfalls (the first being petty differences in each vendor’s WSDL implementation).
  • Where can I see real messages? Wireshark on the HTTP port shows the full XML—far more intuitive than reading the spec.

GB/T 28181 Mechanics

The 20-Digit Code: The Basis of All Addressing

In the GB system every device, channel, and platform has a 20-digit code; From/To/Subject in signaling are all addressed by it—think of it as the surveillance system’s “ID number plus phone number.” The structure is 8+2+3+1+6:

SegmentLengthMeaningExample (34020000001320000001)
Center code prefix8Administrative division (GB/T 2260)34020000 (Wuhu, Anhui)
Industry code2Industry/public-security class00
Type code3Device/system type132 (network camera)
Network identifier11=public-security intranet, etc.0
Sequence6Per-unit serial000001

Common type codes: 111=DVR, 118=NVR, 131/132/134=camera types, 200=central signaling control server (platform), 216=virtual organization. Many articles describe this as “8-digit region + 2-digit type + 10-digit sequence”; the type code is actually 3 digits plus a 1-digit network identifier—parse it as 8+2+3+1+6.

What can you read from a code? The first 8 digits tell you which administrative region owns the device; the 3-digit type code tells you camera or platform. In cascading, the superior platform routes by code: identical prefixes belong to the same subordinate.

SIP Message Anatomy: The Key to Reading GB Signaling

GB28181 signaling is standard SIP, and every captured message shares a fixed skeleton. For an INVITE, these headers are the keys:

SIP headerRoleGB-specific usage
From / ToIdentify initiator and recipientEach carries a 20-digit GB code
ViaRecords the request path, with a branch parameter per transactionResponses return along Via; branch must be unique per transaction
Call-IDIdentifies a dialogShared by requests within one dialog
ContactThe sender’s direct contact addressAfter registration, the platform knows where to reach the device
SubjectTopic (GB extension usage)Carries “senderID:seq-receiverID:seq”, identifying the stream parties
CSeqCommand sequence numberIncrements within a dialog; pairs requests with responses

Remember one rule: requests and responses pair via the Call-ID + CSeq + Via branch triple. Many homegrown SIP stacks stumble on ACK—ACK belongs to a “separate transaction,” and its Via branch must take a new value rather than reusing INVITE’s; reuse gets the request rejected by devices as invalid.

Registration, Keepalive, Catalog: How a Device Comes Online

GB28181 devices register actively—the opposite direction of ONVIF’s client-driven search:

mermaid
sequenceDiagram
    participant D as Device/NVR
    participant P as GB platform
    D->>P: REGISTER (20-digit code + Expires)
    P-->>D: 401 Digest challenge
    D->>P: REGISTER + Authorization
    P-->>D: 200 OK
    loop every 60s (default)
        D->>P: MESSAGE Keepalive (MANSCDP XML)
        P-->>D: 200 OK
    end
    P->>D: MESSAGE Query Catalog
    D-->>P: 200 OK + DeviceList channel list

Three phases:

  • Registration: the device sends REGISTER with its 20-digit code and validity (Expires). The platform replies 401 demanding auth; the device computes a Digest from the password and retries; success returns 200 OK. The device re-registers periodically to maintain state. This is the standard SIP Digest flow.
  • Keepalive: after registering, the device sends a MESSAGE every 60 seconds (default) whose body is MANSCDP XML (CmdType=Keepalive). The platform marks the device offline after 3 consecutive misses—this is the GB system’s liveness check, far more proactive than ONVIF’s “the client finds out the device died only when it next connects.”
  • Catalog: the platform queries the catalog, and the device replies with its channel list (one 20-digit code + name + model per channel). An NVR with 16 attached cameras appears to the platform as one device with 16 channels; all playback requests target “channel codes.”

Live Streaming: How INVITE Negotiates

Streaming is the core GB28181 transaction:

mermaid
sequenceDiagram
    participant P as GB platform
    participant D as Device/NVR
    P->>D: INVITE (SDP: receiving address/port + SSRC)
    D-->>P: 100 Trying
    D-->>P: 180 Ringing
    D-->>P: 200 OK (SDP: rtpmap:96 PS/90000)
    P->>D: ACK (separate transaction, Via branch must be new)
    D-->>P: RTP/PS push (H.264/H.265 ES encapsulated in PS)
    P->>D: BYE (either side ends)
    D-->>P: 200 OK

The details that bite, expanded:

  • What the SDP says: the c= line is the platform’s own receiving IP; m=video <port> RTP/AVP 96 tells the device where to push; a=rtpmap:96 PS/90000 fixes the payload format as PS with a 90kHz clock; the y= line (a GB extension to SDP) carries the SSRC. In one sentence: the platform writes its “delivery address” into the SDP, and the device ships accordingly.
  • The s= field selects the service: s=Play live, s=Playback replay, s=Download download. Same INVITE skeleton; one field changes the business.
  • SSRC is the stream’s identity: a decimal number; first digit 0 for live, 1 for playback. RTP is connectionless bare UDP (or TCP) data—when a stream packet arrives, how does the platform know which transaction it belongs to? By matching the RTP header’s SSRC against the signaling.
  • ACK is a separate transaction: as covered in the SIP anatomy, its branch must be new.
  • Media flows device→platform: the exact opposite direction of RTSP pulling. After this step signaling goes quiet while the media runs; either side sends BYE to end.

PS Encapsulation: The First Wall for GB Developers

Unlike bare RTP fragmentation in RTSP (H.264 packed as FU-A per RFC 6184), the GB standard mandates encapsulating encoded data into MPEG-PS (Program Stream) before RTP. The data passes through three wrappers:

mermaid
flowchart TD
    A["ES elementary stream<br/>H.264/H.265 NALU"] --> B["PES packet<br/>add timestamps & stream id"]
    B --> C["PS packet<br/>00 00 01 BA header"]
    C --> D["RTP packet<br/>sent over UDP/TCP"]
    style A fill:#FF9800,color:#fff
    style B fill:#2196F3,color:#fff
    style C fill:#9C27B0,color:#fff
    style D fill:#4CAF50,color:#fff

From the inside out: the camera’s encoder emits an ES (H.264 NALU sequence); ES goes into PES packets with 90kHz timestamps; PES plus a PS header (starting code 00 00 01 BA) forms the PS packet; the PS packet ships as RTP payload. An I-frame usually gets its own larger PS packet, with subsequent P-frames following the timestamp rules.

Why the extra layer? Two reasons: PS is codec-agnostic—H.264/H.265/SVAC all fit, the safest choice when the standard was set in 2011; and PS multiplexes audio and video into one program stream, letting intercom audio ride with video. The cost: the platform must de-mux PS and devices must mux PS, and the tooling is more obscure than bare RTP—though FFmpeg and ZLMediaKit both ship ready-made PS demuxing, so you don’t have to hand-roll it.

The 2022 edition also hardened the RTP timestamps (clause 4.3.6): all RTP packets of the same frame must share one timestamp, and different frames must differ. Early device implementations were loose about this, leaving platforms to guess frame boundaries—a major source of legacy compatibility pain.

Beyond Live Streaming: The Full GB Feature Surface

Besides live streaming, GB28181 defines a full set of business signaling:

BusinessSignaling essentials
Recording playbackINVITE with s=Playback + time range; the device streams from recordings; speed control via INFO messages
Recording downloadINVITE with s=Download; file-transfer semantics—platforms handle progress and resume
PTZ controlMESSAGE carrying MANSCDP PTZ command codes (direction/speed/preset/cruise in one encoding)
Voice intercomThe platform sends INVITE to establish a reverse audio channel; audio also rides RTP
Alarm subscriptionThe platform subscribes to device alarms; the device reports events via MESSAGE
Image captureNew in 2022 (clause 9.14): the platform commands an on-the-spot snapshot
Device upgradeNew in 2022 (clause 9.13): remote firmware push
CascadingA subordinate platform registers as a “device” to its superior; catalogs propagate level by level; the superior dispatches across levels

Compare this with ONVIF’s Profile system: ONVIF slices capabilities into certified tiers (S/G/M…), while GB28181 defines everything in one standard and lets vendors implement as needed. So “GB device” capability variance is larger than “ONVIF Profile S device” variance—check which signaling the peer supports before integrating.

What’s New in 2022

GB/T 28181-2022 was published 2022-12-30 and implemented 2023-07-01. Main changes versus 2016:

ChangeContent
Codec upgradesOfficial support for H.265 / AAC / G.722.1
Mandatory RTP timestampsAll RTP packets of the same frame must share one timestamp; different frames differ (clause 4.3.6)
Registration redirect302 redirect, supporting platform migration (clause 9.1.2.3)
Remote upgradeDevice software upgrade signaling (clause 9.13)
Image captureNew snapshot signaling (clause 9.14)
PTZ enhancementsPrecise position query, cruise-track query, position-change event subscription
SecurityChapter 8 rewritten, new “high security” tier integrating GB 35114 national crypto

In the field, the 2011/2016/2022 editions coexist: new projects are mostly 2022, installed devices are heavily 2016, and legacy systems still run 2011. The three editions differ in signaling details and defaults (keepalive intervals, catalog formats), so version negotiation is unavoidable engineering—take the device type and protocol version fields reported at registration seriously; don’t hardcode a single edition.

Security: From Digest to National Crypto

The two security stacks compared:

LayerONVIFGB/T 28181
AuthenticationWS-Security UsernameToken (PasswordDigest)SIP Digest (401 challenge)
Transport encryptionHTTPS / TLSTLS (SIPS over TLS)
High-security scenariosProfile T mandates HTTPS media streamingGB 35114: level A SM2 certificate auth / B +SM3 per-frame signing / C +SM4 stream encryption

Digest/TLS suffices for ordinary commercial projects; for government/enterprise projects, GB 35114’s national-crypto trio (SM2 elliptic-curve certificates, SM3 hashing, SM4 block encryption) is a hard acceptance requirement—confirm national-crypto support when choosing a signaling library.

Their Relationship: Complementary, Not Alternatives

Back to the opening question. Three sentences summarize the relationship:

  • Different scenarios: mixed-brand campuses and overseas projects go ONVIF; domestic government, public security, and cross-region cascading go GB28181.
  • Architecturally upstream/downstream: ONVIF covers the NVR’s device-facing access; GB28181 covers the NVR’s platform-facing uplink. Dual-stack is the norm for modern NVRs.
  • Asymmetric development cost: ONVIF is SOAP/WSDL verbosity—shallow pitfalls, lots of legwork. GB28181’s signaling itself is simple, but vendor discrepancies, NAT traversal, and version fragmentation make it the “deep pits” one. The SRS official docs put it plainly: “please lower your expectations—the GB pits are too deep to fill.” That sentence is more honest than any “GB is easy” tutorial.

How to Learn by Packet Capture

Reading the mechanics ten times beats capturing one packet. Both protocols can be observed for free:

  • ONVIF: Wireshark filter udp.port == 3702 for the Probe/ProbeMatch multicast exchange; filter the device’s HTTP traffic to see complete SOAP request/response XML.
  • GB28181: filter sip to watch the 401 challenge at registration and the keepalive cadence; filter rtp and use Telephony → RTP → Stream Analysis for timestamp sequences and packet loss.
  • No real hardware needed: ONVIF via simulators (see the integration section of the ecosystem post), GB28181 via EasyGBD simulating a device against SRS/ZLMediaKit as platform—the full loop without a single camera.
