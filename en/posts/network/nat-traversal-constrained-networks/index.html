A Comprehensive Analysis of P2P Direct Connection and Tunneling across Complex Network Environments

October 10, 2026 October 9, 2026 Network P2P, NAT Traversal, CGNAT, Frp, WireGuard, Tailscale, EasyTier, DERP, IPv6, Tunneling Network Development Practice 3261 words 16 min read
🔊

The three earlier posts in this series covered the landscape (the survey), the RDP protocol itself (the deep dive), and product comparisons (the comparison); P2P protocol theory was dissected in the P2P signaling and relay survey. One layer remains: the network environment itself. The same STUN/TURN/ICE machinery, dropped into different network constraints, yields entirely different hole-punch success rates, viable solutions, and experience floors — CGNAT-dense access networks, UDP-hostile paths, carriers with uneven peering, congested long-distance backbones at peak. These constraints combine very differently across networks, and selection logic must follow the environment rather than any template.

This post covers that layer: networks are first classified into “constrained” and “conventional” by measurable properties, then the environmental math of hole-punch success, the complete spectrum of self-built tunneling tools (frp, rathole, bore, EasyTier, Tailscale with self-hosted DERP, ZeroTier moon, wstunnel), the engineering math of relay placement, a scenario-matching matrix, and finally a diagnostic methodology that can be followed step by step. Environmental data comes from APNIC Labs measurements (fetched 2026-10) and the GitHub API; link latencies are experience-level magnitudes with their basis noted.

Two Classes of Network Environment

One goal — direct connection between two endpoints — and two different realities:

First, the basis of the classification: it classifies network properties, not geography. Which class a broadband line belongs to is decided by measurement, not by its location — two lines in the same building, one with a dynamic public IPv4 and one behind CGNAT, belong to different classes.

Five Traits of Constrained Environments

CGNAT is the default; public IPv4 is scarce. Carriers facing IPv4 exhaustion generally no longer assign public IPv4 by default: requesting one means a process with no guarantee, and large user populations sit directly behind carrier NAT. The test is direct: if the router’s WAN address falls in 100.64.0.0/10 (RFC 6598’s CGNAT range) or a private range while STUN shows a different public address, two-layer NAT is confirmed. Home router NAT plus carrier NAT multiplies the mapping combinations — hole-punching difficulty does not double, it compounds (the math below).

The animation shows the key detail: every packet the device sends crosses two rewrites in sequence — one on the home router, one on the CGNAT — and the peer sees only the CGNAT egress address and port. Traversal requires both ends’ mappings to line up at once; that is the geometric reason success rates collapse behind double NAT.

IPv6 capability varies widely; high-v6 environments are the end-to-end opportunity. APNIC Labs’ ad-based measurement of IPv6 capability across the world’s networks (stats.labs.apnic.net, fetched 2026-10) shows capability shares (capable) ranging from under thirty percent to nearly eighty — the viability of the same direct-connection approach therefore differs by an order of magnitude. In high-v6 environments, cellular is nearly always v6, and home broadband gets a /60 or larger prefix when the router supports it; with IPv6 end-to-end there is no NAT — “hole punching” degrades to “firewall allowlisting.” The thresholds are equally concrete: both ends need v6; routers’ v6 firewalls block inbound by default and need allowlisting or mapping; some applications need separate validation on v6-only paths.

Some paths are UDP-hostile. Specific throttling policies are undisclosed and vary by carrier and network type, but the tooling ecosystem is checkable evidence: EasyTier’s README explicitly states its built-in KCP/QUIC proxies exist to “mitigate high UDP packet loss environments,” and wstunnel — a tool for wrapping UDP inside WebSocket — counts UDP-hostile paths among its community’s common use cases. The engineering conclusion: never assume a quality floor for UDP paths; every plan needs a non-UDP fallback (TCP/WSS transport).

Uneven peering is an independent source of degradation. Inter-carrier paths degrade visibly at evening peak (latency rise, packet loss); cross-carrier same-city routes are sometimes slower than same-carrier cross-region ones. A single-homed relay node forces other carriers’ users to detour — BGP multi-homing with multiple carrier transits is the first principle of relay placement.

Long-distance paths congest at peak. Submarine cables and intercontinental backbones universally experience bandwidth contention at peak, with large jitter and packet-loss increases; the gap between ordinary transit and paid optimized lines opens widest here. This fixes the far-away relay’s position: treat it as fallback, not as the primary path.

Four Traits of Conventional Environments

  • Residential broadband is dominated by dynamic public IPv4: many carriers still hand out public IPv4 by default; CGNAT is spreading but not yet dominant; the VPS ecosystem defaults to a public IPv4 at low cost — “having a public machine” is an easy precondition
  • IPv6 capability is high: on APNIC’s same measurement, networks leading v6 capability sit in the sixty-to-eighty percent band, and v6 end-to-end opportunities are large
  • UDP is generally unrestricted: residential and mobile networks impose no discriminatory UDP limits, so the standard STUN/TURN playbook succeeds often; enterprise and campus networks are the exception, commonly allowing only HTTP(S) egress — exactly where wstunnel-class tools fit
  • The constraints sit elsewhere: conventional environments lack the access-network and UDP problems, but corporate compliance (data protection regulations, audit requirements) constrains traffic paths — the difference is the kind of constraint, not fewer constraints

The Environmental Math of Hole Punching

Punch success is decided by the two ends’ NAT-type combination (theory in the signaling survey); constrained environments are special because CGNAT turns “bad NAT types” from the exception into the default:

End A \ End BCone NAT (EI/AD)Symmetric NAT / CGNAT
Cone NAT (EI/AD)High UDP punch successDepends on initiation direction; medium
Symmetric NAT / CGNATDepends on initiation direction; mediumLow success; plan as unavailable

Both ends behind CGNAT (the common form of two home devices connecting in CGNAT-dense environments) lands in the bottom-right cell — which is why direct connection there so often depends on luck, and why designs must assume punching fails.

Three mitigations, in priority order:

  1. IPv6 direct connection: with v6 on both ends, the NAT problem vanishes. Cellular v6 adoption generally exceeds residential; home broadband depends on the router’s prefix. Only firewall allowlisting remains. In high-v6 environments this is an order-of-magnitude improvement in success rate
  2. Port mapping/UPnP: with a public IPv4, DDNS plus port mapping is the deterministic path; UPnP/NAT-PMP automates it at an accepted security trade-off
  3. Punching with self-hosted STUN: mechanisms like frp’s xtcp accept a self-hosted STUN server — when default public STUN is unreachable or unstable on some paths, this decides whether punching works at all

One command gets the decision inputs: tailscale netcheck (official CLI docs, tailscale.com/kb/1080) reports UDP availability, IPv4/IPv6 connectivity and public addresses, MappingVariesByDestIP (the symmetric-NAT indicator), UPnP/NAT-PMP/PCP support, and per-DERP latencies — essentially a physical checkup of the network environment.

Viewed end to end, the setup runs in phases: in phase one both ends register with STUN and learn their public mappings; in phase two they send to each other’s mapped address simultaneously, opening a path through both NATs — success means a direct connection at the lowest latency; failure at any step (typically symmetric NAT) drops the session onto a relay. The self-hosting spectrum in the next section is organized entirely around that one structure: relay as the floor, direct connection as the upside.

Port-Forwarding: frp, rathole, bore

This family’s model is “one public server as reverse proxy”: clients dial out from inside the NAT, visitors hit mapped ports on the public server.

frp (fatedier/frp, 109.8k stars, Apache-2.0, fetched 2026-10) is the de facto standard of self-hosted tunneling. Proxy types span tcp/udp/http/https/stcp/xtcp: tcp/udp relay through the server; stcp is the secure mode — the visitor also runs frpc and the two handshake with a secretKey, so no port is publicly exposed; xtcp is the P2P mode — both ends punch via STUN and transfer directly, with the server only brokering (official docs: punching “may not work with all types of NAT devices,” suggesting “fallback to stcp if xtcp doesn’t work,” and natHoleStunServer accepts a self-hosted STUN). These three tiers form the complete gradient of “relay guaranteed, direct as a bonus” — which explains frp’s position: the default path (relay) is deterministically available, and direct connection is upside rather than dependency.

rathole (rathole-org/rathole, 14.3k stars, Rust) positions itself as the higher-performance frp alternative: official benchmarks claim higher throughput, better stability under heavy connection loads, and lower memory, with binaries as small as ~500KB (fitting soft routers/embedded); encryption is Noise protocol (default Noise_NK_25519_ChaChaPoly_BLAKE2s, avoiding self-signed certificate management) or TLS; transports span tcp/tls/noise/websocket; tcp/udp services and config hot reload are supported. Note: no P2P punching mode, relay only — choose it for relay-path quality, not for direct connection.

bore (ekzhang/bore, 11.5k stars, MIT) is the minimalist: one command publishes a local TCP port on a public server, a single Rust binary. Few features (no UDP, no HTTP routing, no encryption layer), suited to ad-hoc exposure and development debugging.

nps (34.2k stars) deserves its own warning line: broader in features than frp (tunnels, proxies, socks5, load balancing), but the repository has had no commits since 2024-05 — a stalled project is a demerit in the security-tooling category, and new deployments should not choose it.

Mesh Networking: EasyTier, Tailscale + Self-Hosted DERP, ZeroTier moon

This family builds virtual LANs rather than forwarding single ports — every port of every node is reachable inside the network.

EasyTier (14k stars, Rust, LGPL-3.0) is a newer open-source project, decentralized in architecture — no server/client distinction; any node that can reach any existing node can join. Three features map directly onto constrained environments: punching supports both UDP and IPv6, officially covering NAT4-to-NAT4 (multi-layer NAT on both ends); on punch failure, traffic relays through shared nodes (community public nodes or self-hosted); and a built-in KCP/QUIC proxy layer resists UDP loss, with WSS transport — a ready-made downgrade path on UDP-hostile links. Subnet proxying (-n broadcasting a local subnet into the virtual net) and WireGuard protocol access (acting as a “VPN portal” for native WG clients) cover both meshing and access. Default ports: TCP/UDP 11010, WebSocket 11011, WSS 11012, WireGuard 11013.

Tailscale + self-hosted DERP: Tailscale’s signaling and relaying depend on the vendor’s coordination server and DERP network (the comparison post covered this); in constrained environments the problem is DERP reachability and latency. The official docs (tailscale.com/kb/1118) fully support self-hosting derper: ports are HTTP 80 (not configurable) + HTTPS 443 + STUN 3478; the server must be directly internet-facing (NAT/firewalls/load balancers break source-address identification, and most cloud LBs don’t support the HTTP upgrade clients use); --verify-clients restricts use to your own tailnet (otherwise anyone knowing the IP can borrow it). Custom regions use IDs 900-999, and OmitDefaultRegions restricts traffic to your own regions. The official position is worth noting: self-hosting DERP is an advanced operation — first troubleshoot why direct connections fail or deploy peer relays; sustained DERP load signals a network problem. For a self-hosted control plane there is Headscale (44.4k stars, BSD-3-Clause).

ZeroTier + moon: a moon is a user-hosted supplementary root; after clients orbit it, peer discovery and relaying prefer the faster-responding roots (docs.zerotier.com). Its value in constrained environments is moving discovery plus relay from the default global roots onto servers you control. Two cautions: officially, deploy at least two moon nodes and keep them off the controller host; and the docs have marked private moons as deprecated (outside SLA) — usable, but with the expectation that this is not the vendor’s promoted direction.

Restricted-Path Wrapping: wstunnel and WSS Transport

When a path allows only HTTP(S) (corporate egress and campus networks are the typical cases), UDP and non-standard TCP ports simply do not pass. The remaining road is dressing traffic as HTTPS.

wstunnel (7.1k stars, Rust, BSD-3-Clause) is the representative: it wraps TCP/UDP/Unix sockets/stdio/SOCKS5 inside WebSocket (ws/wss, TLS 1.3, self-signed certs and mTLS supported), HTTP/2, or WebTransport (HTTP/3 over QUIC, requiring end-to-end UDP). The CLI mimics SSH tunnel syntax (-L/-R), with server-side destination restrictions and path-based auth. The canonical use: WireGuard over WSS — when WG’s UDP is throttled, carry it over WebSocket (with reduced MTU and routing care); the docs also document deployment options such as own certificates and SNI override. EasyTier’s WSS mode is the same idea built in.

Vendor-Hosted Integrated Commercial Options

Outside the self-hosting spectrum sits another route: vendor-hosted integrated commercial mesh/tunneling (bundled hardware routers or clients plus the vendor’s signaling/relay network). The selling point is a zero-configuration threshold; the price is plan-based bandwidth caps, closed-source tunneling, and vendor account lock-in. The trade-off against self-hosting is the same axis as the comparison post: convenience and auditability do not come together. Specific product forms are already covered in the comparison post’s consumer and enterprise sections; this post does not repeat them.

Relay Placement and the Link Math

Relay location sets the experience floor. Tiered by the relay’s topology relative to the two ends (experience-level magnitudes; real numbers vary by line and time of day):

Relay-to-ends topologyTypical RTT magnitudeBandwidth cost structureRole
Same region, BGP multi-homed datacenter5-40ms both endsExpensive per-bandwidth; lightweight clouds have traffic bundlesPrimary relay when both ends share a region
Adjacent region (across a sea / neighboring region)30-80ms (large evening variance)Mostly traffic-billed; optimized lines cost extraCross-region fallback, unstable at peak
Intercontinental130-200msCheap, high volumeThe fallback behind the fallback

Three engineering conclusions: for tunnels within one region, put the relay in a well-peered multi-homed datacenter — one lightweight cloud instance’s traffic bundle usually covers SSH/remote-desktop-scale use; cross-region access must accept peak degradation as normal — tolerable for SSH/ops interactions, visibly choppy for desktop streaming; multi-relay failover should ride protocol built-ins — DERP’s multi-region auto-selection and EasyTier’s latency-first routing are ready-made, while frp requires running multiple servers and switching manually.

One compliance line: tunnels carrying personal data must comply with the data protection regulations applicable at both ends and the organization’s own policies. This is not a technology choice, but it decides whether the connection is permitted at all.

Diagnostics: A Followable Procedure

Step one: count NAT layers. If the router’s WAN address is inside 100.64.0.0/10, 10/8, 172.16/12, or 192.168/16, there is another carrier NAT above it. Cross-check with tailscale netcheck: a public address in its output differing from the WAN address confirms multi-layer NAT; MappingVariesByDestIP=true means symmetric NAT — this one field sets the punching expectation.

Step two: confirm UDP and port-mapping capability. UDP=false in netcheck means direct connection is off the table (tunnels only); an empty UPnP / NAT-PMP / PCP line means the router has no automatic mapping — even with a public IP, mapping is manual.

Step three: measure link quality by time of day. Run mtr toward the relevant peer or relay IP once at midday and once at evening peak, comparing latency and loss. Peering degradation and long-distance congestion only show up in time-of-day comparison; a single run proves nothing.

Step four: verify IPv6 end to end. On both ends confirm a global unicast v6 address (home broadband: check the router’s prefix delegation), allowlist the needed inbound, and ping the peer’s v6 address. With all three done, v6 direct connection is the optimal path for that environment.

Step five: fall through the attribution order. Signaling unreachable (public STUN/DERP unreachable) → self-host STUN/DERP; UDP blocked or unusable → downgrade to WSS/TCP wrapping; bad NAT combination → move from punching to relay (frp stcp/rathole), or bypass NAT first with IPv6/mapping. Each step has a concrete criterion; no guessing required.
