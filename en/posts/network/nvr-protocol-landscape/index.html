The Surveillance Protocol Panorama: A Four-Layer Map from Access to Compliance — What Each Protocol Is and When to Use It

August 12, 2026 Network ONVIF, GB28181, RTSP, WebRTC, RTMP, HLS, SRT, GA/T 1400, SVAC, GB 35114, ISAPI, Surveillance, NVR Network Development Practice 2505 words 12 min read
🔊

The previous post took ONVIF and GB/T 28181 apart down to signaling and messages. But building an NVR means the protocol environment extends well beyond those two: vendor-private APIs on the camera side, a view-library standard on the public-security side, Ministry-of-Transport standards for vehicles, WebRTC for browser viewing, HLS for public distribution, and a national-crypto compliance stack. This post lays out the entire landscape—each protocol gets four sections: what it is → key technical points → its relationship with other protocols → its value to an NVR. Beginners can read it as an index; veterans can jump straight to the priority table.

The overview first; layers expand below.

Each layer owns one job—before evaluating any “surveillance protocol,” first ask which layer it belongs to; many protocol arguments are really cross-layer comparisons. ONVIF and GB28181 form the spine; everything else positions around it.

Access / Interoperability Layer

This layer answers “how the NVR operates cameras.” The ideal is all brands speaking one language; the reality is standards coexisting with private APIs.

ONVIF: The De-facto International Standard

The previous post fully dissected its mechanics (WS-Discovery → SOAP signaling → RTSP pull); here is its landscape positioning: founded in 2008 by Axis, Bosch, and Sony, the ONVIF forum now counts 500+ members and 33,000+ certified products—penetration deep enough that “ONVIF support” is a default expectation in procurement, not a bonus. Even Hikvision and Dahua, who have their own private APIs, can’t bypass it for export and third-party integration.

PSIA: The Loser of the Standards War

  • What it is: the Physical Security Interoperability Alliance, ONVIF’s only meaningful competitor, kicked off in early 2008 by Axis, Cisco, GE Security, IBM, Panasonic, Pelco, Sony and others (Bosch sided with ONVIF).
  • Key points: took the REST route—HTTP + XML, cleaner interfaces than SOAP; covered video and access control; flexible device search but uneven vendor compatibility; no mandatory certification system.
  • Relationship: the losing side of the video surveillance standards war. REST was arguably nicer than SOAP, but ONVIF won on founding-giant backing plus a testable certification system—the certainty of “you can walk into a certification lab and test it” beat interface aesthetics.
  • NVR value: skip it. Its lesson outlives the protocol: when choosing a protocol ecosystem, governance and certification matter more than technical style. Only access-control credential interoperability (PLAI, PKOC) remains active today.

Hikvision ISAPI / Dahua HTTP APIs: Proprietary but Unavoidable

  • What it is: Hikvision’s private HTTP interface (ISAPI—Internet Server Application Programming Interface), REST-style HTTP + XML; Dahua has an analogous HTTP API family.
  • Key points: clean path conventions, e.g. GET /ISAPI/Streaming/channels/101/ (101 = channel 1 main stream); constructible RTSP URLs: rtsp://ip:554/Streaming/Channels/101 (102 = substream); beyond streaming, it reaches Hikvision-specific features ONVIF doesn’t cover—alarm arming, analytics parameters, face-parameter configuration.
  • Relationship: complementary to ONVIF: ONVIF is the generic layer, ISAPI the vendor enhancement layer. Nearly every Hikvision device supports both; the same function is often reachable via either.
  • NVR value: general-purpose NVRs prefer ONVIF; deep Hikvision integration (fine-grained alarm linkage, analytics configuration) requires ISAPI. Reserve a “vendor adapter layer” in the architecture—don’t scatter private-API calls through business code.

Networking / Signaling & Data Layer

This layer answers “how systems interconnect.” GB28181 networks video streams, GA/T 1400 networks structured data, JT/T 808 networks vehicles—three unrelated systems, each self-contained.

GB/T 28181: The Domestic Networking Backbone

Its mechanics were dissected in the previous post (SIP registration + INVITE + RTP/PS push). Its landscape position in one sentence: the mandatory base standard for cross-region video networking in China—government, public-security, state-owned-enterprise, and finance projects basically require GB28181 support to interconnect. It isn’t ONVIF’s competitor but its upstream: cameras enter the NVR over ONVIF, the NVR uplinks over GB28181.

GA/T 1400: The “Data Plane” of GB28181 (View Library)

  • What it is: the public-security video/image database standard, colloquially the “view library.” If GB28181 networks video streams, GA/T 1400 networks structured data.
  • Key points: manages four object families—persons/faces, motor/non-motor vehicles, objects, and scene/image labels; each “view” bundles an image or video with structured metadata (a face photo + capture time/place + attribute description). Interfaces fall into three categories: collection (devices/platforms reporting into the library), data services (query, subscription/notification, surveillance alerts), and cascading (sync between hierarchical libraries).
  • Relationship: GB28181’s “video plane + data plane” counterpart: 28181 pulls the streams, 1400 stores and shares the analysis results. The international analog is ONVIF Profile M (standard interfaces for AI metadata and events)—one domestic public-security edition, one international edition.
  • NVR value: mandatory when doing AI value-add (face/plate detection) for public-security integration; home and ordinary commercial use can ignore it entirely.

JT/T 808 / 1076 / 1078: The Ministry-of-Transport Vehicle Stack

  • What it is: the transport ministry’s vehicle standard family: 808 is BeiDou/GPS terminal-to-platform communication, 1076 terminal technical requirements, 1078 vehicle audio/video.
  • Key points: 808 runs on TCP long connections + binary messages (header + body + checksum)—a completely different world from text protocols like SIP/RTSP; 1078 extends live video, playback, and snapshots; newer versions add active-safety alarms (ADAS, DSM driver-status monitoring).
  • Relationship: no direct relation to ONVIF/GB28181; the vehicle domain is its own universe.
  • NVR value: mandatory for vehicle NVRs, body cameras, and “two passengers one hazard” fleet compliance; fixed-surveillance product lines never touch it.

Media Transport Layer

This layer answers “how the stream travels.” RTSP/RTP is the surveillance ingress standard, WebRTC the ready answer for real-time browser viewing, RTMP/FLV/HLS face distribution, SRT faces weak networks. Deep internals (congestion, packaging, CDN adaptation) across 14 protocols are in the streaming panorama; here the focus is their roles in surveillance scenarios.

One stream comes in; egress can run several paths simultaneously: real-time guarding on WebRTC, web live view on FLV, mobile playback on HLS. That’s the standard shape of a modern NVR—single ingress (RTSP/ONVIF), multi-protocol egress.

RTSP / RTP / RTCP: The NVR’s Lifeline

  • What it is: RTSP is the “remote control of streaming”—it issues control commands and carries no data; data rides RTP, with RTCP handling statistics and synchronization.
  • Key points: the four-step session DESCRIBE → SETUP → PLAY → TEARDOWN; H.264 over RTP packs per RFC 6184, large frames split into FU-A fragments; RTSP over TCP uses interleaved mode (RTP inside the RTSP connection—firewall-friendly); RTP header timestamps and sequence numbers drive framing and loss detection.
  • Relationship: the final carrier of ONVIF streaming—GetStreamUri returns an RTSP URL. GB28181 doesn’t use RTSP (it pushes RTP/PS directly).
  • NVR value: top priority, for both ingress and egress. Nearly every IP camera supports RTSP, including white-label devices without ONVIF—an RTSP URL is practically the camera world’s lingua franca. Reference implementations: gortsplib (Go), retina (Rust).

WebRTC: The Modern NVR’s Preferred Egress

  • What it is: the browser-native real-time A/V standard: sub-second latency, plugin-free, end-to-end encrypted.
  • Key points: connection setup via SDP Offer/Answer (server-side wrapped as the WHIP/WHEP standard interfaces); media over SRTP with DTLS-negotiated keys; NAT traversal via ICE/STUN/TURN; codecs VP8/VP9/H.264/AV1.
  • Relationship: ONVIF’s new Profile V (cloud/VSaaS) uses WebRTC for media uplink; ZLMediaKit/SRS/MediaMTX all support direct GB28181 → WebRTC conversion—GB in, browser out, no RTMP relay.
  • NVR value: strongly recommended. Surveillance guarding is the most latency-sensitive scenario (when something happens, you need to see it now), and WebRTC is the only mature sub-second browser option. Go: pion/webrtc; Rust: str0m / webrtc-rs.

RTMP / HTTP-FLV: The Distribution Workhorses

  • What it is: RTMP is the Adobe-era live push protocol—Flash is dead but pushing over RTMP remains the de-facto standard; HTTP-FLV wraps the FLV container in a long HTTP connection.
  • Key points: RTMP runs on TCP + AMF messages, 3–5s latency; HTTP-FLV at 1–3s, CDN-friendly, common on domestic live platforms and in the WeChat ecosystem.
  • Relationship: no direct tie to ONVIF/GB28181, but a common GB-platform egress: GB28181 ingest → server converts to FLV → web playback.
  • NVR value: needed when interfacing with live platforms or legacy players; replaced by WebRTC for pure low latency. Practical RTMP pushing to domestic platforms was covered in gortmplib vs go2rtc vs FFmpeg.

HLS: The Compatibility Fallback

  • What it is: video sliced into small segments (ts/fMP4) plus an m3u8 playlist, distributed over plain HTTP—playable on any platform.
  • Key points: standard HLS latency 6–30s (segment duration × 3+); LL-HLS compresses to 2–3s; especially friendly to recording playback—playback is just VOD, and slicing is playback.
  • Relationship: complements the real-time protocols rather than competing; MiBeeNvr’s HLS live view and recording playback share one pipeline.
  • NVR value: the fallback for public sharing, mobile viewing, and recording playback. When latency matters, switch to WebRTC/FLV.

SRT: The Weak-Network Weapon

  • What it is: an open-source (Haivision) reliable transport over UDP, designed for unstable public links.
  • Key points: ARQ retransmission + FEC forward error correction; AES encryption; native NAT traversal; configurable latency (trading retransmission window for delay).
  • Relationship: competes with RTMP for the “contribution first hop”: over the public internet, SRT’s loss resilience beats RTMP over TCP by a wide margin.
  • NVR value: strongly recommended for cross-WAN ingest/return—branch-office cameras back to HQ, construction sites back to the cloud. MediaMTX/SRS support it natively; the Go ecosystem has datarhei/gosrt.

Codec & Security Layer

This layer is the compliance center of gravity; domestic projects split it three ways:

SVAC: The Domestic Surveillance Codec

  • What it is: China’s own surveillance video coding standard, GB/T 25724-2017 (SVAC 2.0), designed for surveillance pain points.
  • Key points: three things H.264/265 cannot do—① source encryption (national-crypto encryption at encode time); ② per-frame signing (tamper-evident, court-admissible evidence); ③ in-stream structured data (object-detection results embedded in the bitstream—decode and you have the metadata). GB 37300-2018 requires key-area capture devices to comply with SVAC.
  • NVR value: required for classified/key-area projects; ordinary commercial use can skip it.

GB 35114: Three-Level National Crypto

  • What it is: China’s first mandatory national standard for surveillance networking information security (published 2017-11-01, effective 2018-11-01).
  • Key points: three ascending levels—Level A: SM2 certificate mutual authentication (blocks rogue device access); Level B: A + SM3 per-frame signing (verifiable integrity when footage is used as evidence); Level C: B + SM4 real-time stream encryption (anti-eavesdropping in transit). Comes with a PKI certificate system. GB/T 28181-2022’s new “high security” tier integrates directly with it.
  • NVR value: mandatory for government/enterprise. Requires national-crypto libraries (SM2/SM3/SM4) and certificate management—confirm signaling-stack support during selection.

MLPS 2.0 and Data Compliance

  • What it is: the IoT extension of Cybersecurity Multi-Level Protection Scheme 2.0 (GB/T 22239-2019), plus industry rules from the Cybersecurity Law and Counter-Terrorism Law.
  • Key points: face and plate data require classification and grading; sensitive data needs SM4-encrypted transport and storage; tiered key management; log retention ≥ 6 months (Cybersecurity Law art. 21) and key-target video ≥ 90 days (Counter-Terrorism Law art. 32); expired data must be irrecoverably destroyed.
  • NVR value: enterprise projects need encrypted storage, audit logs, tiered permissions, and data-destruction capabilities. Reserving “capability slots” in the architecture is far cheaper than retrofitting before acceptance.

How a beginner uses this map: first locate your requirements across the layers (a home NVR usually touches only access + media), then check items off the priority table; for government/enterprise projects, work backward from compliance—whatever acceptance demands is the must-have.
